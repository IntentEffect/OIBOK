# Test work design and future expertise

**Pilot protocol 0.1 · DRAFT · Prepared for use · 26 November 2026**

This pilot helps a team examine one change to a recurring workflow, the judgement that work requires and how people will develop that judgement. It also asks whether OIBOK adds useful questions or changes a decision compared with the team's usual review. No participating organisation, field result or independent review is recorded in this edition.

Download the [protocol in Word](/downloads/pilots/OIBOK-Workforce-Development-Pilot-0.1.docx), [PDF](/downloads/pilots/OIBOK-Workforce-Development-Pilot-0.1.pdf) or [Markdown](/downloads/pilots/OIBOK-Workforce-Development-Pilot-0.1.md).

Use the [working records](/tools/workforce-development-review/) alongside [Guide 0.6.3](/guide/). Keep completed records in the organisation's authorised workspace. The records can be added to an existing improvement review; there is no need to establish a separate reporting process.

## Choose the decision and the people

Choose a small, reversible change that the team can authorise and observe. A proposal moving from drafting to accepted review is one possible workflow. The pilot needs enough recurring work to inspect more than an isolated success, and a clear explanation of what a useful completed case means for its recipient. Record a feasible case count and observation period before starting. Neither is an OIBOK benchmark or a statistically sufficient sample by itself.

Include someone who performs the work, someone who receives it, a person who can authorise the change and a colleague able to assess the required judgement. One person may hold several roles; record that overlap. Identify who can pause the trial and restore the previous arrangement. Use rehearsals where live work would expose people to consequences the team cannot control.

Explain the inquiry to participants, including how observations will be used. Participation in research feedback is voluntary, with a route to decline or withdraw. Agree the handling of earlier contributions if someone withdraws. Separate learning observations from individual employment decisions, and keep identifiable case and staff information within authorised access. Seek separate permission before publishing a case, comment or name.

## Fix the plan before looking at results

Complete record 1 with the workflow's start, accepted completion, beneficiary, eligibility rules, exclusions and the edition being tested. Specify the proposed mechanism and at least one competing explanation. For example, a shorter wait could arise from clearer authority, an easier case mix or additional reviewer time.

Record the baseline and trial intake windows separately. The intake cutoff determines which cases enter. Each case then receives the agreed follow-up window and its own outcome deadline. Use the same window for comparable cases and hold the final review after those deadlines where possible. If follow-up is incomplete, identify those cases and keep their outcome open. Include every eligible case during the agreed intake window, or state the sampling rule in advance. Log screened cases, exclusions and reasons. Preserve the age of unfinished cases and distinguish outcomes observed within the original window from later observations.

Specify what would justify continuing, adapting, pausing or stopping. Include quality and workload limits, who judges them and what happens when information is missing. If volume is insufficient, use the pre-agreed extension rule or report an inconclusive result. Date any amendment, explain why it was necessary and distinguish findings under the original plan from later exploratory observations.

## Compare the reviews

First record what the team already believes and the action it would ordinarily take. Describe the usual review, including its questions, evidence, people and time allowance. This provides a concrete comparator.

Where staffing permits, ask two separate reviewers or small groups to examine the same case packet independently. Give both the same records, access, question and time budget. One uses its usual method; the other uses the selected OIBOK prompts. Allocate reviewers to methods before the review, randomly where feasible, and record relevant experience and prior OIBOK familiarity. A single pair of reviews cannot separate method effects from differences between the reviewers.

Prepare the packet from one or more consecutive eligible cases, retaining difficult cases and missing information. Where the question concerns what could have been recognised at a decision point, initially provide only the information then available. Keep later outcome evidence for checking the completed accounts. Close both review records before sharing conclusions. Include reading, preparation and facilitator assistance in the effort account; extra coaching for one group changes the comparison.

Freeze the selected OIBOK passages and prompts before the review. Preserve the usual review in its ordinary form without first supplying additional reporting prompts. After both accounts are saved, map what each actually contains to explanation, evidence, rival explanation, proposed action, expected consequence and unresolved question. Leave genuinely absent elements absent. Check the findings against case records and the people involved. If a suitable colleague was not involved in either review, ask them to assess the accounts without method labels where practicable. Record their relationship to the work and any disagreement.

Count a contribution as useful only when it identifies a supported issue that matters to the decision, rules out a plausible explanation or improves the proposed action. Record the specific passage, question or ordinary practice responsible. More findings or a longer worksheet alone do not establish added value. Keep unsupported suggestions, missed issues, duplicated effort and findings of no added value in the comparison.

For a small team unable to run separate reviews, save the usual review first, then revisit the same material with OIBOK using the same time allowance. This is a formative walkthrough. A second look, practice, newly noticed evidence or facilitator help may explain any change. Report usefulness and burden without attributing the change causally to OIBOK.

## Follow the selected change through the work

Use the review to decide whether to retain current practice, investigate further or try the bounded change. The authorised owner records that decision. Selecting a change does not establish that the review method is superior.

Before changing the work, capture a comparable baseline using the same definitions and source records. Use concurrent comparison cases where feasible and appropriate; otherwise explain differences between the baseline and trial periods. Record demand, case complexity, staffing, source or policy changes and additional support that could affect the result. If several changes are introduced together, assess that package rather than assigning the outcome to AI alone.

For each eligible case, record arrival, entry to review, acceptance, correction and accepted completion. Track the queue at consistent intervals and retain the opening backlog separately from new intake. Measure accepted completions, waiting and end-to-end time, returned work, final defects and effort across roles. Include supervision, recovery and maintenance. Mark cases rescued by expert intervention, describing what changed and the extra effort. Report quality checks and their denominator, including cases not checked. The [evaluation chapter](/guide/evaluate/) explains how these measures relate to the whole job.

Inspect a waiting case before deciding what failed. Usable work awaiting an occupied reviewer suggests capacity; missing information suggests an input or handoff problem; an unresolved permitted decision suggests authority. Changes in demand or case mix may also explain the pattern. Check whether another team acquired work that the initial measure omitted.

## Examine learning and future staffing

Identify the human judgement the changed workflow still needs. Examples include recognising a material error, finding the governing source, explaining an exception or knowing when to escalate. Decide what assistance is permitted when assessing that judgement. Tools can remain part of a competent working arrangement; this protocol does not require everyone to reproduce every task without them.

Map how less experienced people currently gain practice, feedback and responsibility. Ask which opportunities change when tasks are automated, responsibilities broaden or recruitment changes. Distinguish an observed staffing decision from a forecast. Agree a replacement learning opportunity, supervision time and a person responsible for developing future practitioners. A plan to provide practice is not evidence that learning occurred.

Use a suitable initial rehearsal and a later unfamiliar case of comparable difficulty. Link checks with a pseudonymous practitioner reference, and distinguish repeat checks of that person from a succession check involving someone else. Set the judgement criteria in advance, retain the reasoning and source references, and record all assistance. Record practice, coaching and relevant experience between checks, together with any changes to criteria or permitted support. An experienced assessor should explain the judgement against those criteria. Separate assisted output quality, the practitioner's judgement and the assessor's confidence. A supervisor's unrecorded help can conceal what the practitioner can do.

Schedule a later ordinary-work or succession check when the work permits it. Ask whether another suitably prepared person can find the current guidance and carry out the required judgement within their authority. Record the support still needed. Limited observations cannot establish a long-term supply of expertise or predict the employment effects of AI. Research Chapters [3](/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/), [8](/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/) and [9](/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/) provide the relevant distinctions.

## Review the result and make the next decision

At the agreed review, report baseline and trial counts separately. Reconcile screened cases as excluded plus eligible. Assign every eligible case one recorded end state: accepted completion, operational cancellation or abandonment, still open at its outcome deadline, or outcome unknown. Flag incomplete follow-up within unknown outcomes and give the reason. Flag expert rescue within these states rather than counting it as an additional case.

Keep participant withdrawal or loss of permission to use data separate from operational cancellation. Follow the agreed withdrawal arrangement, report missing observations and explain any change in the usable denominator. Show counts and denominators before summaries, including the number of completed quality checks. Do not estimate a completion-time improvement from completed cases alone without showing unfinished work and the common outcome window.

Assess three questions separately:

- Did the selected work arrangement improve the intended outcome within its quality and workload limits?
- What evidence exists about the required judgement and the opportunities for developing it?
- What did OIBOK add to the usual review, and at what cost in time or complexity?

Record contradictory cases and what would change the judgement. Distinguish an observed result from an interpretation, a completed action and a future commitment. Choose continue, adapt, stop or gather more evidence, with an owner and date. Set a later maintenance or succession check where the capability claim depends on it. If no eligible later case occurs, keep that claim open.

Use record 7 for a short de-identified account. A single team or small case series supports local learning and further questions. Claims about comparative effectiveness, rare harms or persistence across organisations need a stronger design and further evidence. Independent evaluation advice may be useful before expanding the inquiry.
