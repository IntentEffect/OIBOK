# From AI tools to organisational capability

An organisation's investment in AI becomes useful when it improves the work people need to accomplish. That requires a clear outcome, usable knowledge, legitimate decisions, coordinated action and a way to learn from the consequences. OIBOK offers a connected set of questions for designing and examining those conditions.

Begin with the capability the organisation wants to develop. It might be resolving a class of service requests accurately, making a recurring decision with better evidence or detecting an operational problem early enough to respond. The AI task then sits within a wider piece of work whose performance can be assessed.

## Distinguish task performance from organisational capability

A person may use AI to prepare a better summary or finish a task sooner. That is a useful result to examine at the level where it occurred. An organisational claim requires evidence about the arrangements that make the work repeatable: accessible expertise, recognised responsibilities, reliable handoffs and feedback that changes subsequent practice.

OIBOK's [integrated account](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/) treats persistence as part of the question. Can another person continue the work? Does the approach remain usable when the case changes or the system is unavailable? Can the organisation recognise and correct a recurring error? A strong demonstration by one experienced employee leaves these questions open. Their judgement and personal knowledge may be doing work that the proposed process has not yet made available to others.

## Design the work around the task

Consider a hypothetical internal IT service desk using an AI assistant to help prepare recommendations on software access requests. An employee requests access; the assistant brings together the request, relevant role guidance and case information. A useful draft is an intermediate output. The intended outcome is appropriate access, authorised and implemented correctly.

The organisation needs to identify the current source of guidance, provide the relevant context, decide who checks the recommendation and establish who can approve the resulting action. It also needs a way to handle exceptions or conflicting information.

OIBOK's [Knowledge and Expertise chapter](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/) examines how relevant understanding becomes available and credible in practice. In this example, a policy owner maintains the access rules, while the employee's manager confirms their current responsibilities and temporary project needs. The assistant should make the sources and unresolved discrepancies visible. A confidently worded recommendation cannot settle a disagreement between an old role record and the current request.

Source maintenance remains an organisational task. A policy change should trigger a check that the assistant can retrieve the new guidance and avoid the superseded version. Case context needs an owner too: someone must resolve missing details and identify changes in the employee's responsibilities before the request is approved.

The [Decision and Authority chapter](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/decision-and-authority/) distinguishes a recommendation from an authorised organisational commitment. The workflow therefore needs to specify who confirms the business need, who approves access and who implements the approved change. These responsibilities may sit with different people. Reviewers need the evidence, time and expertise to make their judgement, together with authority to question or reject a proposal.

## Coordinate beyond the individual user

An individual's useful interaction with an AI system is one part of organisational work. Other people may need to supply missing information, manage dependencies or continue the case later.

The [Coordination and Collective Action chapter](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/) focuses on those dependencies and the context that must travel across handoffs. For the service desk, the person implementing access needs the approved scope, supporting rationale and any conditions. The case record should distinguish a recommendation awaiting approval from an approved change awaiting implementation.

The [Execution and Operational Reliability chapter](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/) examines authorised execution, exceptions, recovery and operational control. A missing manager, conflicting policy or unavailable system needs a named recipient and an agreed response. Work should remain visibly unresolved until someone accepts responsibility for the next step.

Completion also needs a shared meaning. In this hypothetical workflow, a drafted response is different from a correctly implemented and checked access change. Recording those states helps the organisation examine where work waits, loses context or requires correction. A manual fallback should preserve the same ownership and record of what remains outstanding.

## Give people a shared reference

Project management offers an example of the role a body of knowledge can play. PMI's [PMBOK Guide](https://www.pmi.org/standards/pmbok) organises guidance around principles and areas of practice, with an emphasis on adapting approaches to context. A shared reference gives practitioners a structure for discussing work and improving how it is approached.

OIBOK applies that organising ambition to a different question: how organisations orient, coordinate and improve over time. Its current [research baseline](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/research/release-record/) is a developing, literature-grounded synthesis, with empirical questions still open. The practical value to test is whether its connections help a team design and evaluate a better way of working.

That inquiry can lead to several choices. A team might improve its source material, clarify decision authority, redesign a handoff, introduce AI assistance or combine these changes. The [AI Opportunity and Work Design Canvas](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/) makes the comparison explicit before a pilot is selected. For the service desk, clearer request forms and maintained access guidance provide a credible alternative to examine alongside AI assistance.

## Evaluate the whole intervention

Measure the outcome that matters alongside errors, exceptions and total human effort. Record the baseline, eligible cases, observation period and comparison before interpreting results. A quicker recommendation is useful evidence about one task; the effect on the complete workflow needs its own examination.

For the service desk, evaluation could follow requests through to correctly completed access, including cases returned for clarification or correction. Record elapsed time and the effort contributed by requesters, reviewers and implementers. Include setup, integration, training, source maintenance and ongoing support when judging the proposal. Saving drafting time while creating additional review or correction work changes the overall assessment.

Include requests that the assistant cannot handle. Exceptions are part of the work the organisation must support. Agree who records missing information, corrections and cases still open at the end of the observation window. This makes the comparison's coverage and limitations visible when the team decides what to do next.

If an intervention combines AI with training, clearer policy and redesigned handoffs, an improvement cannot automatically be attributed to AI alone. Workload, case mix, staffing and measurement differences may also contribute. The [Outcome and Learning Record](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/tools/outcome-and-learning/) keeps these alternatives visible. Agree in advance what would support continuing, adapting or stopping the pilot, and report missing observations and unresolved explanations alongside the results.

## Retain what the organisation learns

The next question is what the organisation retains. Which source, routine, responsibility or training changes? Who maintains it? When will someone check whether it still helps? OIBOK's [account of organisational learning](https://intenteffect.github.io/OIBOK/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/) makes that continuing use of experience central to the inquiry.

Suppose the hypothetical pilot exposes repeated uncertainty about temporary access. The response could involve clarifying the policy, revising the request form and assigning responsibility for checking expiry conditions. Each change needs an owner and a location where people can find the current guidance. Preserve the reason for the change so later staff can understand which problem it addressed.

A later review should examine whether another employee can use the revised process and whether problems recur. This gives the organisation evidence about retained capability across cases and personnel. Completing a successful pilot would still leave wider questions about transferability and OIBOK's overall model open.
