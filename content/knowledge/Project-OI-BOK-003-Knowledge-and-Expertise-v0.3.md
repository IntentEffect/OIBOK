## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **organisational knowledge and expertise**: what an organisation knows, where that knowledge resides, how relevant expertise becomes visible and accessible, and how knowledge is brought to bear on organisational situations.

Its central question is:

> **How does knowledge held across people, relationships, practices, records and systems become available to the organisation at the point where it matters?**

The previous chapters established that:

1. a material event must first become an organisational signal;
2. the signal must receive appropriate attention;
3. relevant organisational context must be assembled;
4. and the organisation must then form an orientation capable of guiding response.

Knowledge and expertise are central to that process.

```text
Signal or experience
        ↓
Attention
        ↓
Context
        ↓
Knowledge and expertise
        ↓
Organisational orientation
```

An organisation may possess the answer and still fail to use it.

The answer may be:

- inside one employee’s experience;
- embedded in a routine;
- preserved in a prior decision;
- distributed across several teams;
- encoded in a system;
- represented imperfectly in a document;
- available through an external partner;
- or discoverable only through the relationships among people, tools and work.

This chapter seeks to establish:

- how organisational knowledge differs from information and documentation;
- how expertise differs from tenure, title and general intelligence;
- where knowledge resides;
- how organisations know who knows what;
- what makes expertise accessible, credible and actionable;
- why transfer is difficult;
- how tacit and explicit knowledge interact;
- how expertise becomes an organisational capability rather than an individual dependency;
- how AI changes knowledge work and expertise;
- and which observable conditions may indicate stronger Organisational Intelligence.

---

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **Organisational knowledge is not the total information stored by the organisation. It is the distributed capacity to create, retain, locate, evaluate, combine and apply relevant understanding through people, relationships, practices, artefacts and systems.**

Expertise is one of the most consequential forms of that capacity.

Yet the presence of expertise is not sufficient.

Faraj and Sproull found that software-team performance was strongly related to **expertise coordination**, which they described through three requirements:

- knowing where expertise is located;
- knowing where expertise is needed;
- and bringing the required expertise to bear.[^1]

This supports a critical Project OI distinction:

```text
Expertise present
        ≠
Expertise available
        ≠
Expertise applied
        ≠
Expertise retained by the organisation
```

A business may employ highly capable people and still make weak decisions because it cannot:

- recognise the knowledge required;
- locate the relevant expert;
- gain timely access;
- distinguish expertise from confidence or status;
- translate specialist knowledge into the current context;
- integrate competing perspectives;
- connect advice to legitimate authority;
- or preserve what the organisation learned.

The practical intelligence of the organisation therefore depends less on how much knowledge exists in total than on how reliably knowledge moves from **where it resides** to **where it is needed**.

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Information

Information is data organised or communicated in a form capable of reducing uncertainty for a recipient.

Information may be correct and still fail to provide:

- understanding;
- judgement;
- applicable know-how;
- or a reliable basis for action.

### 3.2 Knowledge

Project OI uses **knowledge** to mean:

> **Understanding, know-how, judgement or justified belief capable of informing interpretation or action within a domain.**

Knowledge may be:

- explicit;
- tacit;
- individual;
- collective;
- embedded;
- procedural;
- declarative;
- relational;
- experiential;
- or externally sourced.

### 3.3 Knowing

**Knowing** is knowledge enacted in practice.

Orlikowski argues that knowing how to perform complex organisational work is not merely a static asset held by an actor. It is an ongoing social accomplishment reproduced through everyday practice.[^2]

This distinction matters because an organisation may possess records describing work while lacking the collective capability to perform it reliably.

### 3.4 Organisational Knowledge

Project OI currently defines **organisational knowledge** as:

> **Knowledge available through the organisation’s people, relationships, practices, routines, artefacts, systems and external ties that can influence organisational interpretation or action.**

This definition does not assume that the organisation knows everything its members know.

Knowledge becomes organisationally available only where the organisation can:

- access it;
- interpret it;
- connect it to the situation;
- and allow it to influence response.

### 3.5 Expertise

Project OI uses **expertise** to mean:

> **High-level, domain-specific capacity to recognise relevant patterns, diagnose conditions, exercise judgement and perform effectively, developed through structured knowledge and sufficiently rich practice and learning.**

A recent integrative review describes expertise as domain-specific hierarchical knowledge structures developed over time, with the quality of education, training, practice and learning opportunities affecting the expertise acquired.[^3]

### 3.6 Expert

An **expert** is a person recognised as possessing expertise relevant to a domain or problem.

Recognition does not prove that the person’s expertise is:

- current;
- applicable;
- unbiased;
- available;
- or sufficient for the present situation.

### 3.7 Experience

**Experience** is exposure to events or practice over time.

Experience can contribute to expertise, but they are not equivalent.

Years of service, age, seniority or repeated exposure may be weak proxies where the person has received:

- poor feedback;
- narrow task variety;
- low-quality practice;
- or little opportunity to update assumptions.

### 3.8 Skill

A **skill** is a learned ability to perform a task or class of tasks.

Expertise usually involves multiple skills plus:

- structured domain knowledge;
- pattern recognition;
- judgement;
- and adaptation to variation.

### 3.9 Competence

**Competence** is sufficient ability to perform work to an expected standard in a defined context.

Competence does not necessarily imply expert-level performance.

### 3.10 Tacit Knowledge

**Tacit knowledge** is knowledge that is difficult to articulate completely and is often inseparable from experience, perception, practice or relationship.

Tacit does not simply mean undocumented.

### 3.11 Explicit Knowledge

**Explicit knowledge** is knowledge that has been articulated into a communicable representation, such as:

- language;
- diagrams;
- procedures;
- rules;
- records;
- models;
- or examples.

Explicit representation can improve transfer without reproducing the complete tacit understanding that produced it.

### 3.12 Knowledge Authority

**Knowledge authority** is the legitimacy and reliability a source holds for a particular claim or domain.

It is distinct from **decision authority**.

A person may know what should happen but lack authority to decide. A decision-maker may possess authority but require specialist knowledge before acting.

### 3.13 Expertise Location

**Expertise location** is the organisation’s capacity to identify where relevant knowledge or skill resides.

### 3.14 Expertise Routing

Project OI uses **expertise routing** as a working term for:

> **The process of matching an organisational knowledge need with relevant, accessible and credible expertise, then bringing that expertise into interpretation, decision or work.**

### 3.15 Transactive Memory System

A **transactive memory system** is a shared system for dividing, encoding, locating and retrieving knowledge across members, often summarised as knowing who knows what.

It includes more than an expertise directory. It depends on differentiated knowledge, credible attribution and coordination.[^4]

### 3.16 Knowledge Transfer

**Knowledge transfer** occurs where one organisational unit’s experience or knowledge changes the knowledge or performance of another.

Transfer is not equivalent to sending information.

### 3.17 Knowledge Integration

**Knowledge integration** is the process through which specialist contributions are combined into coordinated organisational understanding or capability.

## 4. Knowledge Is Not a Repository

Organisations often treat knowledge as a storage problem.

The assumed sequence is:

```text
Capture information
→ store it
→ make it searchable
→ knowledge becomes available
```

That sequence is incomplete.

A repository can contain:

- accurate documents;
- useful examples;
- old decisions;
- technical records;
- policies;
- and lessons learned

while the organisation remains unable to determine:

- which source governs;
- who understands the issue;
- what knowledge is missing;
- which interpretation is credible;
- how the knowledge applies to the present case;
- or what should happen next.

Search improves retrieval.

It does not by itself create:

- understanding;
- expertise;
- problem reformulation;
- validation;
- judgement;
- authority;
- or application.

Cross and Sproull found that relationships with knowledgeable people provided more than answers. They supplied:

1. solutions;
2. referrals to other people or sources;
3. problem reformulation;
4. validation;
5. and legitimation.[^5]

This is why an experienced colleague can be more valuable than a perfect search result.

The colleague may recognise that the original question is wrong.

They may know who else should be involved.

They may identify the exception that changes the answer.

They may validate whether the proposed response is plausible.

They may also provide the legitimacy needed for others to act.

A repository stores representations.

Organisational knowing requires relationships, judgement and use.

## 5. Where Organisational Knowledge Resides

Knowledge is distributed across several organisational reservoirs.

### 5.1 Individuals

People carry:

- domain knowledge;
- experience;
- relationships;
- memory;
- skill;
- judgement;
- and awareness of exceptions.

Individual knowledge is valuable but fragile where no organisational access path exists.

### 5.2 Groups and Teams

Teams develop:

- shared language;
- differentiated expertise;
- coordination patterns;
- expectations;
- and collective familiarity.

A team can know how to perform work even where no individual can describe every part of the capability.

### 5.3 Relationships

Relationships carry knowledge about:

- who knows what;
- who can be trusted;
- who is available;
- who has authority;
- and how to gain access.

Borgatti and Cross found that information seeking was shaped by whether the seeker:

- knew what another person knew;
- valued that knowledge;
- could gain timely access;
- and perceived the relationship as usable.[^6]

### 5.4 Practices

Knowledge is enacted through how work is actually performed.

Brown and Duguid argue that actual workplace practice often differs from official manuals, training programmes and job descriptions. Communities of practice connect working, learning and innovation.[^7]

### 5.5 Routines and Processes

Routines can retain:

- sequence;
- coordination;
- decision rules;
- roles;
- and repeated organisational responses.

A routine can carry knowledge without every participant understanding its complete origin.

It can also preserve obsolete knowledge.

### 5.6 Artefacts

Knowledge may be represented in:

- documents;
- diagrams;
- decision records;
- code;
- models;
- checklists;
- templates;
- contracts;
- dashboards;
- and evidence.

Artefacts improve persistence and transfer where their meaning, authority and currentness remain visible.

### 5.7 Technical Systems

Systems may embody knowledge through:

- data models;
- business rules;
- validation;
- workflow;
- access controls;
- recommendations;
- and automated action.

System-embedded knowledge can scale.

It can also become difficult to inspect, challenge or update.

### 5.8 Language and Classification

Shared vocabulary, definitions and categories influence what the organisation can:

- recognise;
- distinguish;
- discuss;
- and coordinate.

### 5.9 Culture and Norms

Organisational norms affect:

- which knowledge is valued;
- who is considered credible;
- whether uncertainty can be admitted;
- whether dissent is safe;
- and whether knowledge is shared.

### 5.10 External Networks

Customers, suppliers, consultants, professional communities, regulators and research networks may hold knowledge the organisation needs.

External knowledge becomes organisationally useful only where the organisation can recognise, absorb and apply it.

### 5.11 Interactions Among People, Tasks and Tools

Argote and Ingram argue that knowledge embedded in interactions among people, tasks and tools can be especially difficult to transfer because the interaction may not fit a new context.[^8]

This is a critical point.

The organisation may not be able to transfer a capability by copying:

- the procedure;
- the software;
- or the employee

in isolation.

The knowledge may reside in the configuration among them.

## 6. Individual Knowledge Does Not Automatically Become Organisational Knowledge

Kogut and Zander argue that firms do more than aggregate what individuals know. Organisational knowledge is also expressed through the organising principles by which people cooperate.[^9]

This creates an important boundary.

An organisation does not know something merely because one employee knows it.

The knowledge becomes more organisational where it is:

- locatable;
- accessible;
- interpretable;
- trusted appropriately;
- connected to work;
- available beyond one relationship;
- retained through turnover;
- and capable of improving future response.

### Weak Organisational Availability

```text
One person knows
→ colleagues rely on personal memory
→ no durable relationship or record exists
→ the person leaves
→ capability disappears
```

### Stronger Organisational Availability

```text
Expert knowledge exists
→ expertise and scope are discoverable
→ relevant work routes to the expert
→ judgement and evidence enter the decision
→ rationale and outcome are preserved
→ future people, workflows and systems can reuse the learning
```

The second model does not require complete codification.

It requires a reliable organisational path to the knowledge and enough retention for the organisation to benefit over time.

## 7. Expertise Is Not the Same as Tenure

Organisations regularly use proxies for expertise:

- job title;
- seniority;
- credentials;
- reputation;
- years of experience;
- confidence;
- visibility;
- or managerial status.

Those proxies may be useful.

They may also be misleading.

Rousseau and Stouten’s 2025 review notes that organisational research often measures expertise through years of experience rather than the quality and structure of the experience itself. This produces less consistent relationships with performance than measures focused on knowledge, cognitive processes and capability.[^3]

Expertise is:

- domain-specific;
- developed;
- maintained;
- and conditional.

A person may be highly expert in one class of problem and poorly calibrated in a nearby domain.

A long-serving employee may understand historical exceptions but lack current technical knowledge.

A new employee may bring strong external expertise while lacking organisational context.

A credential may establish minimum preparation without proving present performance.

A confident communicator may be more visible than a quieter expert.

### Expertise Development Requires More Than Repetition

Expertise usually develops through some combination of:

- structured knowledge;
- varied and meaningful practice;
- timely feedback;
- opportunities to detect error;
- increasing difficulty;
- reflection;
- and continued updating.

Repeated performance without feedback can stabilise weak practice.

The organisation should therefore ask:

> **What evidence demonstrates this person’s current expertise for this specific problem?**

not only:

> **How long have they been here?**

## 8. The Expertise Availability Chain

Project OI proposes the following working chain:

```text
Knowledge need recognised
        ↓
Relevant expertise identified
        ↓
Expertise accessed
        ↓
Expertise evaluated
        ↓
Expertise translated into the situation
        ↓
Contributions integrated
        ↓
Knowledge influences authorised action
        ↓
Rationale and outcomes retained
```

Each transition can fail.

### 8.1 Need Recognition

The organisation must recognise that specialist knowledge is required.

A familiar-looking problem may conceal:

- legal;
- technical;
- cultural;
- safety;
- customer;
- or operational complexity.

### 8.2 Identification

The organisation must know:

- who knows;
- what they know;
- how current that knowledge is;
- and where supporting knowledge resides.

### 8.3 Access

The expert must be:

- reachable;
- available in time;
- willing or permitted to contribute;
- and not overwhelmed by every ambiguous request.

### 8.4 Evaluation

The organisation must distinguish:

- relevant expertise;
- adjacent expertise;
- formal authority;
- reputation;
- confidence;
- and evidence.

### 8.5 Translation

Specialist knowledge must be translated into:

- the current organisational context;
- the vocabulary of other functions;
- the relevant decision;
- and the consequences for work.

### 8.6 Integration

Competing expert contributions may need to be combined without:

- averaging incompatible claims;
- hiding disagreement;
- or allowing status to substitute for evidence.

### 8.7 Application

Knowledge must influence:

- orientation;
- decision;
- workflow;
- action;
- or deliberate non-action.

### 8.8 Retention

The organisation should preserve:

- material rationale;
- sources;
- conditions;
- exceptions;
- outcome;
- and what changed.

Expertise that enters one meeting and disappears has helped the episode.

It has not necessarily strengthened the organisation.

## 9. Theoretical Foundations

### 9.1 The Knowledge-Based View of the Firm

Grant conceptualises the firm as an institution for integrating the specialist knowledge of its members.[^10]

This perspective treats knowledge application and integration as central organisational functions.

The organisation exists partly because complex work requires:

- differentiated specialist knowledge;
- coordination;
- and mechanisms for combining contributions

without requiring every individual to know everything.

### 9.2 Knowledge as Individual and Social

Kogut and Zander distinguish knowledge held by individuals from knowledge expressed through regularities of cooperation in a social community.[^9]

This supports a multilevel view:

- individuals hold knowledge;
- relationships distribute access;
- practices enact knowing;
- and organising principles allow knowledge to become collective capability.

### 9.3 Tacit and Explicit Knowledge

Nonaka’s organisational knowledge-creation theory describes a continuous dialogue between tacit and explicit knowledge, with organisations amplifying knowledge developed through individuals.[^11]

The distinction is valuable, but it should not be interpreted as a guarantee that all tacit knowledge can be converted cleanly into explicit form. Later work by Nonaka and von Krogh addresses controversy around tacit knowledge and treats tacit and explicit knowledge as distinguishable along a continuum.[^12]

Project OI therefore adopts a restrained position:

- some knowledge can be articulated;
- articulation can improve access and transfer;
- representation remains partial;
- and practice, interaction and judgement may remain necessary.

### 9.4 Communities of Practice

Brown and Duguid show that working, learning and innovation are connected through communities of practice, and that actual practice often diverges from formal organisational descriptions.[^7]

This explains why:

- process manuals may be accurate but insufficient;
- peer relationships matter;
- examples and stories carry knowledge;
- and knowledge creation cannot be separated entirely from work.

### 9.5 Knowing in Practice

Orlikowski treats knowing as an ongoing social accomplishment rather than a static stock.[^2]

This supports the distinction between:

- owning a description of capability;
- and collectively enacting the capability under real conditions.

### 9.6 Transactive Memory Systems

Ren and Argote’s review describes transactive memory systems as multidimensional systems through which groups differentiate, coordinate and trust knowledge responsibilities.[^4]

A strong transactive memory system helps members:

- specialise;
- avoid duplicating all knowledge;
- locate the right person;
- and coordinate retrieval.

It can also create dependency where expertise becomes inaccessible or leaves.

### 9.7 Expertise Coordination

Faraj and Sproull demonstrate that team performance depends not only on expertise present, but on the team’s ability to coordinate it.[^1]

Their formulation provides one of the strongest foundations for the Project OI **Know** capability.

### 9.8 Relational Information Seeking

Borgatti and Cross show that information seeking depends on learned characteristics of relationships, including awareness, perceived value and timely access.[^6]

An expertise directory can identify a person.

It cannot create:

- trust;
- accessibility;
- shared language;
- or a usable relationship.

### 9.9 Actionable Knowledge

Cross and Sproull show that knowledgeable relationships provide several contributions beyond direct answers, including referrals, reformulation, validation and legitimation.[^5]

This suggests that AI or enterprise search should not be assessed only on answer retrieval.

The system should also help the organisation:

- identify missing experts;
- reformulate the problem;
- test the proposed interpretation;
- and understand whether the result is legitimate for use.

### 9.10 Knowledge Creation, Retention and Transfer

Argote, McEvily and Reagans organise knowledge management around creation, retention and transfer, with outcomes shaped by the characteristics of units, relationships and knowledge.[^13]

Project OI adds an explicit connection to orientation and response:

```text
Create
→ retain
→ locate
→ transfer
→ integrate
→ apply
→ observe outcome
→ improve
```

## 10. Tacit Knowledge and the Limits of Codification

Tacit knowledge is often discussed as though the problem were simply to document it.

That misunderstands the concept.

Tacit knowledge may involve:

- perceptual discrimination;
- embodied skill;
- timing;
- relational judgement;
- situational recognition;
- and patterns developed through experience.

An expert may recognise that something is wrong before they can explain every cue.

A craftsperson may adjust technique through feel.

A manager may recognise a politically unsafe recommendation.

A customer-service employee may know that the literal policy does not fit the relationship.

### Codification Can Help

Organisations can externalise:

- examples;
- heuristics;
- decision criteria;
- failure modes;
- exceptions;
- evidence;
- and rationale.

This can:

- improve access;
- support training;
- reduce repeated explanation;
- and preserve learning.

### Codification Has Limits

The resulting artefact may not preserve:

- the original practice;
- the ability to recognise when the rule does not apply;
- the relationships that supported judgement;
- or the feedback required to maintain skill.

### Project OI Guardrail

> **Externalise what can be represented, preserve access to expertise for what cannot, and make the limits of the representation visible.**

A knowledge system should be able to say:

- this guidance requires experienced judgement;
- the source contains undocumented exceptions;
- the expert should be consulted above this risk threshold;
- the example is illustrative, not governing;
- or the current case falls outside prior experience.

## 11. Knowledge Transfer Is Recontextualisation

Knowledge transfer is often represented as movement:

```text
Source
→ document or system
→ recipient
```

The harder problem is adaptation.

Knowledge developed in one:

- team;
- customer relationship;
- technology;
- jurisdiction;
- product;
- or operating environment

may not fit another without translation.

Argote and Ingram emphasise that knowledge embedded in interactions among people, tools and tasks is difficult to transfer because those interactions may not fit the receiving context.[^8]

A successful transfer therefore requires:

1. identifying what knowledge is being transferred;
2. preserving the conditions under which it worked;
3. understanding the receiving context;
4. adapting without destroying the useful mechanism;
5. verifying performance;
6. and retaining local learning.

### Weak Transfer

```text
Copy the procedure
→ train the recipient
→ assume capability transferred
```

### Stronger Transfer

```text
Identify the underlying know-how
→ preserve source and conditions
→ compare receiving context
→ involve relevant practitioners
→ adapt and test
→ observe outcomes
→ update local practice
```

Transfer may also change the source.

The questions and failures encountered by the recipient can expose assumptions the original team had never articulated.

## 12. Expertise Must Be Integrated, Not Merely Consulted

Inviting an expert to a meeting does not prove that expertise shaped the response.

Experts may be:

- consulted too late;
- given an incomplete brief;
- asked the wrong question;
- ignored because the answer is inconvenient;
- overruled by hierarchy;
- or treated as final authority outside their domain.

Integration requires the organisation to connect the contribution to:

- the actual problem;
- other relevant disciplines;
- current evidence;
- decision authority;
- and the work that follows.

### Multi-Expert Situations

Complex problems may require:

- technical expertise;
- customer knowledge;
- legal interpretation;
- operational experience;
- financial judgement;
- and frontline observation.

These contributions may legitimately conflict.

The organisation should preserve:

- each expert’s domain;
- evidence;
- assumptions;
- uncertainty;
- and implications.

Consensus is not always the correct output.

Sometimes the correct output is:

- a bounded disagreement;
- reversible action;
- additional evidence;
- escalation;
- or separate response paths.

## 13. Knowledge Authority and Decision Authority

Organisations frequently confuse knowing with deciding.

### Knowledge Authority

Who or what is credible regarding the claim?

Examples:

- a policy owner;
- a technical specialist;
- a frontline observer;
- a verified dataset;
- a customer;
- a regulator;
- or a tested system.

### Decision Authority

Who is authorised to commit the organisation?

Examples:

- delegated manager;
- approver;
- Process owner;
- governance body;
- or authorised workflow boundary.

The two may overlap.

They often do not.

### Example

A cybersecurity analyst may have the strongest knowledge of a vulnerability.

A product owner may understand the customer consequence.

A legal adviser may understand disclosure obligations.

An executive may hold authority to accept or mitigate the risk.

A strong organisational response connects those roles without allowing formal hierarchy to erase knowledge authority or allowing expertise to bypass legitimate decision authority.

### Project OI Principle

> **Make knowledge authority and decision authority visible as separate relationships, then define how they interact.**

## 14. Expertise Recognition and Credibility

An organisation must evaluate whether a source is relevant and credible.

Potential evidence of expertise includes:

- demonstrated performance;
- quality of practice;
- domain-specific training;
- peer recognition;
- calibrated judgement;
- relevant experience;
- current engagement with the domain;
- and the ability to explain limits.

No single indicator is sufficient in every setting.

### Expertise Is Contextual

The organisation should ask:

- expert in what?
- for which class of task?
- under which conditions?
- based on what evidence?
- how current?
- with which known limitations?
- and who can challenge the judgement?

### Confidence Is Not Expertise

Confident communication can increase perceived credibility.

The organisation should preserve ways to inspect:

- evidence;
- track record;
- calibration;
- and domain fit.

### Reputation Can Lag

An expert reputation may remain after:

- the technology changes;
- the operating context changes;
- the person stops practising;
- or the domain moves beyond their experience.

Expertise maps require maintenance.

## 15. Expertise Concentration and Organisational Fragility

SMBs often benefit from highly concentrated expertise.

The founder or a long-serving employee may know:

- every important customer;
- why the process works the way it does;
- which supplier can be trusted;
- which exceptions matter;
- and how to interpret weak signals.

This can make the organisation fast.

It can also make it fragile.

### Expertise Concentration Risks

- one person becomes the default routing layer;
- work waits for their availability;
- other employees stop developing judgement;
- the expert receives every ambiguous problem;
- decisions remain undocumented;
- succession becomes difficult;
- and the organisation loses capability when the person leaves.

### Reducing Fragility Does Not Mean Removing the Expert

The objective is not to convert every expert judgement into a rigid rule.

It is to create:

- visible expertise domains;
- clear access paths;
- overlap and backup;
- examples and decision records;
- shared practice;
- developmental opportunities;
- and retained learning.

### Project OI Working Term: Expertise Dependency

**Expertise dependency** is the degree to which an organisational capability relies on continued access to one or a small number of people.

The term is descriptive and requires later operationalisation.

## 16. Knowledge Across Boundaries

Knowledge is often lost or distorted when it crosses:

- functions;
- professions;
- teams;
- organisations;
- seniority levels;
- people and systems;
- or humans and AI.

### Boundary Problems

Different groups may disagree on:

- terminology;
- evidence;
- risk;
- time horizon;
- quality;
- and what constitutes completion.

A technical explanation may be accurate but unusable by a customer team.

A legal constraint may be simplified until it becomes wrong.

A frontline observation may be dismissed because it lacks executive language.

An AI summary may remove the source-specific uncertainty that specialists considered material.

### Boundary Work

Knowledge crossing a boundary may require:

- translation;
- examples;
- shared objects;
- joint review;
- preserved source language;
- and an explicit record of local implications.

The objective is semantic interoperability, not universal vocabulary.

## 17. AI Changes the Distribution of Expertise

AI can make expert-derived patterns available to more people at lower marginal cost.

This creates genuine opportunities.

It also creates new organisational risks.

### 17.1 AI Can Accelerate Novices

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied a generative AI assistant used by customer-support agents. They found a 15 per cent overall productivity increase, with substantially larger gains among less-skilled and less-experienced workers. Newer workers moved down the experience curve faster, and some gains persisted when the AI became temporarily unavailable.[^14]

The system had been trained partly on conversations from top performers.

This suggests that AI can distribute aspects of expert practice through:

- recommendations;
- examples;
- language;
- retrieval;
- and real-time guidance.

### 17.2 AI Can Compress Performance Differences

Lower-skilled workers can become more like higher performers on tasks the system represents well.

That can:

- improve consistency;
- accelerate onboarding;
- expand access to competent performance;
- and reduce the cost of routine expertise.

It does not guarantee expert judgement across novel or ambiguous conditions.

### 17.3 Expert Workers May Benefit Less

In the same study, more-skilled workers received smaller productivity gains, and there was evidence of a small decline in their conversation quality.[^14]

The authors also raise a longer-term concern: if top workers produce fewer original solutions because they follow model recommendations, the organisation may weaken the stream of expert behaviour needed to improve future systems.

### 17.4 The Frontier Is Jagged

Dell’Acqua and colleagues found that AI improved consultant performance substantially for tasks inside its capability frontier but reduced performance for a task outside that frontier. The boundary was not obvious even to experienced knowledge workers, and overreliance contributed to failure.[^15]

The organisational question is not simply whether the person is expert.

It is whether the human–AI system can recognise:

- which task is inside the model’s capability;
- where expert judgement remains essential;
- and when confident output should be challenged.

### 17.5 Human–AI Combination Is Not Automatically Superior

Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone’s meta-analysis found that human–AI systems did not automatically outperform the strongest human or AI alternative. Complementarity depended on task and interaction design.[^16]

This challenges the assumption that adding a human reviewer to AI, or adding AI to an expert, necessarily creates a better system.

### 17.6 Algorithmic Advice Can Weaken Experiential Learning

Sundaresan and Guler found that adoption of an algorithmic clinical recommendation tool was associated with flatter organisational learning curves, which they explain through reduced knowledge retention and retrieval, skill erosion and routine disruption.[^17]

The implication is not that recommendation tools should be avoided.

It is that immediate performance and long-term capability must be evaluated separately.

### 17.7 AI Can Become an Unearned Knowledge Authority

AI systems can produce:

- fluent explanations;
- confident recommendations;
- detailed citations;
- and expert-like language.

Those characteristics can cause model output to be treated as knowledge authority without sufficient evidence.

A model’s output should remain distinguishable as:

- retrieved source material;
- synthesis;
- prediction;
- inference;
- recommendation;
- or generated content.

### 17.8 AI Does Not Eliminate Expertise Routing

A well-designed AI system can help the organisation:

- locate sources;
- identify possible experts;
- recognise recurring patterns;
- prepare a brief;
- expose missing evidence;
- and route a question.

It should also know when to stop.

AI can reduce the cost of routine knowledge access while increasing the value of experts who can:

- diagnose novel situations;
- evaluate evidence;
- recognise the frontier;
- challenge assumptions;
- and develop new knowledge.

## 18. Human–AI Expertise Design

Project OI proposes that stronger human–AI expertise systems should preserve six distinctions.

### 18.1 Model Capability Versus Organisational Authority

The model may be capable of producing an answer.

It may not be authorised to decide or act.

### 18.2 Model Confidence Versus Source Authority

A confident model output does not make a weak source authoritative.

### 18.3 Retrieved Knowledge Versus Generated Inference

The user should be able to distinguish:

- source;
- extraction;
- synthesis;
- and inference.

### 18.4 Routine Guidance Versus Novel Judgement

The system should recognise when work falls outside:

- known examples;
- approved rules;
- model capability;
- or prior organisational experience.

### 18.5 Immediate Performance Versus Capability Development

A recommendation can improve today’s output while weakening:

- practice;
- learning;
- independent diagnosis;
- or knowledge creation.

### 18.6 Expert Augmentation Versus Expert Displacement

AI should help experts:

- search;
- compare;
- model;
- draft;
- monitor;
- and transfer knowledge

without suppressing the expert contributions needed for future learning.

## 19. Knowledge and Expertise Failure Modes

### 19.1 Expertise Invisibility

Relevant expertise exists but the organisation does not know where.

### 19.2 Expertise Misclassification

A person is treated as expert because of title, tenure, confidence or proximity rather than demonstrated domain fit.

### 19.3 Expert Bottleneck

One expert receives every ambiguous request and becomes a queue.

### 19.4 Founder as Organisational Router

Every difficult question defaults to the founder.

### 19.5 Knowledge Hoarding

Knowledge is withheld for status, security, workload or incentive reasons.

### 19.6 Search Substitution

Search results are treated as a substitute for judgement or expertise.

### 19.7 Documentation Substitution

The organisation assumes that an artefact fully reproduces the capability that created it.

### 19.8 Repository Graveyard

Knowledge is captured but not maintained, trusted or used.

### 19.9 Stale Expertise

Past expertise is applied after the domain or technology changes.

### 19.10 Expertise Without Access

The organisation knows who the expert is but cannot reach them in time.

### 19.11 Access Without Translation

The specialist contribution cannot be understood or used by the receiving group.

### 19.12 Consultation Without Influence

The expert is consulted but the contribution does not affect the decision or work.

### 19.13 Authority Confusion

The expert is treated as decision-maker, or the decision-maker substitutes authority for knowledge.

### 19.14 Status-Driven Consensus

The most senior or confident view suppresses stronger evidence.

### 19.15 Transfer Without Recontextualisation

A practice is copied into a context where its assumptions do not hold.

### 19.16 Expertise Concentration

Capability remains dependent on one person or relationship.

### 19.17 AI Authority Laundering

Generated synthesis is presented as established organisational knowledge.

### 19.18 Expertise Homogenisation

AI recommendations cause workers to converge on familiar patterns and reduce original expert contributions.

### 19.19 Automation Deskilling

Workers lose opportunities to practise diagnosis, judgement or manual skill.

### 19.20 Knowledge Without Application

The organisation knows but cannot coordinate a response.

### 19.21 Application Without Retention

Expert judgement resolves the episode but does not improve future organisational capacity.

## 20. Knowledge and Expertise Quality

Project OI proposes the following working quality dimensions.

### 20.1 Relevance

Does the knowledge or expertise apply to the problem?

### 20.2 Domain Fit

Is the expertise specific to the task and context?

### 20.3 Evidence

What supports the claim or judgement?

### 20.4 Authority

What knowledge authority does the source hold?

### 20.5 Currency

Is the knowledge current enough for the purpose?

### 20.6 Calibration

Does the source recognise uncertainty and limits?

### 20.7 Accessibility

Can the organisation gain timely access?

### 20.8 Interpretability

Can the knowledge be understood by the receiving actor?

### 20.9 Integrability

Can it be combined with other necessary perspectives?

### 20.10 Actionability

Can it influence orientation or authorised response?

### 20.11 Contestability

Can the knowledge or expert judgement be challenged?

### 20.12 Resilience

Does the capability survive absence, turnover or system failure?

### 20.13 Retention

Can learning influence future work?

### 20.14 Development

Does use of the knowledge strengthen or weaken future human and organisational capability?

## 21. The Knowledge and Expertise Routing Process

Project OI proposes the following working process.

```text
Define the organisational knowledge need
        ↓
Identify required knowledge and expertise domains
        ↓
Discover relevant people, sources, practices and systems
        ↓
Evaluate domain fit, authority, evidence and currency
        ↓
Gain timely access
        ↓
Translate into the current context
        ↓
Integrate contributions and preserve disagreement
        ↓
Connect knowledge to legitimate decision and action
        ↓
Record rationale, outcome and learning
        ↓
Update organisational memory and expertise maps
```

### 21.1 Define the Need

State:

- the question;
- the decision;
- the task;
- the risk;
- and why specialist knowledge may be required.

### 21.2 Identify Domains

Determine which knowledge is needed.

Examples:

- technical;
- customer;
- legal;
- operational;
- commercial;
- safety;
- cultural;
- or historical.

### 21.3 Discover

Search:

- people;
- prior work;
- decisions;
- records;
- communities;
- external sources;
- and systems.

### 21.4 Evaluate

Assess:

- domain fit;
- evidence;
- currentness;
- independence;
- authority;
- and known limitations.

### 21.5 Access

Provide a usable brief and gain access in time.

### 21.6 Translate

Connect specialist knowledge to:

- current context;
- organisational vocabulary;
- consequences;
- and decision options.

### 21.7 Integrate

Preserve:

- shared facts;
- competing interpretations;
- assumptions;
- and unresolved questions.

### 21.8 Apply

Connect the integrated orientation to:

- the authorised decision;
- workflow;
- action;
- or deliberate monitoring.

### 21.9 Retain

Record:

- material rationale;
- evidence;
- conditions;
- expert contributions;
- and outcome.

### 21.10 Improve

Update:

- knowledge;
- expertise map;
- routing;
- training;
- overlap;
- and future context assembly.

## 22. Proportionate Expertise Involvement

Not every task should wait for an expert.

Expert involvement should be proportionate to:

- novelty;
- risk;
- reversibility;
- uncertainty;
- consequence;
- and the quality of existing rules.

### Low-Risk Routine Work

May use:

- approved guidance;
- examples;
- AI assistance;
- and exception escalation.

### Moderate-Risk or Ambiguous Work

May require:

- specialist review;
- evidence check;
- or decision-owner consultation.

### High-Risk, Novel or Irreversible Work

Should involve:

- relevant expertise;
- explicit authority;
- source evidence;
- disagreement handling;
- and preserved rationale.

### Project OI Principle

> **Do not turn every expert into a bottleneck. Build clear rules for when represented knowledge is sufficient and when situated expertise is required.**

## 23. SMB Context

SMBs often possess rich knowledge through close relationships and long experience.

They may have weak infrastructure for making that knowledge organisational.

### Common Strengths

- direct customer knowledge;
- rapid access to founders;
- informal communication;
- integrated commercial and operational awareness;
- and practical judgement.

### Common Weaknesses

- founder dependence;
- limited overlap;
- tacit decisions;
- inconsistent records;
- weak source authority;
- absent expertise maps;
- and knowledge loss through turnover.

### Lightweight SMB Practices

- record the rationale behind material decisions;
- identify who knows what;
- distinguish expertise from decision authority;
- maintain backups for critical domains;
- preserve examples and exceptions;
- link knowledge to real work;
- review stale guidance;
- and use AI to make expertise easier to access without hiding its source.

The objective is not enterprise knowledge bureaucracy.

It is a reliable path from knowledge need to organisational use.

## 24. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Possession Is Not Availability

> Knowledge held somewhere in the organisation contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where it can be located, accessed and applied.

### P2 — Expertise Coordination Matters

> Organisational performance depends not only on the expertise present, but on knowing where it resides, where it is needed and how to bring it to bear.

### P3 — Experience Is an Imperfect Proxy

> Years of experience will predict useful expertise less reliably than the quality, variety and feedback structure of the experience.

### P4 — Knowledge Is Distributed

> Organisational knowledge resides across people, relationships, practices, routines, artefacts, systems and external ties.

### P5 — Knowing Is Enacted

> A documented description of work is not equivalent to the collective capability to perform it.

### P6 — Tacit Knowledge Cannot Be Fully Captured

> Organisational systems should externalise useful knowledge while preserving access to situated expertise and the limits of representation.

### P7 — Transfer Requires Recontextualisation

> Knowledge transfer succeeds when the receiving context can adapt and enact the relevant mechanism, not merely receive a copy.

### P8 — Knowledge Authority Differs From Decision Authority

> Stronger organisational response requires explicit connection between credible knowledge and legitimate authority.

### P9 — Relationships Are Part of Knowledge Access

> Knowing who knows what is insufficient without trust, timely access and a usable relationship.

### P10 — Expertise Concentration Creates Fragility

> Capability dependent on one person may be operationally effective while remaining weak as Organisational Intelligence.

### P11 — Search Is Not Expertise

> Retrieval systems improve access to representations but do not automatically provide reformulation, validation, legitimation or judgement.

### P12 — AI Can Distribute Expert Patterns

> AI can accelerate novice performance where expert-derived patterns are represented well and the task falls within the model’s capability.

### P13 — AI Can Weaken Learning

> AI assistance may improve immediate output while reducing opportunities for experiential learning, skill development or original expert contribution.

### P14 — Human–AI Expertise Requires Task Fit

> Human–AI combinations will not consistently outperform the strongest human or AI alternative without appropriate task and interaction design.

### P15 — Expertise Must Remain Contestable

> Expert status and AI confidence should not prevent evidence, dissent or later outcomes from challenging a judgement.

### P16 — Use Must Improve Future Availability

> Expert involvement strengthens Organisational Intelligence where rationale and outcomes improve future knowledge, routing and capability.

## 26. Practical Principles

### 26.1 Start With the Knowledge Need

Do not begin with the repository or expert directory.

Begin with the organisational problem.

### 26.2 Ask Who Knows and Who Decides

Make expertise and authority separate and explicit.

### 26.3 Route to Domain Fit, Not Hierarchy

The most senior person is not automatically the most relevant expert.

### 26.4 Make Expertise Discoverable

Represent:

- domain;
- evidence;
- scope;
- currentness;
- access path;
- and limitations.

### 26.5 Preserve Relationships

Expertise access depends on trust, availability and shared language, not only metadata.

### 26.6 Externalise Rationale and Exceptions

Record why the rule applied and when it did not.

### 26.7 Preserve Tacit-Context Boundaries

State when an artefact is insufficient without practice or expert review.

### 26.8 Create Overlap

Build backup and succession for critical expertise.

### 26.9 Protect Experts From Becoming Queues

Use approved guidance and triage for routine questions.

### 26.10 Translate Across Boundaries

Make specialist knowledge usable without removing its conditions or uncertainty.

### 26.11 Use AI to Extend Access

Use AI for:

- retrieval;
- briefing;
- pattern recognition;
- examples;
- and routing.

Do not use fluency as proof of knowledge authority.

### 26.12 Preserve Expert Contribution

Capture:

- corrections;
- novel solutions;
- edge cases;
- and model limitations

so AI does not reduce the organisation’s future knowledge supply.

### 26.13 Evaluate Long-Term Capability

Measure whether AI support improves or weakens:

- human learning;
- independent judgement;
- knowledge creation;
- and organisational resilience.

### 26.14 Connect Use to Outcome

Later consequences should update knowledge and expertise routing.

## 27. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Knowledge and expertise form the **Know** capability within **Orient**.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Relevant context
× available knowledge
× coordinated expertise
× interpretive quality
= orientation potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Knowledge Without Routing

```text
The answer exists
→ nobody knows where
→ the organisation acts without it
```

### Expertise Without Access

```text
The expert exists
→ the expert is unavailable
→ the organisation delays or improvises
```

### Expertise Without Integration

```text
The expert advises
→ the advice is not translated or connected to authority
→ the response does not change
```

### Expertise Without Retention

```text
The expert resolves the episode
→ rationale disappears
→ the organisation pays to learn again
```

### AI Without Organisational Integration

```text
The model generates an answer
→ source, authority and task boundary are unclear
→ output appears intelligent
→ organisational capability does not improve
```

The higher-level principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when relevant knowledge and expertise can reliably reach the situation where they are needed, influence legitimate collective response, and leave the organisation better able to respond next time.**

## 29. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises individual, team and organisational research;
- does not provide a validated measure of organisational knowledge or expertise coordination;
- does not resolve every boundary between knowledge, knowing, skill, competence and capability;
- does not establish that the proposed routing process is complete;
- does not prove the Project OI propositions;
- and does not establish the generalisability of current AI studies across occupations and organisations.

Important limitations include:

### Expertise Measurement

Expertise is difficult to observe directly and is often inferred from imperfect proxies.

### Level of Analysis

Individual expertise, team transactive memory and organisation-level knowledge are related but not interchangeable.

### Tacit Knowledge

The boundary between tacit and explicit knowledge remains conceptually contested.

### AI Evidence

Current findings come from:

- specific tools;
- tasks;
- firms;
- occupations;
- and model generations.

The capability frontier changes rapidly.

### Causality

Knowledge access, expertise coordination and performance may influence one another.

### Normative Questions

The ability to mobilise expertise does not establish that the organisation’s purpose or response is ethical.
