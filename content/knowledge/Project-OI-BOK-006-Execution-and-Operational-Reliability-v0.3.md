## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **execution and operational reliability**: how an organisation performs authorised work, maintains sufficient control over changing operational conditions, detects and contains failure, recovers when necessary and completes work without losing the connection to the decision, evidence and authority that made the work legitimate.

Its central question is:

> **How does an organisation perform authorised work reliably while managing variation, failure, safety, recovery and operational control?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant organisational context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. form a useful orientation;
6. make an attributable and authorised decision;
7. and coordinate interdependent collective action.

Coordination does not guarantee execution.

The organisation must still:

- translate commitments into operational intent;
- establish readiness;
- perform work through people, routines, workflows, systems and AI;
- preserve authority and evidence as action unfolds;
- maintain an accurate view of operational state;
- detect deviation, overload, exception and failure;
- distinguish local completion from satisfaction of the wider commitment;
- contain harmful or ambiguous effects;
- retry, recover, roll back or compensate where appropriate;
- operate safely under degraded conditions;
- and transfer enough operational evidence into outcome evaluation and future learning.

```text
Authorised decision
        ↓
Coordinated collective action
        ↓
Operational readiness
        ↓
Execution under control
        ↓
Completion, recovery or safe termination
        ↓
Output and changed organisational state
        ↓
Outcome, feedback and evaluation
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what execution is;
- how execution differs from action, coordination, completion and outcome;
- what operational reliability means in a sociotechnical organisation;
- how intent becomes observable and governable work;
- how routines and procedures enable rather than merely constrain performance;
- how operational state, observability and control support action;
- how reliability differs from availability, robustness, resilience and safety;
- how faults, errors, failures, incidents and near misses should be distinguished;
- how exceptions, workarounds and local repair can restore service while concealing structural weakness;
- how recovery, rollback, compensation and degraded operation differ;
- how high-reliability organisations manage complexity and surprise;
- how automation changes human work and intervention;
- how AI and AI agents should execute within bounded identity, authority, tool and evidence conditions;
- which failure modes prevent coordinated work from becoming reliable organisational response;
- and how execution capability contributes to Organisational Intelligence.

Detailed evaluation of outcomes belongs to OI-BOK-007.

Organisational memory and learning belong to OI-BOK-008.

Capability change and adaptation belong to OI-BOK-009.

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **Execution is not the completion of assigned activity. It is the governed conversion of an authorised organisational commitment into observable state change through people and systems operating under explicit conditions of readiness, authority, control, reliability and recovery.**

Operational reliability is therefore not merely consistency.

Project OI defines it provisionally as:

> **The organisation’s capacity to perform authorised work within acceptable service, safety, integrity and time boundaries, repeatedly and over time, while detecting material deviation, containing failure and restoring or safely adapting operation when assumptions no longer hold.**

This creates several necessary distinctions:

```text
Decision
        ≠
Coordination
        ≠
Execution
        ≠
Completion
        ≠
Output
        ≠
Outcome
```

A valid decision may never be executed.

A coordinated plan may fail under real operating conditions.

A task may be completed while its dependency remains unsatisfied.

A workflow may reach a terminal state while an external effect remains ambiguous.

A reliable process may produce a harmful result if the authorised purpose or safety constraints are wrong.

A system may remain available while returning incorrect or degraded service.

A local failure may be contained without the organisation becoming more reliable.

The Project OI position is:

> **Execution capability is the organisational capacity to preserve authorised intent through operation, make current state and failure visible, manage variation proportionately, and complete, contain or recover work in a form that can be evaluated and learned from.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Authorised Work

**Authorised work** is activity that:

- follows a legitimate decision, policy, role or delegation;
- remains inside an approved scope;
- uses permitted resources and tools;
- and is attributable to an actor or mechanism.

Technical ability to perform an action is not authorisation.

### 3.2 Action

An **action** is a discrete intervention that may change:

- organisational state;
- a physical or digital environment;
- another actor’s obligations;
- or the availability of resources.

Examples:

- update a customer record;
- send a message;
- approve a payment;
- change a configuration;
- perform a physical procedure;
- or invoke an external service.

### 3.3 Execution

Project OI defines **execution** as:

> **The performance and control of authorised work through a sequence or configuration of actions that is intended to realise an organisational commitment under actual operating conditions.**

Execution includes:

- preparation;
- action;
- observation;
- adjustment;
- exception handling;
- and termination.

### 3.4 Operation

An **operation** is a bounded instance of work performed by:

- a person;
- team;
- workflow;
- machine;
- software service;
- AI agent;
- or combination of actors.

### 3.5 Operational System

An **operational system** is the sociotechnical arrangement of:

- people;
- roles;
- routines;
- workflows;
- tools;
- interfaces;
- information;
- authority;
- infrastructure;
- and environmental conditions

through which work is performed.

### 3.6 Operational Intent

**Operational intent** is the execution-ready statement of:

- what must change;
- what must remain protected;
- which conditions apply;
- who or what may act;
- and how acceptable completion will be recognised.

Operational intent translates a decision into work without reducing it to one task.

### 3.7 Action Fidelity

Project OI uses **action fidelity** as a working term for:

> **The degree to which actual execution preserves the purpose, authority, constraints and material conditions of the authorised commitment.**

Action fidelity is not literal adherence to a plan.

Adaptation can preserve intent better than rigid compliance.

### 3.8 Operational State

**Operational state** is the current condition of:

- the work;
- relevant entities;
- dependencies;
- resources;
- authority;
- and operating environment

at a specific time.

### 3.9 State Transition

A **state transition** is an attributable change from one recognised state to another.

A transition should identify:

- source state;
- intended target state;
- actor;
- authority;
- time;
- evidence;
- and result.

### 3.10 Completion

**Completion** is the governed determination that the defined execution obligations have been satisfied or terminated.

Completion should distinguish:

- successful completion;
- partial completion;
- failed completion;
- cancellation;
- safe termination;
- compensation complete;
- and indeterminate state.

### 3.11 Acceptance

**Acceptance** is the receiving actor’s or governance mechanism’s confirmation that an output or state satisfies the relevant dependency or completion condition.

Execution may finish before acceptance.

### 3.12 Operational Readiness

**Operational readiness** is the condition in which the organisation has sufficient:

- authority;
- context;
- resources;
- capability;
- capacity;
- prerequisites;
- control;
- observability;
- and failure handling

to begin work responsibly.

### 3.13 Operational Control

**Operational control** is the set of structures and mechanisms used to keep execution within authorised and acceptable boundaries.

Control may operate through:

- constraints;
- procedures;
- monitoring;
- permissions;
- feedback;
- limits;
- supervision;
- automation;
- and intervention.

Control is not synonymous with surveillance or centralisation.

### 3.14 Control Loop

A **control loop** is a recurring process that:

1. observes state;
2. compares it with intent, constraint or acceptable range;
3. selects or triggers adjustment;
4. and observes the new state.

### 3.15 Feedforward Control

**Feedforward control** acts before or at initiation to reduce predictable failure.

Examples:

- validation;
- readiness checks;
- permission checks;
- resource reservation;
- simulation;
- and preconditions.

### 3.16 Concurrent Control

**Concurrent control** acts during execution.

Examples:

- monitoring;
- rate limiting;
- supervision;
- guardrails;
- interlocks;
- and real-time approval.

### 3.17 Feedback Control

**Feedback control** uses observed results or state to adjust current execution.

This operational feedback is distinct from the broader outcome evaluation owned by Chapter 7.

### 3.18 Observability

Project OI defines **operational observability** as:

> **The organisation’s capacity to infer and inspect material operational state, progress, deviation and failure from available evidence and signals.**

Observability is broader than a dashboard.

It depends on whether the relevant state is:

- represented;
- current;
- attributable;
- and interpretable.

### 3.19 Monitoring

**Monitoring** is the repeated observation of selected operational variables, events or conditions.

Monitoring supplies data.

Observability is the resulting capacity to understand state.

### 3.20 Reliability

Adapted from dependability engineering, **reliability** is the continuity of acceptable service or performance under stated conditions and for a stated period.[^4]

Organisational reliability requires the service and conditions to be defined.

### 3.21 Availability

**Availability** is readiness to provide acceptable service when required.[^4]

A system may be available and still:

- provide wrong information;
- perform slowly;
- or violate a safety constraint.

### 3.22 Maintainability

**Maintainability** is the capacity to undergo repair, correction or modification while preserving or restoring acceptable operation.[^4]

### 3.23 Dependability

**Dependability** is an umbrella term covering attributes such as:

- reliability;
- availability;
- safety;
- integrity;
- and maintainability.[^4]

Project OI uses the term as a technical reference rather than a proposed organisation-level capability.

### 3.24 Robustness

**Robustness** is the ability to continue acceptable performance under a defined range of disturbance or variation without material reconfiguration.

### 3.25 Resilience

Project OI uses **resilience** to mean:

> **The capacity to sustain, recover or extend viable performance when disruption, saturation or surprise challenges the system’s ordinary operating assumptions.**

Woods distinguishes resilience as:

- rebound;
- robustness;
- graceful extensibility;
- and sustained adaptability.[^11]

The distinctions should not be collapsed.

### 3.26 Graceful Degradation

**Graceful degradation** is the deliberate reduction of:

- quality;
- scope;
- speed;
- or functionality

to preserve the most important service or safety conditions under constraint.

### 3.27 Safety

**Safety** is the absence of unacceptable harm or loss arising from operation.

Safety is not equivalent to reliability.

A system can perform a harmful function reliably.

### 3.28 Hazard

A **hazard** is a system state or condition that, together with particular environmental conditions, can lead to harm or loss.

### 3.29 Risk

**Risk** combines uncertainty about events or conditions with the severity and distribution of potential consequences.

Risk is not exhausted by probability multiplied by impact.

### 3.30 Fault

Adapted from dependable-computing terminology, a **fault** is the adjudged or hypothesised cause of an error.[^4]

Faults may be:

- human;
- technical;
- organisational;
- environmental;
- accidental;
- or malicious.

### 3.31 Error

An **error** is a system state capable of leading to failure.[^4]

In human work, “error” should not become a moral label for the person involved.

### 3.32 Failure

A **failure** occurs when delivered service or performance deviates from the specified or acceptable service at the system boundary.[^4]

Failure depends on:

- the boundary;
- expectation;
- and affected actor.

### 3.33 Incident

An **incident** is an operational event or condition requiring coordinated attention because:

- service;
- safety;
- integrity;
- authority;
- or continuity

may be materially affected.

### 3.34 Near Miss

A **near miss** is an event that could have produced material failure or harm but did not, because of:

- recovery;
- intervention;
- redundancy;
- timing;
- or chance.

A near miss is not proof that the system is safe.

### 3.35 Exception

An **exception** is a condition in which ordinary execution rules or assumptions do not fit.

The exception may be:

- expected and governed;
- unexpected but manageable;
- or outside authorised operation.

### 3.36 Deviation

A **deviation** is a difference between:

- expected;
- specified;
- planned;
- and actual execution.

A deviation may be beneficial, neutral or harmful.

### 3.37 Violation

A **violation** is a deliberate departure from a rule, constraint or procedure.

A violation may be:

- reckless;
- routine;
- situational;
- necessary to preserve safety;
- or evidence that the procedure is unusable.

Intent alone does not determine consequence.

### 3.38 Workaround

A **workaround** is a local action that restores or preserves immediate performance without correcting the source condition.

Workarounds can be intelligent operational repair.

They can also hide recurring failure.

### 3.39 Degraded Mode

A **degraded mode** is an explicitly recognised operating state in which the organisation provides reduced service while preserving critical constraints.

### 3.40 Redundancy

**Redundancy** is the deliberate provision of overlapping:

- capability;
- resources;
- information;
- or pathways

to reduce dependence on one component.

Redundancy can improve resilience.

It can also increase complexity and inconsistency.

### 3.41 Slack

**Slack** is available capacity or time that allows:

- variation;
- recovery;
- and adaptation

without immediate system failure.

### 3.42 Containment

**Containment** limits the spread, consequence or scope of failure.

### 3.43 Recovery

**Recovery** is the restoration of acceptable operation after disturbance or failure.

Recovery may return to:

- the previous state;
- a safe state;
- or a new viable state.

### 3.44 Repair

**Repair** corrects or bypasses the immediate condition preventing acceptable operation.

Repair does not necessarily remove the underlying fault.

### 3.45 Rollback

**Rollback** is an attempt to restore a previous system or organisational state.

Rollback is feasible only where:

- the prior state is known;
- intervening effects can be reversed;
- and external commitments do not make the state irrecoverable.

### 3.46 Compensation

**Compensation** is a new action intended to counteract or account for a completed or partially completed effect.

The saga pattern in distributed systems formalises long-lived work as a series of transactions with compensating actions where full rollback is not possible.[^19]

### 3.47 Retry

A **retry** repeats an attempted action after failure or uncertainty.

Retries are safe only where:

- the failure is transient;
- the action is idempotent or otherwise protected;
- the retry is bounded;
- and added load will not worsen the failure.

### 3.48 Idempotency

An **idempotent action** can be repeated without changing the effective result beyond the first successful application.

Organisational actions are often not naturally idempotent.

Sending the same customer message twice is not equivalent to sending it once.

### 3.49 Checkpoint

A **checkpoint** is a durable record of execution state from which work can:

- resume;
- recover;
- or be reconstructed.

### 3.50 Safe State

A **safe state** is a condition in which unacceptable harm is controlled, even if ordinary service is unavailable.

### 3.51 Human Intervention

**Human intervention** is an authorised human action that:

- supervises;
- confirms;
- corrects;
- pauses;
- redirects;
- contains;
- or terminates execution.

Human intervention is meaningful only where the person has:

- sufficient state;
- expertise;
- time;
- authority;
- and effective controls.

### 3.52 Operational Evidence

**Operational evidence** is the durable record required to understand:

- what was attempted;
- by whom or what;
- under which authority;
- with which inputs and state;
- which actions occurred;
- and how execution terminated.

### 3.53 Operational Debt

Project OI uses **operational debt** as a working term for accumulated:

- brittle workarounds;
- untested recovery;
- hidden dependencies;
- stale procedures;
- manual repair;
- and deferred reliability work

that increases future execution risk and cost.

## 4. Execution Is Distinct From Decision, Coordination and Outcome

The complete sequence is:

```text
Decision
        ↓
Authorised commitment
        ↓
Coordination
        ↓
Execution
        ↓
Output and changed state
        ↓
Outcome
```

### 4.1 Decision

The organisation commits to what should happen.

### 4.2 Coordination

The organisation arranges interdependent contributions, resources, roles and handoffs.

### 4.3 Execution

People and systems perform the work under actual conditions.

### 4.4 Output

Execution produces:

- an artefact;
- message;
- transition;
- payment;
- release;
- service;
- or other immediate result.

### 4.5 Outcome

The output interacts with:

- customers;
- markets;
- systems;
- people;
- and environment

to produce consequences.

### Example — Customer Refund

Decision:

> Approve a $2,400 refund under the customer’s contract.

Coordination:

- Finance confirms authority.
- Customer Service prepares communication.
- Billing corrects the charge.
- Operations reviews similar transactions.

Execution:

- refund instruction submitted;
- payment provider confirms;
- customer record updated;
- message sent;
- related-review workflow started.

Output:

- refund processed;
- customer notified;
- case state changed.

Outcome:

- customer receives funds;
- trust may improve;
- billing defect may or may not be corrected;
- other customers may remain affected.

### Boundary Principle

> **Execution owns the faithful and reliable performance of authorised work. Outcome evaluation owns what the work caused.**

## 5. Operational Intent and Action Fidelity

A decision must be translated into operational intent before execution can be controlled.

### 5.1 Operational Intent Should State

```text
Purpose
Authorised change
Protected constraints
Scope
Actor or mechanism
Prerequisites
Expected state transition
Permitted variation
Prohibited effects
Completion condition
Failure behaviour
Recovery or compensation
Evidence requirement
```

### 5.2 Weak Operational Intent

> Fix the customer account.

### 5.3 Stronger Operational Intent

> Apply the approved $2,400 refund to Customer A’s disputed transaction, preserve the original invoice and decision record, do not modify unrelated account balances, record the payment-provider result, notify the customer only after payment acceptance and route an ambiguous provider response to Finance without retrying automatically.

### 5.4 Action Fidelity

Action fidelity can fail through:

- wrong scope;
- wrong actor;
- stale state;
- missing precondition;
- partial execution;
- unauthorised adaptation;
- incorrect sequence;
- and hidden external effect.

### 5.5 Literal Compliance Can Reduce Fidelity

A procedure may assume:

- the source system is available;
- the customer has one account;
- or the provider response is immediate.

Rigidly following the procedure after those assumptions fail may violate the original decision.

### Project OI Principle

> **Fidelity is adherence to authorised purpose and constraints, not mindless reproduction of the expected action sequence.**

## 6. Execution as a Control Process

Project OI proposes execution as a governed control process.

```text
Operational intent
        ↓
Establish readiness and initial state
        ↓
Authorise and perform action
        ↓
Observe resulting state
        ↓
Compare with expected and acceptable state
        ↓
Continue, adjust, pause, contain or terminate
        ↓
Determine completion or recovery state
```

### 6.1 Feedforward Controls

Before execution:

- validate inputs;
- confirm authority;
- confirm current state;
- reserve resources;
- check dependencies;
- test constraints;
- and verify recovery.

### 6.2 Concurrent Controls

During execution:

- monitor state;
- enforce limits;
- preserve ordering;
- detect anomalies;
- and support intervention.

### 6.3 Feedback Controls

After each material step:

- compare actual and expected state;
- evaluate whether assumptions remain valid;
- and adjust within authority.

### 6.4 Constraint-Based Control

Not every path needs to be specified.

The organisation may define:

- states that must not occur;
- effects that are prohibited;
- limits;
- and escalation conditions.

Leveson’s systems-theoretic safety model treats accidents as failures of control and enforcement of safety constraints across a sociotechnical system rather than as simple chains of component failure.[^10]

### 6.5 Control Is Not One Dashboard

A dashboard can show selected signals while:

- the underlying state is stale;
- the wrong boundary is monitored;
- or the operator lacks authority to intervene.

Control requires:

- observable state;
- meaningful thresholds;
- permitted action;
- and a response path.

## 7. Theoretical Foundations

### 7.1 Organisational Control

Ouchi describes market, bureaucratic and clan mechanisms for organisational control, each relying on different informational and social conditions.[^1]

Execution may be controlled through:

- measurable outputs;
- specified behaviour;
- shared values and professional norms;
- or a combination.

The appropriate control depends on whether:

- outputs can be measured;
- behaviour can be specified;
- and goals are sufficiently aligned.

### 7.2 Enabling and Coercive Formalisation

Adler and Borys distinguish formalisation that helps employees master their work from formalisation designed primarily to enforce compliance.[^2]

An enabling procedure helps the actor:

- understand the process;
- recognise abnormal conditions;
- repair breakdown;
- and improve performance.

A coercive procedure:

- hides purpose;
- treats deviation as disobedience;
- and limits visibility to management control.

Operational reliability requires standardisation.

The design of that standardisation affects whether the organisation gains:

- reliable performance;
- or brittle compliance.

### 7.3 Routine Dynamics

Feldman and Pentland distinguish the ostensive aspect of a routine, the abstract idea of the routine, from the performative aspect, the actions through which people enact it in specific situations.[^3]

This explains why:

- one procedure can produce several performance patterns;
- local variation is inevitable;
- and execution can modify the routine over time.

Operational reliability cannot be understood by reading the procedure alone.

### 7.4 Dependability Taxonomy

Avizienis and colleagues distinguish:

- faults;
- errors;
- failures;
- and dependability attributes

such as reliability, availability, safety, integrity and maintainability.[^4]

The taxonomy is technical.

It provides useful discipline for sociotechnical analysis where organisations often use “failure” to describe:

- cause;
- symptom;
- error;
- outage;
- and consequence

interchangeably.

### 7.5 High-Reliability Organisations

Roberts examines organisations that operate hazardous technologies with unusually strong safety records.[^5]

Weick, Sutcliffe and Obstfeld describe high reliability through processes of collective mindfulness, commonly associated with:

- preoccupation with failure;
- reluctance to simplify;
- sensitivity to operations;
- commitment to resilience;
- and deference to expertise.[^6]

High reliability is not the absence of variation.

It is an organisational capacity to detect, interpret and respond before variation becomes catastrophe.

### 7.6 Normal Accidents and Tight Coupling

Perrow argues that systems combining interactive complexity with tight coupling are vulnerable to failures that interact in unexpected and rapidly propagating ways.[^7]

The implication is not that reliability work is futile.

It is that:

- complexity;
- coupling;
- slack;
- isolation;
- and recoverability

are design choices, not merely operator concerns.

### 7.7 Person and System Models of Error

Reason contrasts a person approach, focused on individual error and blame, with a system approach focused on the conditions that make errors likely and defences that prevent or mitigate consequence.[^8]

A system approach does not remove personal responsibility.

It prevents the organisation from treating retraining or discipline as a complete reliability strategy.

### 7.8 Dynamic Risk and Boundary Migration

Rasmussen models risk management as a control problem across:

- regulators;
- managers;
- planners;
- and operators.[^9]

Under pressure for:

- efficiency;
- workload;
- and performance,

work can migrate toward the boundaries of acceptable operation.

Failure may emerge from normal adaptations made at several levels rather than one obvious violation.

### 7.9 Systems-Theoretic Safety

Leveson’s STAMP model treats safety as an emergent system property maintained through control constraints across technical and organisational levels.[^10]

This is especially relevant to:

- software-intensive systems;
- autonomous systems;
- and organisations where several correct local actions can combine into an unsafe outcome.

### 7.10 Resilience Engineering

Woods distinguishes several meanings of resilience and highlights **graceful extensibility**, the ability to bring additional adaptive capacity to bear when surprise challenges ordinary boundaries.[^11]

A system that merely returns to the prior state may restore a condition that was already fragile.

### 7.11 Error Management Culture

Van Dyck and colleagues describe error-management culture through norms and practices that support:

- communication;
- detection;
- analysis;
- correction;
- and reduction of negative consequences.[^12]

The evidence associates stronger error-management culture with organisational performance.

### 7.12 Frontline Workarounds

Tucker’s study of hospital nurses finds that operational failures often arose from breakdowns in the supply of materials and information across organisational boundaries, while frontline staff quickly compensated for many of them.[^13]

The repair restored immediate work.

It also made recurring failures less visible to actors able to change the source process.

Tucker and Edmondson describe an illusory equilibrium in which frontline problem solving keeps the system functioning while preventing structural learning.[^14]

## 8. Procedures, Routines and Enabling Formalisation

Operational execution needs repeatability without pretending that every situation is identical.

### 8.1 Procedures Are Models of Expected Work

A procedure expresses:

- expected sequence;
- responsibilities;
- controls;
- and normal exceptions.

It is not the work itself.

### 8.2 Routines Are Performed

The same routine can be enacted differently because of:

- context;
- skill;
- resource;
- customer;
- workload;
- and local judgement.

### 8.3 Enabling Procedure Design

An enabling procedure should help the actor answer:

- Why does this step exist?
- Which condition does it protect?
- What counts as normal?
- What should I observe?
- Which variation is permitted?
- When must I stop?
- How can I recover?
- Who can resolve an exception?
- How should I report a mismatch?

### 8.4 Coercive Procedure Design

A coercive procedure emphasises:

- compliance;
- inspection;
- blame;
- and concealment of local variation.

Actors may then:

- follow the letter while violating the purpose;
- create hidden workarounds;
- or avoid reporting exceptions.

### 8.5 Standardisation and Expertise

A standard should reduce routine coordination and cognitive load.

It should not suppress the expertise needed to recognise that the current situation falls outside the standard.

### 8.6 Procedure Drift

A procedure drifts where:

- work changes;
- local repair accumulates;
- and the formal model remains static.

### 8.7 Completion Rule

Every routine or workflow should distinguish:

- local task complete;
- dependency satisfied;
- operational state changed;
- and evidence captured.

## 9. Operational Readiness

Execution should not begin merely because a task exists or a schedule has arrived.

### 9.1 Readiness Dimensions

#### Authority

- Does the actor or system hold the required action authority?
- Is the delegation current?

#### Intent

- Is the authorised change clear?
- Are protected constraints visible?

#### State

- Is the current state known?
- Has it changed since the decision?

#### Preconditions

- Are required prior actions complete?
- Are inputs valid?

#### Resources

- Are time, people, capacity, data, tools and funds available?

#### Capability

- Can the actor or system perform the work?

#### Dependency Readiness

- Are producing and receiving actors ready?

#### Observability

- Can execution and failure be observed?

#### Recovery

- Are containment, rollback, compensation and escalation available?

#### Safety and Security

- Are relevant hazards, permissions and data boundaries controlled?

### 9.2 Readiness Is Temporal

A readiness check can expire.

A state validated at planning time may be stale at execution time.

### 9.3 Readiness Overhead

Not every action needs the same preflight.

The readiness burden should be proportionate to:

- consequence;
- novelty;
- reversibility;
- coupling;
- and uncertainty.

### Project OI Principle

> **Readiness is evidence that the operational system can begin responsibly, not a ceremonial approval state.**

## 10. Operational State, Observability and Completion

### 10.1 State Must Be Represented

An organisation cannot control what it cannot identify adequately.

Relevant state may include:

- current step;
- committed and completed actions;
- resource use;
- external acknowledgements;
- pending approvals;
- exceptions;
- and recovery status.

### 10.2 Observability Requires Meaning

Logs, messages and status fields are not useful merely because they exist.

The organisation needs to know:

- what happened;
- whether it succeeded;
- what remains uncertain;
- and what may happen next.

### 10.3 Ambiguous External Effect

An external action may time out after the remote system performed it.

The local system cannot assume:

- success;
- or failure.

The correct state may be:

> **Outcome unknown, reconciliation required.**

### 10.4 Silent Success Can Be a Failure

A payment call can return success while:

- the wrong account was charged;
- or the customer obligation remains unresolved.

### 10.5 Completion Integrity

Project OI uses **completion integrity** as a working term for the degree to which a completion state accurately represents:

- work performed;
- required effects;
- unresolved ambiguity;
- evidence;
- and receiving acceptance.

### 10.6 Terminal States Should Be Specific

Avoid one generic “done”.

Use meaningful states such as:

- completed and accepted;
- completed with degraded service;
- failed before effect;
- partially completed;
- compensated;
- cancelled;
- safe termination;
- and indeterminate.

### 10.7 Operational Trace

Material execution should preserve enough evidence to reconstruct:

```text
Intent
Initial state
Actor and authority
Inputs
Actions
Tool or system results
State transitions
Exceptions
Human interventions
Recovery
Terminal state
```

## 11. Reliability, Availability and Dependability

### 11.1 Reliability Requires a Service Definition

The organisation must state:

- what service;
- for whom;
- under which conditions;
- for how long;
- and within which tolerance.

### 11.2 Local Reliability Versus System Reliability

A component can be reliable while the end-to-end service is not.

Examples:

- the billing system records correctly;
- the notification system fails;
- and the customer remains unaware.

### 11.3 Availability Versus Correctness

Availability measures whether service can be provided.

It does not prove that service is:

- correct;
- safe;
- or appropriate.

### 11.4 Reliability and Change

Change can improve capability and introduce instability.

A reliability system needs an explicit mechanism for balancing:

- feature or policy change;
- and operational stability.

### 11.5 Reliability Targets

Targets can help define acceptable service.

They can also produce:

- metric gaming;
- ignored minority harm;
- or optimisation around what is easy to measure.

Detailed outcome and measurement design belongs to Chapter 7.

### 11.6 Reliability as Repeated Organisational Capability

Operational reliability should be assessed across:

- people;
- workflows;
- systems;
- partners;
- and recovery,

not only the technical component.

## 12. Robustness, Resilience and Graceful Degradation

### 12.1 Robustness

Robustness absorbs defined variation.

Examples:

- capacity margin;
- validation;
- redundancy;
- and tolerance.

### 12.2 Resilience as Rebound

The system returns to prior operation after disruption.

### 12.3 Resilience as Graceful Extensibility

The system brings additional capability into operation when ordinary capacity or assumptions are exceeded.[^11]

Examples:

- emergency roles;
- manual intervention;
- alternate supplier;
- degraded mode;
- or temporary resource reallocation.

### 12.4 Sustained Adaptability

Longer-term adaptation belongs to Chapter 9.

Execution should preserve the evidence required for that adaptation.

### 12.5 Graceful Degradation

A service may prioritise:

1. safety;
2. integrity;
3. critical users or functions;
4. reduced service;
5. convenience.

### 12.6 Degradation Must Be Visible

A system in degraded mode should not appear fully healthy.

### 12.7 Brittleness

A brittle system performs efficiently within a narrow range and fails sharply beyond it.

### 12.8 Slack and Efficiency

Eliminating all slack may improve average efficiency while reducing:

- recovery;
- adaptation;
- and error tolerance.

## 13. Safety and System Constraints

### 13.1 Safety Is a System Property

Safety cannot be guaranteed by requiring each component or person to “be safe”.

Interactions matter.

### 13.2 Safety Constraint

A safety constraint states what must not occur or what must be preserved.

Example:

> No refund may alter unrelated customer balances.

### 13.3 Reliability Can Conflict With Safety

A system may continue service by bypassing a protective check.

Reliability of output increases while safety decreases.

### 13.4 Fail-Safe and Fail-Secure

A failure response may prioritise:

- prevention of harm;
- or prevention of unauthorised access.

The correct state depends on context.

### 13.5 Control Structure

Leveson and Rasmussen both emphasise cross-level control structures, including:

- policy;
- management;
- planning;
- technology;
- and operations.[^9][^10]

### 13.6 Boundary Pressure

Work can migrate toward unsafe boundaries when:

- efficiency pressure;
- workload;
- and local incentives

are stronger than safety feedback.

### 13.7 Safety Evidence

Safety-critical execution should preserve:

- hazard;
- constraint;
- control;
- deviation;
- and intervention

as operational evidence.

## 14. Faults, Errors, Failures, Incidents and Near Misses

### 14.1 Why the Distinction Matters

If every unexpected event is called an error:

- cause and symptom are confused;
- accountability becomes personal;
- and repair targets the wrong layer.

### 14.2 Fault–Error–Failure Relationship

```text
Fault
        ↓ activates
Error state
        ↓ propagates
Failure at a service boundary
        ↓ may produce
Incident, harm or wider consequence
```

The chain is not always linear.

### 14.3 Multiple Faults

Failures may emerge from several individually tolerable faults interacting.

### 14.4 Latent Conditions

Organisational conditions can remain dormant until:

- workload;
- change;
- or unusual combination

activates them.

### 14.5 Incident

Incident management should focus first on:

- containment;
- stabilisation;
- and restoration.

Causal learning belongs later.

### 14.6 Near Miss

Near misses may reveal:

- successful defence;
- adaptive capacity;
- or luck.

They should not be treated as failures only when visible harm occurs.

### 14.7 Error Reporting

An organisation that punishes every error will reduce the evidence available for reliability.

An organisation that treats every violation as blameless will weaken accountability.

### Project OI Principle

> **Classify the operational condition before assigning cause, responsibility or remedy.**

## 15. Exceptions, Deviations, Workarounds and Drift

### 15.1 Expected Exception

An expected exception has:

- a recognised route;
- authority;
- and resolution behaviour.

### 15.2 Unexpected Exception

An unexpected exception requires:

- diagnosis;
- containment;
- and possibly escalation.

### 15.3 Deviation as Information

A deviation may indicate:

- local expertise;
- stale procedure;
- resource shortage;
- or unsafe behaviour.

### 15.4 Workarounds Restore Function

Frontline workarounds are often rational responses to operational failure.[^13]

They can protect:

- customers;
- patients;
- and deadlines.

### 15.5 Workarounds Can Hide the System

When repair is fast and local:

- the failure disappears from management view;
- the upstream process remains unchanged;
- and the employee absorbs the coordination and operational cost.[^13][^14]

### 15.6 Normalisation of Deviation

Repeated deviation can become ordinary practice without:

- approval;
- evidence;
- or policy change.

### 15.7 Workaround Register

For material recurring workarounds, capture:

```text
Trigger
Local repair
Immediate benefit
Risk
Source condition
Frequency
Owner
Permanent resolution
```

### 15.8 Drift

Operational drift occurs when actual work gradually moves away from:

- procedure;
- authority;
- or safety boundary

without one deliberate decision.

### 15.9 Repair Is Not Learning

Repair restores the episode.

Learning and capability change are owned by Chapters 8 and 9.

## 16. Failure Containment and Incident Response

### 16.1 Contain Before Optimising

During material failure, priorities may be:

1. protect people and critical assets;
2. prevent spread;
3. stabilise state;
4. preserve evidence;
5. restore essential service;
6. and communicate.

### 16.2 Incident Authority

An incident should identify:

- incident owner;
- operational authority;
- communication responsibility;
- domain expertise;
- and decision escalation.

### 16.3 Shared Operational State

Incident response needs one sufficiently current view of:

- scope;
- impact;
- action;
- ownership;
- and next decision.

### 16.4 Parallel Work

Containment, diagnosis and recovery may proceed in parallel.

Dependencies and integration must remain visible.

### 16.5 Automation During Incident

Automation can:

- isolate;
- fail over;
- restart;
- or shed load.

It can also amplify failure.

### 16.6 Cascading Failure

Tight coupling, overload and automated feedback can turn local failure into systemic failure.

Google’s SRE guidance describes retries, load redistribution and resource saturation as potential amplifiers of cascading failure, and recommends bounded retries, backoff, load shedding and graceful degradation.[^27]

This is an industry practice source, not a universal organisational law.

### 16.7 Evidence Preservation

Emergency response should preserve enough operational evidence for later evaluation.

It should not delay containment to create a perfect record.

## 17. Recovery, Rollback, Compensation and Continuity

### 17.1 Recovery Strategy Must Be Designed Before Failure

A backup does not prove recovery.

A rollback command does not prove reversibility.

### 17.2 Restore

Restore recreates acceptable state from:

- backup;
- replica;
- checkpoint;
- or source evidence.

### 17.3 Rollback

Rollback attempts to return to a prior state.

It can fail where:

- external effects occurred;
- data changed elsewhere;
- or the prior state is incompatible.

### 17.4 Compensation

Compensation performs a new organisational action to address a prior effect.

Examples:

- refund a payment;
- send a correction;
- revoke access;
- or create an offsetting entry.

Compensation does not erase history.

### 17.5 Retry

Retries should be:

- bounded;
- classified;
- delayed appropriately;
- and protected against duplication.

### 17.6 Reconciliation

Reconciliation determines actual state where local and external records disagree or the result is unknown.

### 17.7 Checkpoint and Resume

Long-running work should preserve:

- completed steps;
- exact versions;
- pending work;
- and current authority.

### 17.8 Continuity

Continuity is the ability to preserve critical service during disruption.

It may use:

- alternate people;
- systems;
- suppliers;
- sites;
- or degraded modes.

### 17.9 Recovery Time and Recovery Integrity

Fast recovery is not sufficient if:

- data is wrong;
- effects are duplicated;
- or safety constraints are violated.

### 17.10 Recovery Exercise

Recovery should be tested under controlled conditions.

Chaos engineering uses deliberate experiments to test resilience assumptions in distributed systems.[^20]

Empirical work on production exception-handling fault injection shows that such experiments can reveal weaknesses in resilience code that normal operation does not expose.[^21]

## 18. High Reliability and Collective Mindfulness

High reliability does not mean the organisation never fails.

It describes persistent organising for conditions in which failure is consequential and absence of recent failure can produce complacency.

### 18.1 Preoccupation With Failure

Treat:

- small anomalies;
- near misses;
- and weak signs

as potential evidence.

### 18.2 Reluctance to Simplify

Avoid:

- one root cause;
- one blame target;
- or one convenient narrative

where system interaction is complex.

### 18.3 Sensitivity to Operations

Maintain awareness of:

- real work;
- current conditions;
- resource pressure;
- and frontline adaptation.

### 18.4 Commitment to Resilience

Build:

- recovery;
- improvisational capacity;
- and redundancy

before crisis.

### 18.5 Deference to Expertise

Move operational authority toward relevant expertise when the situation requires it, within governed boundaries.

### 18.6 Reliability Paradox

The better the safety record, the less direct failure experience the organisation possesses.

Reliability therefore requires:

- imagination;
- simulation;
- near-miss attention;
- and controlled testing.

## 19. Human Operation and Intervention

People do not merely create operational variability.

They also detect, interpret and repair conditions the system was not designed to handle.

### 19.1 Situation Awareness

Endsley defines situation awareness through:

- perception of elements;
- comprehension of meaning;
- and projection of future status.[^15]

Operational intervention depends on whether the person understands:

- what is happening;
- why;
- and what may happen next.

### 19.2 Workload

High workload can:

- narrow attention;
- delay detection;
- and degrade coordination.

Low workload under automation can reduce engagement and readiness.

### 19.3 Expertise

Human intervention should defer to relevant operational expertise rather than hierarchy alone.

### 19.4 Manual Takeover

A manual fallback is meaningful only where the person:

- can observe state;
- remembers the task;
- has access;
- and has practised the transition.

### 19.5 Intervention Authority

The operator should know whether they may:

- pause;
- cancel;
- reverse;
- override;
- or escalate.

### 19.6 Human Error

A system should distinguish:

- slip;
- mistake;
- violation;
- and adaptation

before selecting a response.

### 19.7 Error Management

Van Dyck and colleagues emphasise:

- rapid communication;
- detection;
- analysis;
- and correction.[^12]

The objective is not to celebrate error.

It is to reduce its negative consequences and increase useful information.

## 20. Automation and Human Factors

### 20.1 Automation Changes the Human Role

Bainbridge’s “ironies of automation” observes that automation can leave people responsible for monitoring and recovery precisely when routine engagement has been reduced.[^16]

The more reliable the automation appears, the less practice the operator may have when it fails.

### 20.2 Types and Levels of Automation

Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens distinguish automation of:

1. information acquisition;
2. information analysis;
3. decision and action selection;
4. and action implementation,

across different levels of automation.[^17]

Automation design should therefore ask:

- what function is automated;
- to what level;
- and with what human consequence.

### 20.3 Appropriate Reliance

Lee and See argue for designing trust in automation around appropriate reliance rather than maximum trust.[^18]

Reliance should reflect:

- capability;
- context;
- predictability;
- and consequence.

### 20.4 Automation Surprise

Automation surprise occurs when:

- mode;
- state;
- or action

does not match the operator’s mental model.

### 20.5 Out-of-the-Loop Performance

A person may remain nominally responsible while lacking:

- state awareness;
- skill;
- or time

to intervene effectively.

### 20.6 Oversight Load

Human review of every automated action can produce:

- delay;
- fatigue;
- and rubber stamping.

### 20.7 Adaptive Allocation

Human and machine roles may change with:

- risk;
- uncertainty;
- workload;
- and system condition.

Role change must remain visible.

## 21. AI and AI agent Execution

AI changes execution because a model may:

- interpret goals;
- plan;
- select tools;
- generate parameters;
- adapt its route;
- and perform external actions.

This makes the model part of an operational system.

### 21.1 Model Capability Is Not Execution Authority

An AI agent’s ability to call a tool does not establish the organisational right to use it.

### 21.2 AI agent Identity

Every consequential AI agent action should be attributable to:

- a stable AI agent identity;
- acting principal;
- delegated authority;
- session or run;
- model and relevant configuration;
- and tool invocation.

NIST’s 2026 AI Agent Standards Initiative explicitly identifies secure operation, interoperability, authentication and identity infrastructure as standards priorities.[^23]

The NIST NCCoE concept paper similarly focuses on:

- identification;
- authorisation;
- auditing;
- non-repudiation;
- and prompt-injection mitigation

for software and AI agents.[^24]

### 21.3 Execution Envelope

Project OI uses **AI agent execution envelope** as a working term for the bounded conditions under which an AI agent may operate.

It should identify:

```text
Objective
Acting principal
AI agent identity
Permitted tools
Permitted data
Authority scope
Budget
Time
State
Required evidence
Prohibited actions
Approval conditions
Stop conditions
Recovery
```

This is a BOK concept, not a proposed product primitive.

### 21.4 Tool Use

Tool execution should enforce:

- typed operation;
- parameter validation;
- permission;
- rate and budget;
- destination;
- and expected effect.

### 21.5 Untrusted Input

Retrieved documents, email, web content and tool output may contain instructions intended to alter AI agent behaviour.

Operational systems should distinguish:

- governing instruction;
- user intent;
- source content;
- and untrusted data.

### 21.6 Long-Horizon Execution

Long-running AI agent work needs:

- checkpoints;
- explicit state;
- task decomposition;
- verification;
- and bounded continuation.

Anthropic’s 2026 harness work reports that structured handoffs, context resets and a separate evaluator improved long-running coding performance, while adding orchestration complexity, latency and cost.[^26]

This is vendor engineering evidence from one implementation, not a general theorem.

### 21.7 Self-Evaluation Limits

An AI agent should not be the sole evaluator of its own work where:

- consequence;
- subjectivity;
- or hidden failure

is material.

### 21.8 AI agent Security

NIST’s 2026 analysis of responses on AI agent security reports broad agreement among respondents that AI agent security creates novel threats requiring adaptation of established cybersecurity practices.[^25]

The report is a synthesis of consultation responses, not an empirical estimate of incident prevalence.

### 21.9 AI agent Execution Evidence

Preserve:

- prompt or instruction reference;
- context sources;
- tool calls;
- results;
- generated artefacts;
- verification;
- human intervention;
- and final state.

Private chain-of-thought is not required.

### 21.10 Kill, Pause and Revoke

The organisation should be able to:

- pause;
- cancel;
- revoke tools;
- expire authority;
- and contain external effects.

### 21.11 Provider and Model Change

A model or provider change can alter:

- planning;
- tool use;
- and error mode.

It should not be treated as a transparent implementation detail for consequential execution.

Multiple AI agents may coordinate planning, generation, review and action.

They create additional operational dependencies.

### 22.1 Required Conditions

- one overall objective;
- role boundaries;
- shared and private state;
- dependency graph;
- tool authority;
- output contracts;
- integration owner;
- and termination conditions.

### 22.2 Failure Amplification

AI agents can:

- duplicate actions;
- retry one another’s failures;
- overwrite state;
- disagree on completion;
- or form circular delegation.

### 22.3 Shared Resource Control

Budgets, tools, credentials and rate limits must be coordinated.

### 22.4 Independent Verification

Separate evaluators may improve detection.

They can also share the same model blind spot.

### 22.5 Accountability

The organisation remains accountable for:

- role design;
- model selection;
- permissions;
- and effects.

### Project OI Guardrail

> **AI agent count is not operational capacity. Reliable multi-AI agent execution depends on bounded authority, shared state, verification and one accountable integration path.**

- prepare;

- execute;
- approve;

Emergency or exception authority may expand temporarily.

It should:

- be explicit;
- expire;

- actor;

- initiation;

## 24. Execution and Operational Reliability Failure Modes

### 24.1 Execution Ambiguity

The organisation cannot state what work should occur.

### 24.2 Action Without Authority

A person or system performs a consequential action without legitimate permission.

### 24.3 Authority Without Readiness

The actor may act but lacks context, capability, resources or recovery.

### 24.4 Decision–Execution Loss

Material conditions from the decision disappear before operation.

### 24.5 Coordination–Execution Gap

Dependencies are planned but do not operate under real conditions.

### 24.6 Task Completion Substitution

A task is marked complete while the operational obligation remains unsatisfied.

### 24.7 Output–Outcome Confusion

An immediate output is treated as proof of desired consequence.

### 24.8 Stale Operational Intent

Execution uses a decision or condition that has changed.

### 24.9 Scope Drift

Work expands beyond authorised boundaries.

### 24.10 Version Divergence

Actors execute different versions of:

- plan;
- rule;
- model;
- or artefact.

### 24.11 State Blindness

The organisation acts without an accurate view of current state.

### 24.12 Observability Theatre

Many metrics exist, but material state or failure remains unknowable.

### 24.13 Hidden Side Effect

Execution changes a system, person or obligation outside the represented scope.

### 24.14 Partial Completion

Some effects occur while the operation is presented as wholly failed or complete.

### 24.15 Indeterminate Effect

The organisation cannot determine whether the action occurred.

### 24.16 Duplicate Execution

A retry, race or repeated handoff performs the effect more than once.

### 24.17 Orphaned Execution

Work continues after the initiating decision, user or authority has ended.

### 24.18 Zombie workflow

A run remains active without valid progress, ownership or termination.

### 24.19 Silent Failure

The service fails without a usable signal.

### 24.20 False Success

The system reports completion while the intended state was not achieved.

### 24.21 Acceptance Failure

The producer completes work the receiver cannot use.

### 24.22 Procedure Literalism

Actors follow the sequence after its assumptions fail.

### 24.23 Coercive Formalisation

Controls suppress local knowledge and exception reporting.

### 24.24 Procedure Drift

Actual work changes while the formal routine remains static.

### 24.25 Workaround Concealment

Local repair hides recurring operational failure.

### 24.26 Exception Normalisation

A deviation becomes ordinary practice without authorisation.

### 24.27 Boundary Migration

Efficiency or workload pressure moves operation toward unsafe limits.

### 24.28 Local Reliability

A component performs correctly while the end-to-end service fails.

### 24.29 Availability Substitution

The system is reachable but incorrect, unsafe or unusable.

### 24.30 Reliability–Safety Conflict

Continuity is preserved by bypassing a safety control.

### 24.31 Tight-Coupling Cascade

A local failure propagates too quickly for isolation or repair.

### 24.32 Resource Saturation

Capacity exhaustion creates delay, error and secondary failure.

### 24.33 Hidden Queue

Work waits in an actor, system or approval path not represented in state.

### 24.34 Retry Storm

Automated retries amplify load or duplicate effects.

### 24.35 Failover Oscillation

Systems repeatedly shift load or authority without reaching stable operation.

### 24.36 Redundancy Illusion

Backup capability shares the same hidden dependency as the primary path.

### 24.37 Degradation Invisibility

Reduced service appears normal.

### 24.38 Recovery Fantasy

Rollback, restore or failover exists only as an untested plan.

### 24.39 Rollback Incompatibility

The prior state cannot coexist with intervening data or external effect.

### 24.40 Compensation Mismatch

The compensating action does not address the actual consequence.

### 24.41 Backup Without Restore

Data is copied, but usable recovery is not proven.

### 24.42 Incident Ownership Ambiguity

No actor can contain or coordinate response.

### 24.43 Evidence Destruction

Recovery action removes the evidence needed to understand the incident.

### 24.44 Blame Substitution

The organisation identifies one person instead of examining system conditions.

### 24.45 Error Suppression

People hide errors because reporting is unsafe or burdensome.

### 24.46 Near-Miss Dismissal

Absence of harm is treated as proof of control.

### 24.47 Alarm Flood

Too many signals reduce operator ability to detect the material condition.

### 24.48 Manual Fallback Illusion

Humans are nominally available but cannot take over effectively.

### 24.49 Automation Surprise

The system acts in a mode or sequence the operator does not understand.

### 24.50 Out-of-the-Loop Failure

The operator lacks state awareness or practised skill when intervention is required.

### 24.51 Oversight Congestion

Human review becomes the execution bottleneck.

### 24.52 Override Without Trace

A human changes automated behaviour without preserving reason or effect.

### 24.53 AI Role Ambiguity

Actors cannot tell whether AI is:

- advising;
- planning;
- acting;
- or waiting.

### 24.54 AI agent Authority Leakage

An AI agent gains access through a service account or tool beyond its delegated scope.

### 24.55 AI agent Hijacking

Untrusted data alters AI agent behaviour or tool use.

### 24.56 AI agent Context Drift

The AI agent’s working state diverges from authoritative organisational state.

### 24.57 Long-Horizon Coherence Loss

The AI agent loses objective, constraint or progress across extended work.

### 24.58 Self-Approval

The same AI agent generates, evaluates and accepts work without independent control.

### 24.59 Model-Change Drift

A provider or model change alters execution behaviour without governance.

### 24.60 Multi-agent Duplication

Several AI agents perform or repeat the same action.

### 24.61 Multi-agent Goal Conflict

AI agents optimise incompatible local objectives.

### 24.62 Tool Contention

Automated actors compete for the same resource, budget or credential.

### 24.63 Circular Delegation

AI agents or teams pass unresolved work among one another.

### 24.64 Kill-Switch Failure

The organisation cannot pause or revoke execution.

### 24.65 Operational Evidence Gap

The organisation cannot reconstruct what occurred.

### 24.66 Completion Without Evaluation Handoff

Execution terminates without transferring evidence into outcome review.

### 24.67 Repair Without Structural Resolution

The same operational failure recurs because local repair concealed the source.

## 26. The Execution and Operational Reliability Process

Project OI proposes the following working process.

```text
Receive coordinated commitment
        ↓
Define operational intent and protected constraints
        ↓
Establish readiness and current state
        ↓
Select execution mode and control structure
        ↓
Authorise actors, tools and resources
        ↓
Perform and observe work
        ↓
Compare actual with expected and acceptable state
        ↓
Continue, adjust, pause, contain or terminate
        ↓
Handle exception, failure and ambiguity
        ↓
Recover, roll back, compensate or degrade
        ↓
Determine completion and acceptance
        ↓
Preserve operational evidence
        ↓
Transfer output and trace to outcome evaluation
```

### 26.1 Receive

Confirm:

- decision;
- scope;
- authority;
- and coordinated commitments.

### 26.2 Define Intent

State:

- desired change;
- constraints;
- and acceptable terminal states.

### 26.3 Establish Readiness

Validate:

- preconditions;
- resources;

## 27. Proportionate Execution Modes

### 27.1 Human-Performed Work

Use where:

- judgement;
- physical skill;
- or relationship

dominates.

Controls:

- role;
- guidance;
- peer support;
- and evidence.

### 27.2 Procedure-Assisted Human Work

Use where:

- work is repeatable;
- variation exists;
- and human recognition remains important.

Controls:

- enabling procedure;
- checklist;
- exception route;
- and stop authority.

### 27.3 Deterministic Automation

Use where:

- rules are stable;
- inputs are validatable;
- and effects are bounded.

Controls:

- typed inputs;
- idempotency;
- observability;
- and recovery.

### 27.4 Human-Supervised AI Execution

Use where AI can:

- plan;
- draft;
- classify;
- or perform bounded tool use,

while a human retains active review and intervention.

Controls:

- state visibility;
- evidence;
- authority;
- and meaningful pause.

### 27.5 Bounded Autonomous AI agent Execution

Use only where:

- objective and authority are explicit;
- tools are constrained;
- state is observable;
- recovery exists;
- and consequence is proportionate.

### 27.6 Multi-agent Execution

Use where specialisation creates value exceeding coordination cost.

Controls:

- roles;
- dependencies;
- integration owner;
- and termination.

### 27.7 Emergency Execution

Use:

- temporary authority;
- containment;
- degraded service;
- and later review.

### Mode Principle

> **Select execution mode according to operational conditions, consequence and recoverability, not according to the maximum capability of the available technology.**

## 28. SMB Context

SMBs often achieve reliability through:

- experienced people;
- direct communication;
- flexible roles;
- and rapid repair.

This can be effective.

It can also conceal dependency and weak recovery.

### Common Strengths

- short intervention paths;
- high local knowledge;
- direct owner authority;
- and rapid adaptation.

### Common Weaknesses

- one-person operational dependency;
- undocumented workarounds;
- no tested restore;
- broad system permissions;
- hidden manual steps;
- and ambiguous completion.

### Lightweight SMB Operational Model

For material recurring work, define:

```text
Purpose
Owner
Authority
Inputs
Current state
Expected result
Critical constraint
Exception route
Recovery
Evidence
Completion
```

### Minimum Reliability Practices

- test backup restoration;
- preserve current operating procedures;
- separate routine and emergency authority;
- use explicit external-effect confirmation;
- limit automated retries;
- record recurring workarounds;
- maintain one visible incident route;
- and make degraded operation visible.

### AI and AI agent Use

For an SMB AI agent:

- use a dedicated identity;
- grant narrow tools;
- preserve approval for high-impact effects;
- define cost and time limits;
- and ensure the owner can stop the run.

### Founder Guardrail

The objective is not to remove founder judgement.

It is to prevent the founder from becoming the only:

- monitor;
- exception handler;
- recovery mechanism;
- and source of operational state.

## 29. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Execution Is Distinct From Decision and Outcome

> An authorised decision contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where it becomes controlled execution, and execution should not be treated as proof of outcome.

### P2 — Operational Intent Enables Fidelity

> Execution reliability improves where purpose, protected constraints, expected state change and failure behaviour are explicit.

### P3 — Reliability Is Conditional

> Reliability can be evaluated only relative to a defined service, boundary, condition and period.

### P4 — Control Should Be Enabling

> Formalisation will support reliability more effectively where it helps actors understand, detect and repair work rather than merely enforce compliance.

### P5 — Routines Are Performed, Not Merely Specified

> Variation between documented and actual work is an inherent feature of organisational routines and a potential source of both resilience and risk.

### P6 — Readiness Is a Material State

> A schedule or approval should not substitute for evidence that authority, prerequisites, capacity, observability and recovery are sufficient.

### P7 — Observability Is Required for Control

> An organisation cannot govern material execution reliably where current state, deviation and effect cannot be inferred.

### P8 — Completion Requires Integrity

> Terminal status should represent actual effect, unresolved ambiguity and receiving acceptance rather than local task cessation.

### P9 — Reliability and Safety Differ

> Reliable performance of an unsafe or illegitimate function does not constitute responsible Organisational Intelligence.

### P10 — Complexity and Tight Coupling Increase Systemic Risk

> Interactively complex and tightly coupled execution will be more vulnerable to rapid and unanticipated failure propagation.

### P11 — Robustness and Resilience Differ

> Tolerance of expected variation should not be confused with the capacity to recover or extend performance under surprise.

### P12 — Slack Supports Resilience

> Elimination of all spare capacity can improve average efficiency while increasing brittleness and recovery risk.

### P13 — Errors Are System Evidence

> Error analysis should examine the conditions and defences of the operational system rather than defaulting to individual blame.

### P14 — Workarounds Can Conceal Operational Debt

> Frontline repair can preserve immediate performance while reducing organisational visibility of recurring failure.

### P15 — Recovery Must Be Designed and Tested

> Backup, rollback and failover claims are weak until restoration and state integrity have been demonstrated under realistic conditions.

### P16 — Compensation Is Not Rollback

> Where external or organisational effects have occurred, recovery may require new accountable actions rather than fictional restoration of the past.

### P17 — Retry Can Amplify Failure

> Repetition of an uncertain or overloaded action will reduce reliability where idempotency, classification, budget and backoff are inadequate.

### P18 — High Reliability Requires Attention to Weak Failure Evidence

> Absence of recent harm should not reduce attention to anomalies, near misses and operational drift.

### P19 — Human Variability Is Both Risk and Resource

> People create error and also supply detection, interpretation and adaptive repair that automated systems may lack.

### P20 — Automation Redistributes Work

> Automation changes rather than eliminates human responsibility, skill, situation awareness and coordination demand.

### P21 — Human Intervention Must Be Operationally Real

> Human oversight is weak where the person lacks current state, expertise, time, authority or an effective control.

### P22 — AI Capability Does Not Confer Action Authority

> AI agent execution rights must originate from explicit organisational identity and delegation.

### P23 — AI agent Reliability Is End-to-End

> Model response quality alone cannot establish reliable execution where planning, tools, state, authority, verification and external effects are involved.

### P24 — Multi-agent Execution Increases Coordination Requirements

> Specialisation among AI agents can improve capability while increasing dependency, integration and failure-amplification risk.

### P25 — Operational Evidence Enables Improvement

> Execution strengthens Organisational Intelligence where material state, intervention, failure and recovery can be transferred into outcome evaluation and learning.

## 31. Practical Principles

### 31.1 Translate Decisions Into Operational Intent

Do not execute an ambiguous commitment.

### 31.2 Define Protected Constraints

State what must not be changed or harmed.

### 31.3 Confirm Readiness at the Point of Action

Planning-time readiness may be stale.

### 31.4 Use Enabling Controls

Help actors understand and repair work.

### 31.5 Represent Current State

Avoid execution from assumptions or private memory.

### 31.6 Make External Effects Explicit

Distinguish request, acceptance and confirmed effect.

### 31.7 Monitor Material Boundaries

Do not equate metric volume with observability.

### 31.8 Distinguish Fault, Error and Failure

Choose remedy at the correct layer.

### 31.9 Treat Near Misses as Evidence

Do not wait for visible harm.

### 31.10 Design Exceptions

Every recurring process needs a route for conditions it cannot handle.

### 31.11 Record Workarounds

Repeated local repair is system evidence.

### 31.12 Preserve Slack Where Failure Is Costly

Efficiency should not remove all recovery capacity.

### 31.13 Bound Retries

Retry only where transient failure and duplicate-effect protection are credible.

### 31.14 Test Recovery

Backups, rollback and failover must work under realistic conditions.

### 31.15 Use Compensation Honestly

Do not pretend irreversible effects disappeared.

### 31.16 Make Degraded Mode Visible

Reduced service should not appear normal.

### 31.17 Preserve Human Situation Awareness

Do not leave people accountable for systems they cannot understand or control.

### 31.18 Match Automation to Function and Risk

Do not automate action selection and implementation merely because acquisition and analysis are reliable.

### 31.19 Give AI agents Explicit Identity and Authority

Tool access is not delegation.

### 31.20 Separate Generation and Verification

Do not rely solely on self-evaluation for consequential AI agent work.

### 31.21 Provide Pause, Revoke and Stop

Operational control must remain effective during execution.

### 31.22 Use One Accountable Integration Path

Especially for multi-AI agent execution.

### 31.23 Set Accurate Terminal States

Avoid generic “done”.

### 31.24 Preserve Operational Evidence

Record what occurred without requiring private mental process.

### 31.25 Hand Off to Outcome Evaluation

Execution is incomplete as an OI conversion if evidence cannot reach the next stage.

## 32. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Execution completes the operational portion of **Mobilise**.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Authorised intent
× coordination quality
× operational control
× reliability and recovery
= executable response potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Decision Without Execution

```text
The organisation commits
→ work never occurs
→ orientation does not change the world
```

### Coordination Without Readiness

```text
Roles and dependencies are clear
→ resources, state or recovery are insufficient
→ execution fails
```

### Execution Without Observability

```text
Work occurs
→ material state and failure are unknown
→ control becomes retrospective guesswork
```

### Reliability Without Safety

```text
The system performs consistently
→ unacceptable harm remains possible
→ performance is not responsible OI
```

### Recovery Without Evidence

```text
Service is restored
→ actual cause and effect remain unknown
→ later evaluation is weak
```

### AI Without Operational Governance

```text
The AI agent can plan and use tools
→ identity, authority, state and verification are weak
→ capability becomes uncontrolled effect
```

### Execution Without Evaluation Handoff

```text
The action terminates
→ evidence and uncertainty are not transferred
→ outcome cannot improve the organisation
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when it can perform legitimate commitments reliably under real conditions, detect and contain deviation, recover without losing integrity or accountability, and preserve enough operational evidence for consequences to improve future response.**

## 33. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises organisational control, routine dynamics, high-reliability research, safety science, resilience engineering, human factors, dependable computing and emerging AI-AI agent practice;
- does not establish a validated organisation-level operational-reliability construct;
- does not prove that the proposed Execution and Operational Reliability Process is complete;
- and does not provide a universal method for selecting controls or execution modes.

### 33.1 Cross-Disciplinary Translation

Terms such as:

- reliability;
- fault;
- error;
- availability;
- and resilience

have precise meanings in engineering and broader meanings in organisation research.

Project OI adapts them carefully but not without risk.

### 33.2 Service Definition

Reliability depends on what counts as acceptable service.

That definition may be:

- contested;
- incomplete;
- or ethically weak.

### 33.3 Level of Analysis

Reliability of:

- a person;
- team;
- workflow;
- software service;
- organisation;
- or ecosystem

cannot be treated as identical.

### 33.4 High-Reliability Settings

Research on aircraft carriers, healthcare, aviation and emergency organisations may not transfer directly to ordinary SMB work.

### 33.5 Normal Accident and HRO Tension

Normal Accident Theory and High Reliability research make different claims about the manageability of hazardous complexity.

The chapter uses both as productive tensions rather than claiming reconciliation.

### 33.6 Safety and Ethics

Safe operation is not the same as ethical purpose.

A system can operate safely and reliably while producing unfair or harmful policy outcomes.

### 33.7 Workarounds

A workaround may be:

- intelligent adaptation;
- hidden policy;
- or unsafe deviation.

Classification requires local evidence.

### 33.8 Measurement

Failure rates, uptime and recovery time may not capture:

- minority harm;
- hidden manual work;
- or degraded quality.

Chapter 7 will address outcome and evaluation limits.

### 33.9 AI-AI agent Evidence

The 2026 AI agent standards and security sources describe emerging priorities, consultations and engineering practice rather than settled empirical conclusions.

### 33.10 Vendor Practice

Anthropic and Google SRE examples are implementation evidence from particular organisations.

They are not universal organisational laws.

### 33.11 Long-Horizon AI agents

AI agent models, harnesses and benchmarks are changing quickly.

Claims about current capability and failure require continual update.

### 33.12 Learning Boundary

This chapter records evidence required for later learning.

It does not establish that the organisation will use that evidence.
