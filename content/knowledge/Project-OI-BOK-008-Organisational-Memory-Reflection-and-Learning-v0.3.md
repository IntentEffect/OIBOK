## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **organisational memory, reflection and learning**: how an organisation preserves, locates, reconstructs and interprets experience so that credible feedback can change future understanding and response.

Its central question is:

> **How does an organisation preserve, reconstruct and reinterpret experience so that consequences can change future understanding and response?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant organisational context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. form a useful orientation;
6. make an attributable and authorised decision;
7. coordinate interdependent collective action;
8. execute authorised work reliably;
9. observe material consequences;
10. and produce credible evaluative feedback.

Feedback does not create learning automatically.

The organisation must still:

- preserve what happened and what was known at the time;
- distinguish source evidence from later interpretation;
- locate relevant prior experience;
- reconstruct episodes without allowing hindsight to rewrite them;
- create enough psychological and procedural safety for inquiry;
- reflect on actions, assumptions, mechanisms and consequences;
- identify what was learned and the limits of that lesson;
- decide what should be retained, revised, superseded, forgotten or investigated further;
- update organisational knowledge, routines, policies, relationships and future decision conditions;
- make the resulting learning available where similar work occurs;
- and test whether the learning persists beyond one person, meeting, report or system.

```text
Evaluative feedback
        ↓
Organisational remembering
        ↓
Reflection and inquiry
        ↓
Lesson formation
        ↓
Memory, knowledge and routine update
        ↓
Future retrieval and application
        ↓
Evidence of learning persistence
        ↓
Capability development and adaptation
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what organisational memory is;
- why memory is not a repository;
- how organisational remembering differs from data retrieval;
- where organisational memory resides;
- how experience is selected, encoded, retained, maintained, retrieved and reconstructed;
- how memory becomes stale, distorted, unavailable, deliberately superseded or forgotten;
- how decision-time memory should remain distinct from knowledge acquired later;
- how reflection differs from review, reporting and evaluation;
- how organisations learn from success, failure, near misses and external experience;
- how single-loop and double-loop learning differ;
- how defensive routines, blame and power interfere with inquiry;
- why identifying and recording a lesson are not the same as applying or institutionalising it;
- how AI-mediated memory differs from organisational memory;
- which risks arise when AI agents retain, retrieve and generate memory;
- which failure modes prevent feedback from changing future response;
- and how learning capability contributes to Organisational Intelligence.

The durable development and reconfiguration of organisational capability belongs to OI-BOK-009.

This chapter owns the process through which evaluative feedback becomes retained and reusable learning. It stops before complete capability adaptation.

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **Organisational memory is not a repository, and organisational learning is not the existence of feedback or change. An organisation learns when credible experience is reconstructed and examined in a way that changes shared understanding or future response, and when that change remains available beyond the individual or episode that produced it.**

Project OI defines **organisational memory** provisionally as:

> **The distributed and dynamic system through which past experience, decisions, knowledge, relationships, routines, artefacts and interpretations are selectively retained, located, reconstructed, contested, updated and made available to current organisational work.**

Project OI defines **organisational reflection** provisionally as:

> **The deliberate examination of experience, evidence, assumptions, action and consequence in order to understand what occurred, why it occurred, what remains uncertain and what should be reconsidered.**

Project OI defines **organisational learning** provisionally as:

> **A change in organisationally available understanding or future response arising from experience, inquiry or acquired knowledge that is retained sufficiently to influence more than the immediate individual or episode.**

This creates several necessary distinctions:

```text
Data
        ≠
Archive
        ≠
Repository
        ≠
Organisational memory
```

```text
Feedback
        ≠
Reflection
        ≠
Lesson
        ≠
Learning
        ≠
Capability adaptation
```

```text
Individual learning
        ≠
Group learning
        ≠
Organisational learning
```

An organisation can:

- store every document and forget why it mattered;
- record a lesson and never retrieve it;
- retrieve a lesson and apply it outside the conditions in which it was valid;
- change behaviour without understanding why;
- improve an outcome through luck and encode the wrong lesson;
- experience failure and suppress the evidence needed for learning;
- conduct a high-quality review whose findings never change policy or practice;
- institutionalise one team’s interpretation while excluding other evidence;
- preserve obsolete knowledge so effectively that it blocks adaptation;
- or allow an AI system to recall a plausible but unverified organisational memory.

The Project OI position is:

> **Learning capability is the organisational capacity to preserve experience with enough provenance and temporal integrity to support reconstruction, create conditions for candid inquiry, form appropriately scoped lessons, revise future understanding or response and keep that revision available long enough to be tested and developed into capability.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Experience

**Experience** is an event, condition, action, interaction or consequence encountered by an individual, group, organisation or system.

Experience does not become learning automatically.

### 3.2 Episode

An **episode** is a bounded sequence of events and actions examined as one unit for:

- reconstruction;
- evaluation;
- reflection;
- or learning.

### 3.3 Organisational Memory

Project OI defines **organisational memory** as:

> **The distributed and dynamic system through which past experience, decisions, knowledge, relationships, routines, artefacts and interpretations are selectively retained, located, reconstructed, contested, updated and made available to current organisational work.**

The definition deliberately avoids treating the organisation as though it has one human-like mind.

### 3.4 Organisational Remembering

**Organisational remembering** is the enacted process through which prior experience is:

- located;
- reconstructed;
- interpreted;
- and brought into current work.

Remembering is not exact playback.

### 3.5 Memory Trace

A **memory trace** is retained evidence or representation from which part of a prior experience may be reconstructed.

Examples:

- document;
- event record;
- conversation;
- decision rationale;
- routine;
- relationship;
- code;
- physical arrangement;
- or person’s recollection.

### 3.6 Memory Item

Project OI uses **memory item** as a working term for a bounded retained representation with identifiable:

- source;
- time;
- scope;
- status;
- and relationship to organisational experience.

The term does not imply a new platform primitive.

### 3.7 Memory Reservoir

A **memory reservoir** is a location, medium or organisational arrangement through which memory is retained.

Walsh and Ungson identify retention structures including:

- individuals;
- culture;
- transformations;
- structures;
- ecology;
- and external archives.[^1]

Project OI uses the broader term **reservoir** without assuming these categories are complete or mutually exclusive.

### 3.8 Acquisition

**Acquisition** is the process through which the organisation encounters or obtains potentially relevant experience, information or knowledge.

### 3.9 Selection

**Selection** is the process through which some aspects of experience receive retention attention while others do not.

Selection may be:

- deliberate;
- habitual;
- technical;
- political;
- or accidental.

### 3.10 Encoding

**Encoding** is the process through which selected experience is represented in a form capable of later retention and retrieval.

Encoding always involves:

- framing;
- compression;
- categorisation;
- and omission.

### 3.11 Retention

**Retention** is the continued preservation of a memory trace or organisational disposition across time.

Retention does not establish accessibility or use.

### 3.12 Maintenance

**Maintenance** is the work required to preserve:

- integrity;
- currentness;
- meaning;
- permissions;
- and retrievability

of retained memory.

### 3.13 Retrieval

**Retrieval** is the process of locating and bringing retained memory into a current context.

Retrieval can be:

- deliberate;
- triggered;
- social;
- procedural;
- or system-mediated.

### 3.14 Recall

**Recall** is the production or reconstruction of prior information without the complete original stimulus.

### 3.15 Recognition

**Recognition** is identifying a current cue, object or condition as connected to prior experience.

### 3.16 Reconstruction

**Reconstruction** is the process of reassembling an account of prior experience from incomplete traces, current context and interpretation.

Reconstruction can introduce:

- hindsight;
- omission;
- narrative coherence;
- and power effects.

### 3.17 Memory Availability

**Memory availability** is the existence of a retained trace that can potentially be accessed.

### 3.18 Memory Accessibility

**Memory accessibility** is the practical ability of an authorised actor or system to locate and use the memory within the required time and context.

### 3.19 Memory Freshness

**Memory freshness** is the degree to which a retained representation reflects the current status of the underlying claim, relationship, routine or condition.

Historical memory may be old and still accurate as history.

Freshness concerns fitness for current use.

### 3.20 Memory Integrity

**Memory integrity** is the degree to which retained memory preserves:

- source;
- time;
- scope;
- status;
- and transformation history

without unauthorised or misleading alteration.

### 3.21 Memory Provenance

**Memory provenance** is the traceable origin and transformation history of a retained memory representation.

### 3.22 Memory Context

**Memory context** is the set of conditions required to interpret the retained experience appropriately.

### 3.23 Decision-Time Memory

**Decision-time memory** is the preserved record of:

- what was known;
- believed;
- authorised;
- and uncertain

when a decision was made.

It remains separate from knowledge acquired later.

### 3.24 Memory Decay

Project OI uses **memory decay** as a working term for loss of:

- detail;
- accessibility;
- confidence;
- currentness;
- or practical usefulness

over time.

### 3.25 Memory Drift

Project OI uses **memory drift** as a working term for gradual divergence between the retained representation and the organisation’s current understanding, practice or environment.

### 3.26 Organisational Amnesia

**Organisational amnesia** is a material loss of access to prior organisational knowledge, rationale or experience required for current or future work.

Amnesia may result from:

- turnover;
- technology change;
- structural change;
- deletion;
- or failure to encode and retrieve.

### 3.27 Institutional Amnesia

**Institutional amnesia** is loss of continuity in the organisation’s understanding of:

- why structures;
- policies;
- relationships;
- and decisions

exist across longer periods or repeated changes.

### 3.28 Forgetting

**Organisational forgetting** is the loss, degradation, inaccessibility or displacement of retained organisational knowledge or memory.

Forgetting may be:

- accidental;
- emergent;
- imposed;
- or deliberate.

### 3.29 Managed Forgetting

Project OI uses **managed forgetting** for the governed retirement, de-emphasis or restriction of memory that is:

- obsolete;
- harmful;
- unlawfully retained;
- duplicative;
- or no longer fit for use.

### 3.30 Unlearning

Project OI uses **unlearning** as:

> **The deliberate questioning and discontinuation of assumptions, routines, interpretations or practices that no longer fit the organisation’s purpose or conditions.**

Unlearning is not equivalent to deleting a record.

### 3.31 Supersession

**Supersession** is the explicit replacement of one:

- claim;
- rule;
- interpretation;
- decision;
- or guidance item

by another while preserving historical trace.

### 3.32 Archival Retention

**Archival retention** preserves material for:

- legal;
- historical;
- accountability;
- or research purposes

without presenting it as current guidance.

### 3.33 Reflection

**Reflection** is deliberate examination of experience and its meaning.

### 3.34 Reflection-in-Action

**Reflection-in-action** is reflective adjustment while work is occurring.

The term is associated with Schön’s work on reflective practice.[^31]

### 3.35 Reflection-on-Action

**Reflection-on-action** is examination after the episode.

### 3.36 Review

A **review** is a structured examination of:

- work;
- evidence;
- outcome;
- or process

against defined questions or criteria.

A review may produce reflection.

It may also remain procedural.

### 3.37 Inquiry

**Inquiry** is disciplined questioning intended to test:

- assumptions;
- explanations;
- evidence;
- and alternative interpretations.

### 3.38 Lesson

A **lesson** is a bounded conclusion about:

- what happened;
- why it matters;
- where it applies;
- and what should influence future understanding or response.

A lesson should preserve uncertainty and scope.

### 3.39 Lesson Identified

A **lesson identified** is a proposed lesson recognised from experience.

### 3.40 Lesson Recorded

A **lesson recorded** has been represented in a durable form.

### 3.41 Lesson Disseminated

A **lesson disseminated** has been made available to actors who may benefit from it.

### 3.42 Lesson Applied

A **lesson applied** has influenced current:

- decision;
- action;
- policy;
- routine;
- or design.

### 3.43 Lesson Institutionalised

A **lesson institutionalised** has become sufficiently embedded in:

- shared knowledge;
- routine;
- role;
- policy;
- structure;
- tool;
- or practice

to influence organisational behaviour beyond the original episode.

Complete capability institutionalisation belongs to Chapter 9.

### 3.44 Organisational Learning

Project OI defines **organisational learning** as:

> **A change in organisationally available understanding or future response arising from experience, inquiry or acquired knowledge that is retained sufficiently to influence more than the immediate individual or episode.**

### 3.45 Individual Learning

**Individual learning** is a change in one person’s:

- understanding;
- skill;
- expectation;
- or behaviour.

### 3.46 Group Learning

**Group learning** is a change in the shared or coordinated understanding and response of a group.

### 3.47 Organisational Learning Process

An **organisational learning process** is the set of interactions through which:

- individual;
- group;
- and organisational

learning becomes connected across levels.

### 3.48 Experiential Learning

**Experiential learning** is learning through action and consequence.

### 3.49 Vicarious Learning

**Vicarious learning** is learning from the experience of other:

- people;
- teams;
- organisations;
- or systems.

### 3.50 Observational Learning

**Observational learning** is learning by observing behaviour, result or demonstration.

### 3.51 Learning Transfer

**Learning transfer** is the movement and application of learning to another:

- person;
- team;
- task;
- or context.

### 3.52 Learning Retention

**Learning retention** is the persistence of learning over time.

### 3.53 Learning Depreciation

**Learning depreciation** is loss in the performance or availability of acquired learning over time.

### 3.54 Learning Persistence

Project OI uses **learning persistence** as a working term for the degree to which a learning-induced change remains available and influential across:

- time;
- personnel;
- and repeated work.

### 3.55 Single-Loop Learning

**Single-loop learning** corrects action or performance while preserving the underlying:

- goals;
- governing values;
- and assumptions.[^6]

### 3.56 Double-Loop Learning

**Double-loop learning** questions and may revise the governing:

- assumptions;
- values;
- policies;
- or objectives

that produced the action.[^6]

### 3.57 Learning About Learning

Project OI uses **learning about learning** for inquiry into how the organisation:

- detects;
- reflects;
- retains;
- and applies learning.

Argyris and Schön use the term **deutero-learning** for learning about organisational learning processes.[^32]

Project OI does not adopt “triple-loop learning” as a core construct in this release because its meanings vary across literatures.

### 3.58 Espoused Theory

An **espoused theory** is the explanation an actor or organisation gives for how it acts.

### 3.59 Theory-in-Use

A **theory-in-use** is the governing pattern inferred from actual behaviour.[^32]

### 3.60 Defensive Routine

A **defensive routine** is an organisational pattern that protects actors from embarrassment or threat while preventing examination of the conditions producing the problem.[^7]

### 3.61 Blame

**Blame** is the attribution of fault or moral responsibility to an actor.

Blame may be warranted.

Blame becomes a learning failure where it substitutes for inquiry into the complete system.

### 3.62 Psychological Safety

**Psychological safety** is a shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking, including speaking about error and uncertainty, is possible within the group.[^28]

Psychological safety does not remove performance standards or accountability.

### 3.63 Debrief

A **debrief** is a structured reflection after performance intended to improve later performance.

### 3.64 After-Action Review

An **after-action review** is a structured review of:

- what was intended;
- what occurred;
- why differences occurred;
- and what should follow.

### 3.65 After-Event Review

**After-event review** is a broader term for structured reflection after:

- success;
- failure;
- or other experience.

### 3.66 Postmortem

A **postmortem** is a structured review of an incident, failure or material event, often used in technical and operational contexts.

### 3.67 Counterfactual Reflection

**Counterfactual reflection** examines how the episode might have unfolded under different actions or conditions.

### 3.68 Near-Miss Learning

**Near-miss learning** is learning from an event that approached failure or harm without producing the complete negative outcome.

### 3.69 Success Learning

**Success learning** is inquiry into:

- why;
- under which conditions;
- and through which mechanism

a desired outcome occurred.

### 3.70 Failure Learning

**Failure learning** is inquiry into an undesired outcome or service failure intended to change future understanding or response.

### 3.71 Superstitious Learning

**Superstitious learning** occurs where the organisation forms a causal belief from coincidental or weakly connected action and outcome.

### 3.72 Success Trap

A **success trap** occurs where successful exploitation of existing knowledge reduces exploration, challenge and sensitivity to environmental change.

### 3.73 Competency Trap

A **competency trap** occurs where improvement in a familiar but potentially inferior routine increases commitment to that routine and reduces search for alternatives.

### 3.74 Learning Myopia

Levinthal and March describe learning myopia as a tendency to privilege:

- the near in time;
- the near in place;
- and success

over distant, external and failure-related knowledge.[^8]

### 3.75 Memory Governance

**Memory governance** is the set of decisions and controls determining:

- what is retained;
- for how long;
- under whose authority;
- with which access;
- and how memory is corrected, superseded or forgotten.

### 3.76 Memory Contamination

Project OI uses **memory contamination** as a working term for the introduction or propagation of:

- incorrect;
- misleading;
- unauthorised;
- or context-stripped

material into organisational memory.

### 3.77 Memory Poisoning

**Memory poisoning** is a deliberate attack or manipulation that causes a memory or retrieval system to return or use harmful content.

### 3.78 AI Memory

**AI memory** is the retained state, records or retrieval mechanism used to make information from prior interactions available to a model or AI agent.

AI memory is not organisational memory by itself.

### 3.79 Episodic AI Memory

**Episodic AI memory** is retained information about specific prior interactions or events.

### 3.80 Semantic AI Memory

**Semantic AI memory** is retained generalised information or assertions derived from prior material.

### 3.81 Procedural AI Memory

**Procedural AI memory** is retained information about:

- how to perform work;
- tool use;
- or preferred process.

These labels borrow from cognitive-memory distinctions and should be treated as engineering analogies rather than evidence that AI has human memory.

### 3.82 Memory Consolidation

**Memory consolidation** is the transformation of lower-level records into more stable or abstract representations.

Consolidation can improve usability.

It can also erase:

- provenance;
- minority evidence;
- and exception.

### 3.83 Memory Retrieval Policy

A **memory retrieval policy** determines which retained material is considered and supplied for a current purpose.

### 3.84 Memory Confidence

**Memory confidence** is the represented strength or certainty associated with a retained claim or reconstruction.

Confidence is not authority.

## 4. Organisational Memory Is Not a Repository

The repository model assumes:

```text
Capture
→ Store
→ Search
→ Remember
```

That sequence is incomplete.

A repository can preserve:

- documents;
- lessons;
- decisions;
- incident reports;
- code;
- conversations;
- and policies

while the organisation remains unable to:

- locate the relevant item;
- determine whether it is current;
- understand why it matters;
- reconstruct the original context;
- distinguish evidence from later interpretation;
- recognise which lesson applies;
- or change future response.

Walsh and Ungson explicitly describe organisational memory through processes of:

- acquisition;
- retention;
- and retrieval,

and warn that memory can be used, misused or abused.[^1]

A technology system can support those processes.

It does not constitute the complete memory system.

### 4.1 Memory Is Distributed

Memory can reside in:

- people;
- teams;
- culture;
- routines;
- structures;
- roles;
- relationships;
- technologies;
- physical arrangements;
- and external archives.

### 4.2 Memory Is Selective

The organisation does not retain everything.

Selection reflects:

- attention;
- incentives;
- power;
- ease of capture;
- and technical affordance.

### 4.3 Memory Is Reconstructive

Past experience is reconstructed from incomplete traces under current conditions.

### 4.4 Memory Is Active

A retained lesson influences the present only when:

- a cue activates it;
- an actor retrieves it;
- a routine embodies it;
- or a system applies it.

### 4.5 Memory Can Constrain

Moorman and Miner find that organisational memory is not unconditionally beneficial to new-product performance and creativity. Its effects depend on how memory is distributed and on environmental conditions.[^10]

Rich memory can improve:

- continuity;
- efficiency;
- and coordination.

It can also increase:

- inertia;
- path dependence;
- and inappropriate reuse.

### 4.6 Memory Requires Social Process

Anand, Manz and Glick argue that information technology needs to be complemented by organisational processes and attention to soft knowledge such as:

- tacit knowledge;
- judgement;
- and intuition.[^13]

### Project OI Principle

> **A repository becomes part of organisational memory only where retained material remains interpretable, retrievable, governable and capable of influencing future work.**

## 5. Feedback Is Not Learning, and Change Is Not Learning

Chapter 7 establishes:

```text
Outcome data
        ≠
Evaluative feedback
        ≠
Organisational learning
```

This chapter adds:

```text
Change
        ≠
Learning
```

Fiol and Lyles distinguish organisational learning from organisational adaptation and caution against assuming that behavioural change proves learning.[^5]

An organisation can change because of:

- command;
- fear;
- turnover;
- resource loss;
- automation;
- imitation;
- or environmental pressure

without improving understanding.

It can also learn without immediate visible behavioural change where:

- the lesson is retained;
- the decision window has not arrived;
- or the organisation deliberately preserves current action under revised understanding.

### 5.1 Feedback Without Reflection

Data arrives.

No one asks what it means.

### 5.2 Reflection Without Learning

The team develops insight.

The insight remains local or temporary.

### 5.3 Learning Without Application

A lesson is retained.

No future cue or process retrieves it.

### 5.4 Application Without Understanding

A rule changes.

People follow it without understanding the mechanism or limits.

### 5.5 Learning Without Persistence

One person changes behaviour.

The change disappears when the person leaves.

### Project OI Principle

> **Organisational learning requires both a meaningful change in understanding or future response and sufficient organisational retention for that change to persist beyond the originating episode.**

## 6. Organisational Memory Reservoirs

Walsh and Ungson’s classic framework identifies several retention structures.[^1]

Project OI updates the interpretation without treating the categories as a fixed ontology.

### 6.1 Individuals

People retain:

- experience;
- skill;
- judgement;
- relationships;
- and recollection.

Strengths:

- rich contextual reconstruction;
- tacit knowledge;
- and adaptive interpretation.

Risks:

- turnover;
- overload;
- selective memory;
- and access dependency.

### 6.2 Culture

Culture retains:

- values;
- categories;
- stories;
- taboos;
- and assumptions

about how the organisation interprets experience.

Culture influences which events become:

- memorable;
- discussable;
- and legitimate.

### 6.3 Transformations and Routines

workflows, routines and practices retain knowledge about:

- sequence;
- coordination;
- controls;
- and response.

Levitt and March describe organisational learning as routine-based, history-dependent and target-oriented, with experience encoded in routines that guide behaviour.[^3]

Routines can preserve knowledge without preserving rationale.

### 6.4 Structures and Roles

Structures retain:

- authority;
- responsibility;
- reporting;
- and expected relationships.

A decision right can embody a lesson about:

- risk;
- or expertise

even where current actors do not know its origin.

### 6.5 Ecology and Material Arrangement

Physical and technical arrangements retain:

- spatial relationships;
- access;
- workflow;
- and constraint.

Examples:

- workstation layout;
- system architecture;
- interface;
- access-control configuration;
- and equipment placement.

### 6.6 Artefacts and Archives

Examples:

- documents;
- datasets;
- code;
- policies;
- decision records;
- recordings;
- and repositories.

Archives provide persistence.

They require interpretation and retrieval.

### 6.7 Relationships and Networks

Relationships retain knowledge about:

- who knows what;
- who can be trusted;
- who has authority;
- and how to gain access.

This connects organisational memory to the transactive-memory system discussed in Chapter 3.

### 6.8 External Memory

Customers, suppliers, regulators, consultants, communities and public sources may retain parts of the organisation’s history and knowledge.

External memory may outlast internal staff.

It may also be:

- inaccessible;
- commercially restricted;
- or interpreted differently.

### 6.9 Technology and AI

Systems can retain:

- state;
- records;
- generated summaries;
- embeddings;
- and prior interactions.

They can improve accessibility.

They can also create:

- false coherence;
- contamination;
- and hidden dependency.

### Reservoir Principle

> **Organisational memory is produced by the relationships among reservoirs, not by declaring one repository to be the organisation’s memory.**

## 7. The Organisational Memory Lifecycle

Project OI proposes the following working lifecycle:

```text
Experience and evaluative feedback
        ↓
Select and acquire
        ↓
Encode with source, time, scope and uncertainty
        ↓
Retain across appropriate reservoirs
        ↓
Maintain integrity, access and currentness
        ↓
Retrieve for a defined purpose
        ↓
Reconstruct with current context and multiple perspectives
        ↓
Use, test and challenge
        ↓
Update, supersede, archive or forget
```

### 7.1 Select and Acquire

Not all experience can or should be retained.

Selection should consider:

- consequence;
- recurrence;
- novelty;
- uncertainty;
- accountability;
- and future value.

### 7.2 Encode

Encoding should preserve:

- what happened;
- source;
- time;
- decision-time context;
- affected scope;
- confidence;
- and unresolved questions.

### 7.3 Retain

Choose reservoirs proportionate to:

- use;
- sensitivity;
- durability;
- and retrievability.

### 7.4 Maintain

Memory needs:

- ownership;
- review;
- correction;
- migration;
- and expiry.

### 7.5 Retrieve

Retrieval should begin with current purpose and context.

### 7.6 Reconstruct

Combine:

- traces;
- perspectives;
- and current knowledge

without pretending the reconstruction is identical to the past.

### 7.7 Use and Test

A memory becomes organisationally meaningful when it influences:

- orientation;
- decision;
- action;
- or inquiry.

### 7.8 Update or Forget

Use:

- correction;
- supersession;
- archival status;
- deletion;
- access restriction;
- or deliberate retirement.

### Project OI Principle

> **Memory lifecycle governance should preserve historical truth while preventing historical material from masquerading as current organisational guidance.**

## 8. Theoretical Foundations

### 8.1 Organisational Memory

Walsh and Ungson establish acquisition, retention and retrieval as foundational memory processes and identify distributed retention structures.[^1]

### 8.2 Organisational Learning Processes

Huber organises organisational learning around:

- knowledge acquisition;
- information distribution;
- information interpretation;
- and organisational memory.[^2]

His framework supports the position that memory is part of learning but not the whole process.

### 8.3 Routine-Based Learning

Levitt and March describe organisational learning as:

- routine-based;
- history-dependent;
- and target-oriented.[^3]

This explains how experience can become embedded in organisational response without remaining in one person’s memory.

### 8.4 Multi-Level Learning

Crossan, Lane and White propose four processes across individual, group and organisational levels:

- intuiting;
- interpreting;
- integrating;
- and institutionalising.[^4]

Their model also highlights the tension between:

- feed-forward learning;
- and feedback from established organisational structures.

Project OI uses this to preserve the difference between individual insight and organisational learning.

### 8.5 Single-Loop and Double-Loop Learning

Argyris distinguishes correction within existing governing variables from inquiry that questions those variables.[^6]

The distinction is central to whether learning improves an existing response or challenges the assumptions producing it.

### 8.6 Defensive Routines

Argyris shows how organisational defensive routines can prevent actors from examining errors and inconsistencies precisely when inquiry is most necessary.[^7]

### 8.7 Learning Myopia

Levinthal and March identify learning biases toward:

- the near;
- the immediate;
- and success.[^8]

The organisation may learn efficiently from experience and still become maladapted because the experience sample is narrow.

### 8.8 Rare Events and Fragments of History

March, Sproull and Tamuz examine how organisations learn from samples of one or fewer, including rare events where experience is limited and histories are reconstructed or simulated.[^9]

### 8.9 Memory and Creativity

Moorman and Miner show that memory can support or constrain creativity depending on conditions.[^10]

### 8.10 Memory in Information Systems

Stein and Zwass describe how information systems can support:

- acquisition;
- retention;
- maintenance;
- search;
- and retrieval

while remaining subject to organisational and technical limitations.[^12]

### 8.11 Forgetting

de Holan and Phillips treat organisational forgetting as dynamic and sometimes strategically necessary rather than only a defect.[^15][^16]

Casey and Olivera emphasise:

- time;
- power;
- and the dynamic relationship between memory and forgetting.[^14]

### 8.12 Unlearning

Nystrom and Starbuck argue that organisations may need to unlearn established beliefs and practices to avoid crisis.[^17]

Project OI treats this as deliberate discontinuation of obsolete governing assumptions, not literal erasure of history.

### 8.13 Debriefing

Tannenbaum and Cerasoli’s meta-analysis of 46 samples and 2,136 participants reports an average effect equivalent to approximately 20 to 25 per cent improvement for properly conducted debriefs, with alignment, structure and facilitation affecting results.[^22]

The average should not be treated as a guaranteed organisational effect.

### 8.14 Success and Failure Learning

Madsen and Desai’s study of the orbital launch vehicle industry finds stronger learning from failure than success in that setting, with failure knowledge depreciating more slowly.[^19]

The result is setting-specific and should not be universalised.

### 8.15 Near Misses

Morris and Moore show how accountability and counterfactual thinking affect learning after close calls.[^25]

Near misses can increase learning.

They can also create false confidence where survival is interpreted as evidence of safety.[^27]

## 9. Selection and Encoding

The organisation remembers selectively.

### 9.1 What Becomes Memorable

Experience is more likely to be retained where it is:

- visible;
- dramatic;
- recent;
- high status;
- measurable;
- blame-relevant;
- or connected to an existing narrative.

Quiet success and weak signals may disappear.

### 9.2 Encoding Is Interpretation

The person writing the record decides:

- what the episode was;
- where it started;
- which actors mattered;
- and what caused the outcome.

### 9.3 Source Separation

Preserve:

- primary evidence;
- participant account;
- evaluator interpretation;
- and lesson

as distinct layers.

### 9.4 Decision-Time Context

Encode:

- original assumptions;
- evidence;
- confidence;
- alternatives;
- and constraints.

Do not update the historical decision record to match later knowledge.

### 9.5 Multiple Perspectives

Material episodes may require:

- frontline;
- customer;
- technical;
- manager;
- and external

perspectives.

### 9.6 Negative and Disconfirming Evidence

Capture evidence that challenges the preferred account.

### 9.7 Selection Governance

For significant learning, state:

- why the episode was selected;
- who selected it;
- and which experiences were excluded.

### 9.8 Compression

Summaries improve accessibility.

They should link to:

- evidence;
- detail;
- disagreement;
- and uncertainty.

### Project OI Principle

> **The organisation begins learning before the review starts because the selection and encoding of experience determine what can later be remembered and questioned.**

### 10.2 Ownership

A memory item or reservoir should have an owner for:

Knowledge and performance can depreciate through:

Darr, Argote and Epple show acquisition, transfer and depreciation of knowledge in a service setting.[^34]

Critical memory may need more than one reservoir.

Redundancy should not create uncontrolled divergence.

### 10.8 Privacy and Legal Retention

Not everything should be retained indefinitely.

Memory governance should respect:

- privacy;
- legal requirements;
- consent;
- and minimisation.

### Project OI Principle

## 13. Reflection and Inquiry

Reflection turns experience into a question rather than a settled narrative.

### 13.1 Reflection Questions

- What did we expect?
- What occurred?
- Which assumptions held?
- Which did not?
- What was missing?
- Which mechanism operated?
- What alternative explanation remains?
- What should we preserve?
- What should we challenge?

### 13.2 Reflection After Feedback

Anseel, Lievens and Schollaert find in two simulation studies that reflection combined with feedback improved subsequent performance more than feedback alone, while reflection without feedback did not consistently produce the same benefit.[^21]

The result concerns specific tasks and does not prove that every reflective exercise improves organisational learning.

### 13.3 Reflection-in-Action

Actors may reflect during work and adjust.

This supports:

- adaptation;
- and repair.

It can also create undocumented variation.

### 13.4 Reflection-on-Action

Post-episode reflection supports:

- reconstruction;
- comparison;
- and lesson formation.

### 13.5 Inquiry Versus Advocacy

Inquiry seeks to test the account.

Advocacy seeks to defend one interpretation or interest.

Both may be legitimate.

A learning review should distinguish them.

### 13.6 Questions Before Answers

Beginning with the lesson can bias the reconstruction.

Begin with:

- evidence;
- expectation;
- and divergence.

### 13.7 Reflection Requires Time

An organisation that removes all slack may preserve activity and eliminate reflection.

### Project OI Principle

> **Reflection should slow interpretation long enough to test experience without delaying necessary operational response.**

## 14. After-Action Reviews, Debriefs and Postmortems

### 14.1 Core Questions

A useful after-action review asks:

```text
What was intended?
What occurred?
Why did the difference occur?
What should be preserved or changed?
Who owns the follow-up?
```

### 14.2 Include Success

Ellis and Davidi find that after-event review of both successful and failed experience can improve learning because success also contains assumptions, variation and mechanism that require examination.[^20]

### 14.3 Structure and Alignment

Tannenbaum and Cerasoli report average performance benefits from debriefs across a diverse evidence base, with effectiveness influenced by alignment, facilitation and structure.[^22]

A later meta-analysis of 61 studies likewise reports a positive average effect and highlights alignment and objective performance media.[^23]

### 14.4 Evidence Before Memory Competition

Use:

- timelines;
- recordings;
- operational trace;
- and artefacts

to support reconstruction.

### 14.5 Blameless Does Not Mean Accountless

A blameless postmortem avoids making personal blame the default causal model.

It should still address:

- decisions;
- authority;
- violations;
- and accountability.

### 14.6 Action Items

An action item should identify:

- owner;
- rationale;
- priority;
- due date;
- evidence of completion;
- and intended learning effect.

### 14.7 Review Closure

The review is not complete when the document is published.

It requires:

- application;
- or an explicit decision not to apply.

### 14.8 Review Fatigue

Too many reviews can produce:

- boilerplate;
- low participation;
- and unowned actions.

### 14.9 NASA Lessons-Learned Lifecycle

NASA’s current lessons-learned lifecycle identifies:

- Collect;
- Record;
- Disseminate;
- and Apply

as non-linear elements, with application into practice treated as crucial.[^29]

Earlier GAO reviews found that lessons were not routinely identified, collected or shared and that the presence of a lessons-learned system did not assure application.[^30]

This provides a practical reminder:

```text
Lesson identified
        ≠
Lesson recorded
        ≠
Lesson disseminated
        ≠
Lesson applied
```

## 15. Learning From Success, Failure and Near Misses

### 15.1 Learning From Failure

Failure can:

- attract attention;
- expose assumptions;
- and motivate search.

Madsen and Desai find stronger learning from failure than success in the orbital-launch industry they studied.[^19]

### 15.2 Failure Can Also Reduce Learning

Failure can produce:

- blame;
- secrecy;
- threat rigidity;
- and premature simplification.

### 15.3 Learning From Success

Success can reveal:

- mechanism;
- enabling condition;
- adaptive performance;
- and positive deviation.

It can also produce:

- overconfidence;
- false attribution;
- and success traps.

### 15.4 Near Miss

A near miss can provide evidence of:

- vulnerability;
- successful defence;
- or luck.

### 15.5 Near-Miss Bias

Survival can be interpreted as proof that the decision was acceptable.

Dillon and Tinsley describe how near misses can distort risk interpretation and increase willingness to take risk.[^27]

### 15.6 Counterfactual Inquiry

Morris and Moore show that learning after close calls is shaped by how actors construct counterfactual accounts and by accountability conditions.[^25]

### 15.7 Complexity

Haunschild and Sullivan find that heterogeneous accident causes in airlines can prompt broader and deeper learning than homogeneous causes in that setting.[^26]

### 15.8 Learning From Small Samples

Rare events require:

- simulation;
- comparison;
- vicarious experience;
- and careful uncertainty

because direct experience is limited.[^9]

### Project OI Principle

> **The organisation should examine success, failure and near miss for mechanism and boundary conditions rather than treating outcome valence as the lesson.**

## 16. Single-Loop, Double-Loop and Learning About Learning

### 16.1 Single-Loop Learning

Example:

```text

→ add capacity and change routing
→ preserve the service target and operating assumptions
```

### 16.2 Double-Loop Learning

Example:

```text
Customer responses are too slow
→ question whether speed is the correct primary objective

```

### 16.3 Learning About Learning

Example:

```text
The organisation repeatedly records lessons but does not apply them
→ examine how reviews, ownership, retrieval and incentives operate
→ redesign the learning process
```

### 16.4 Single-Loop Is Not Inferior

Single-loop correction is appropriate where:

- goals;
- assumptions;
- and values

remain valid.

### 16.5 Double-Loop Is Not Always Safe or Necessary

Questioning governing values can:

- consume time;
- destabilise accountability;
- or reopen settled ethical constraints.

### 16.6 Trigger Conditions

Double-loop inquiry may be warranted where:

- failure recurs;
- workarounds persist;
- metrics conflict with purpose;
- exceptions become normal;
- or the same corrective action produces no improvement.

### 16.7 Theory-in-Use

Compare:

- what the organisation says it values;
- and what its decisions, incentives and routines reveal.

### Project OI Principle

> **Learning depth should match the evidence: correct action where the governing frame remains sound, and challenge the frame where repeated evidence shows that the assumptions themselves are failing.**

limits inquiry.

### 17.9 Confidential and Public Learning

Some detail may require restricted review.

The organisation should still retain a usable and non-misleading lesson for wider application.

> **Learning conditions should allow candid evidence and challenge without removing legitimate accountability or allowing powerful actors to define the memory unilaterally.**

### 18.8 Too Little Memory

- supersede;
- restrict;

### Project OI Principle

> **A learning organisation must preserve enough history to remain accountable while retiring enough obsolete knowledge to remain capable of change.**

## 22. AI-Mediated Memory and AI agent Memory

AI systems make memory more searchable and generative.

They also change the risk.

### 22.1 AI Memory Is Not Organisational Memory

An AI agent’s memory may contain:

- prior messages;
- summaries;
- retrieved records;
- and generated inferences.

The organisation’s memory also includes:

- people;
- routines;
- authority;
- evidence;
- structures;
- and external relationships.

### 22.2 Long-Term Memory Remains Difficult

Maharana and colleagues’ LoCoMo benchmark contains long conversations averaging 600 turns and 16,000 tokens across up to 32 sessions. Long-context and retrieval-augmented approaches improved performance but remained substantially below human performance on long-range temporal and causal understanding.[^36]

LongMemEval evaluates 500 questions across five memory abilities:

- information extraction;
- multi-session reasoning;
- temporal reasoning;
- knowledge updates;
- and abstention.

The authors report roughly a 30 per cent accuracy drop for commercial assistants and long-context models across sustained interactions in that benchmark.[^37]

These are benchmark results, not direct measures of organisational memory.

### 22.3 Retrieval Is a Policy

A memory system decides:

- what to store;
- how to index;
- what to retrieve;
- and what to omit.

### 22.4 Compression and Consolidation

Summarising many interactions can improve efficiency.

It can also:

- convert inference into fact;
- remove disagreement;
- and lose provenance.

### 22.5 Knowledge Update

The system must distinguish:

- current claim;
- historical claim;
- correction;
- and contradiction.

### 22.6 Abstention

The AI agent should be able to state that it:

- does not remember;
- lacks sufficient evidence;
- or finds conflicting memory.

### 22.7 Fabricated Memory

A model can generate a plausible event not supported by retained evidence.

### 22.8 Cross-User and Cross-Workspace Leakage

Memory must remain permission- and tenancy-bound.

### 22.9 Memory Poisoning

AgentPoison demonstrates that long-term memory or RAG knowledge bases can be targeted with stealthy poisoning attacks that alter AI agent behaviour without retraining the underlying model.[^38]

The study uses three AI agent settings and should be treated as security evidence for memory attack surfaces, not as an estimate of real-world prevalence.

### 22.10 Provenance

AI memory should preserve:

- source;
- user;
- time;
- transformation;
- confidence;
- and permission.

### 22.11 Memory Correction

Users and authorised owners need a route to:

- challenge;
- correct;
- and supersede

AI memory.

### 22.12 Model Change

A model change can alter:

- consolidation;
- retrieval;
- and interpretation

of the same retained memory.

### 22.13 AI Memory Record

For material use, preserve:

```text
Memory source
Memory type
Created by
Created at
Transformation
Model and version
Confidence
Scope
Permission
Supersession
Use
```

### Project OI Principle

> **AI can mediate organisational memory, but generated recall should never acquire authority merely because it is fluent, persistent or repeatedly retrieved.**

```text

        ↓
```

- provenance;
- correctness;
- update;
- leakage;

> **Select learning depth according to consequence, recurrence, uncertainty and the level of assumption under challenge, not according to the length of the review template.**

## 25. SMB Context

SMBs often remember through:

- founder recollection;
- long-serving staff;
- direct customer relationships;
- and informal stories.

This creates rich context and high fragility.

### Common Strengths

- rapid access to experience;
- practical judgement;
- and short feedback paths.

### Common Weaknesses

- founder memory dependency;
- lessons retained orally;
- inconsistent source evidence;
- and knowledge loss through turnover.

### Lightweight Memory Practice

For a material lesson, record:

```text
What happened
What we expected
What mattered
Evidence
What we learned
Where it applies
What changes
Owner
Review date
Source links
```

### Monthly Learning Review

Review:

- repeated exceptions;
- customer complaints;
- failed or successful workarounds;
- near misses;
- changed assumptions;
- and stale guidance.

### Founder Guardrail

The objective is not to eliminate founder memory.

It is to prevent one person from being the only place where:

- rationale;
- exception;
- and history

can be reconstructed.

### Tool Discipline

An SMB does not need a vast lessons repository.

It needs a small number of:

- trusted;
- current;
- source-linked;
- and retrievable

lessons connected to real work.

### AI Memory

Where an AI assistant uses memory:

- show the source;
- permit correction;
- restrict sensitive material;
- and avoid presenting generated summaries as approved fact.

## 26. Organisational Memory, Reflection and Learning Failure Modes

### 26.1 Repository Substitution

A storage system is treated as organisational memory.

### 26.2 Capture Fetish

The organisation records increasing amounts without improving retrieval or use.

### 26.3 Lesson Graveyard

Lessons are documented and never applied.

### 26.4 Retention Substitution

The existence of a record is treated as accessibility.

### 26.5 Search Substitution

Keyword retrieval is treated as remembering.

### 26.6 Source Loss

The retained lesson cannot be traced to evidence.

### 26.7 Context Stripping

The lesson loses:

- scope;
- time;
- and conditions.

### 26.8 Decision-Time Erasure

Later knowledge overwrites what was known at the time.

### 26.9 Summary-as-Source

A compressed interpretation replaces the original evidence.

### 26.10 Reconstruction Concealment

The current account is presented as exact historical fact.

### 26.11 Hindsight Contamination

Later outcome makes prior uncertainty appear obvious.

### 26.12 Narrative Simplification

A complex episode becomes one convenient story.

### 26.13 Official-Memory Capture

Powerful actors determine the accepted memory unilaterally.

### 26.14 Prestige Selection

High-status experiences are retained while frontline evidence disappears.

### 26.15 Success-Only Memory

The organisation records achievements and suppresses failure.

### 26.16 Failure-Only Memory

The organisation studies breakdown and ignores successful adaptation.

### 26.17 Near-Miss Dismissal

Absence of harm is treated as absence of learning value.

### 26.18 Near-Miss Normalisation

Repeated survival increases risk tolerance.

### 26.19 Lesson Laundering

Uncertain interpretation becomes accepted organisational fact through repetition.

### 26.20 Lesson Inflation

A local observation is presented as a universal rule.

### 26.21 Lesson Under-Specification

The lesson states what happened without:

- implication;
- scope;
- or owner.

### 26.22 Stale Memory

Historical guidance is retrieved as current.

### 26.23 Supersession Failure

New guidance does not identify or retire prior guidance.

### 26.24 Definition Drift

The lesson retains its name while meaning changes.

### 26.25 Memory Drift

The retained account diverges from practice or current knowledge.

### 26.26 Accidental Forgetting

Turnover, deletion or migration removes needed memory.

### 26.27 Institutional Amnesia

The organisation loses why a structure or policy exists.

### 26.28 Inability to Forget

Obsolete knowledge remains active indefinitely.

### 26.29 Unlearning Theatre

The organisation announces that it has moved on while routines and incentives remain unchanged.

### 26.30 Deletion Substitution

A record is deleted without changing the obsolete theory-in-use.

### 26.31 Knowledge Hoarding

Memory remains with actors who benefit from exclusive access.

### 26.32 Founder Memory Dependency

One person remains the primary reconstruction mechanism.

### 26.33 Transactive-Memory Collapse

The organisation no longer knows who knows what.

### 26.34 Turnover Amnesia

Capability and rationale disappear with personnel.

### 26.35 Technology Lock-In

Memory is trapped in one format or provider.

### 26.36 Archive Without Migration

Retained material becomes unreadable or disconnected.

### 26.37 Retrieval Mismatch

The memory exists but cannot be found using current vocabulary or context.

### 26.38 Retrieval Overload

Too many weakly ranked lessons obscure the relevant one.

### 26.39 Retrieval Without Applicability

A similar episode is used despite different conditions.

### 26.40 Memory Without Authority

The organisation cannot tell which retained guidance governs.

### 26.41 Dissemination Substitution

Sharing the lesson is treated as application.

### 26.42 Application Absence

No future workflow, decision or practice retrieves the lesson.

### 26.43 Application Without Understanding

Actors copy the response without the mechanism or boundary.

### 26.44 Institutionalisation of a Wrong Lesson

A weak causal interpretation becomes routine.

### 26.45 Superstitious Learning

Coincidental action and outcome become a rule.

### 26.46 Overgeneralisation

A narrow lesson is applied broadly.

### 26.47 Undergeneralisation

The organisation treats every episode as unique and learns nothing reusable.

### 26.48 Success Trap

Current success suppresses exploration.

### 26.49 Competency Trap

Improvement in a familiar routine reinforces an inferior path.

### 26.50 Failure Fixation

The organisation overlearns from one failure and creates excessive control.

### 26.51 Recency Bias

Recent experience displaces more relevant historical evidence.

### 26.52 Availability Bias

Vivid events dominate lesson formation.

### 26.53 Single-Loop Trap

### 26.54 Double-Loop Overreach

Every operational defect becomes a challenge to purpose or values.

### 26.55 Defensive Routine

### 26.56 Blame Substitution

### 26.57 Accountability Evasion

Generic language hides material conditions.

### 26.61 Action-Item Decay

### 26.62 Review Without Evidence

### 26.63 Evidence Without Reflection

Insight does not produce an explicit memory or response change.

Known consequences rewrite evaluation of the original decision.

### 26.66 Causal Overclaim

One episode becomes proof of mechanism.

## 28. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Memory Is Not a Repository

> Organisational memory depends on distributed retention, reconstruction, retrieval and use rather than storage alone.

### P2 — Memory Is Selective

> What an organisation remembers reflects attention, power, incentive and technical affordance as well as objective significance.

### P3 — Memory Is Reconstructive

> Organisational remembering will combine historical traces with current context and interpretation rather than reproduce the past exactly.

### P4 — Retention Is Not Availability

> A retained lesson contributes little to Organisational Intelligence where authorised actors cannot locate and interpret it in time.

### P5 — Retrieval Must Be Purpose-Bound

> Similarity alone is insufficient for selecting organisational memory because relevance depends on current purpose, authority and conditions.

### P6 — Decision-Time Memory Protects Learning

> Preserving what was known and uncertain at the time allows outcome learning without hindsight rewriting.

### P7 — Feedback Is Not Learning

> Evaluative evidence becomes learning only through reflection, interpretation and retained change in future understanding or response.

### P8 — Change Is Not Learning

> Behavioural or structural change can occur without improved understanding, while learning may precede visible change.

### P9 — Individual Insight Is Not Organisational Learning

> Learning becomes organisational where it is made available beyond the individual and influences shared or repeated work.

### P10 — Learning Crosses Levels

> Organisational learning depends on conversion among individual insight, group interpretation, integrated action and organisational retention.

### P11 — Lessons Require a Lifecycle

> Identification, recording, dissemination, application and persistence are distinct conditions.

### P12 — Structured Debriefing Can Improve Performance

> Debriefs are more likely to support learning where evidence, purpose, alignment, facilitation and follow-up are adequate.

### P13 — Success and Failure Produce Different Learning Conditions

> Failure may increase attention and search while success may reveal effective mechanism or create overconfidence.

### P14 — Near Misses Are Ambiguous Evidence

> A near miss can reveal vulnerability or successful defence, but survival can also increase unjustified confidence.

### P15 — Single-Loop and Double-Loop Learning Serve Different Needs

> Corrective action is appropriate where governing assumptions remain valid, while repeated or systemic evidence may require questioning the frame.

### P16 — Defensive Routines Block Learning

> Organisations will suppress or distort learning where inquiry threatens identity, status or control.

### P17 — Psychological Safety Is Necessary but Insufficient

> Candour supports learning only where evidence, accountability, interpretation and application also exist.

### P18 — Forgetting Can Be Functional

> Responsible Organisational Intelligence requires both durable historical memory and governed retirement of obsolete or harmful guidance.

### P19 — Unlearning Is Not Deletion

> Discontinuing an assumption or routine requires changed theory-in-use, not merely removal of a record.

### P20 — Memory Can Create Inertia

> Rich organisational memory can improve continuity while reducing exploration and adaptation under changed conditions.

### P21 — Vicarious Learning Requires Recontextualisation

> External experience becomes useful only where mechanism, evidence and receiving conditions are understood.

### P22 — AI Memory Is Not Organisational Memory

> Persistent model context or retrieval improves access to records but does not reproduce organisational roles, routines, authority and social memory.

### P23 — AI Memory Requires Provenance and Security

> AI agent memory will create organisational risk where source, transformation, permission, update and poisoning are not governed.

### P24 — Learning Persistence Requires Use

> A lesson becomes durable where it remains retrievable, is applied in future work and survives changes in personnel and context.

### P25 — Learning Quality Shapes Adaptation Quality

> Capability change will be weak or harmful where the retained learning is inaccurate, overgeneralised, politically filtered or context-stripped.

## 30. Practical Principles

### 30.1 Preserve Evidence Before Interpretation

Do not let the lesson replace the source.

### 30.2 Record What Was Known at the Time

Protect decision-time integrity.

### 30.3 Review Success as Well as Failure

Do not assume positive outcomes explain themselves.

### 30.4 Treat Near Misses as Evidence, Not Proof of Safety

Examine the defence and the luck.

### 30.5 Ask What Changed in Understanding

Do not equate behaviour change with learning.

### 30.6 Distinguish Single-Loop and Double-Loop Questions

Challenge the frame only where evidence warrants it.

### 30.7 Use Structured Debriefs

Align:

- participants;
- evidence;
- question;
- and intended use.

### 30.8 Make Reviews Safe and Accountable

Avoid both blame substitution and accountability evasion.

### 30.9 Form Scoped Lessons

State where the lesson applies and where it may not.

### 30.10 Preserve Uncertainty

A lesson can be useful without being certain.

### 30.11 Give Lessons Owners and Review Dates

Avoid orphaned guidance.

### 30.12 Connect Lessons to Future Cues

Make them retrievable at the point of work.

### 30.13 Apply Before Claiming Institutionalisation

Publication is not application.

### 30.14 Test Learning in Later Work

Observe whether the lesson improves response.

### 30.15 Maintain Current and Historical Status

Do not delete the past to keep the present clean.

### 30.16 Govern Forgetting

Retire obsolete or harmful guidance deliberately.

### 30.17 Preserve Multiple Reservoirs for Critical Memory

Avoid one-person and one-system dependency.

### 30.18 Review Repeated Workarounds

They are memory about system weakness.

### 30.19 Recontextualise External Lessons

Do not copy outcomes without mechanisms.

### 30.20 Treat Metrics and Evaluations as Memory Inputs, Not Memory Truth

Preserve their limits.

### 30.21 Distinguish AI Recall From Source Evidence

Generated memory should remain attributable and contestable.

### 30.22 Allow AI to Abstain

Do not require plausible recall where evidence is missing.

### 30.23 Protect Memory Boundaries

Prevent cross-user and cross-workspace leakage.

### 30.24 Audit AI Memory for Poisoning and Drift

Persistence increases the consequence of contamination.

### 30.25 Hand Learning Into Capability Change

A lesson is incomplete as OI if it cannot inform the next capability decision.

## 31. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Organisational memory, reflection and learning form the primary operational capability of **Learn**.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Feedback credibility
× memory integrity
× reflective inquiry
× learning application
= organisational learning potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Feedback Without Memory

```text
The organisation observes consequence
→ evidence is not retained
→ later actors start from zero
```

### Memory Without Retrieval

```text
The lesson exists
→ nobody finds it
→ prior experience does not influence work
```

### Retrieval Without Reflection

```text
A prior case is found
→ its assumptions remain untested
→ history is copied rather than learned from
```

### Reflection Without Organisational Change

```text
The team gains insight
→ no shared memory or future route changes
→ learning remains local
```

### Learning Without Persistence

```text
Behaviour improves temporarily
→ turnover or disuse erases it
→ organisational capability does not strengthen
```

### AI Memory Without Governance

```text
The AI agent recalls prior material
→ source, currentness and permission are unclear
→ persistence amplifies error or leakage
```

### Learning Without Capability Handoff

```text
A credible lesson is retained
→ no capability-change decision follows
→ Improve remains incomplete
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when it can preserve experience without falsifying it, reconstruct and challenge that experience under current conditions, form appropriately bounded lessons, and keep those lessons available strongly enough to change future understanding and response.**

## 32. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises organisational memory, organisational learning, routine theory, debriefing, failure learning, forgetting, knowledge systems and emerging AI-memory research;
- does not establish a validated Project OI memory or learning construct;
- does not prove that the proposed Organisational Memory, Reflection and Learning Process is complete;
- and does not provide one universal method for determining whether organisational learning has occurred.

### 32.1 Anthropomorphism

Organisations do not remember or learn in the same way as individual humans.

Organisation-level language is an analytic abstraction over:

- people;
- routines;
- structures;
- and artefacts.

### 32.2 Memory Reservoir Model

Walsh and Ungson’s retention bins are foundational but have been debated and extended.

Project OI uses them as a starting framework rather than a final ontology.

### 32.3 Reconstruction

The historical account is shaped by:

- current identity;
- power;
- and available evidence.

Complete historical neutrality is impossible.

### 32.4 Learning Evidence

Changed behaviour may not indicate learning, while learning may not produce immediate visible action.

### 32.5 Single- and Double-Loop Learning

These concepts are influential but difficult to measure reliably at organisational level.

### 32.6 Debrief Meta-Analyses

Average debrief effects combine:

- training;
- simulation;
- medical;
- and operational settings.

They do not guarantee the same effect in all organisations.

### 32.7 Failure Learning

Madsen and Desai’s findings come from orbital launch vehicles.

Other settings may learn differently from success and failure.

### 32.8 Near Misses

Near misses can increase inquiry or false confidence.

Classification depends on interpretation.

### 32.9 Forgetting

It can be difficult to distinguish:

- intentional forgetting;
- loss;
- suppression;
- and unlearning.

### 32.10 Power

Official memory may privilege dominant actors and omit marginalised experience.

### 32.11 Privacy and Rights

Retention and learning needs can conflict with:

- privacy;
- legal deletion;
- and employee rights.

### 32.12 AI Memory Benchmarks

LoCoMo and LongMemEval evaluate conversational memory in artificial benchmark settings.

They do not measure complete organisational memory.

### 32.13 AI Memory Security

AgentPoison demonstrates a feasible class of attack under experimental conditions.

It does not estimate prevalence in deployed organisations.

### 32.14 Model Change

AI-memory capability and architecture are changing quickly.

Current findings require continual review.

### 32.15 Institutionalisation Boundary

This chapter includes learning persistence and initial update.

The complete formation, distribution, retirement and reconfiguration of capability belongs to Chapter 9.

## 35. Editorial Guardrail

> **Project OI must not confuse data retention, repositories, lessons documents, review meetings, behavioural change or persistent AI context with organisational memory or learning. Learning contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where experience can be reconstructed with integrity, examined through candid and evidence-bearing inquiry, translated into appropriately scoped lessons, and retained strongly enough to change future understanding or response.**
