## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines how organisations encounter, select and direct attention towards signals that may matter.

Its central question is:

> **How does something occurring inside or outside an organisation become worthy of collective attention?**

The chapter does not assume that more information creates greater intelligence. It examines the organisational structures through which some events become visible, interpretable and actionable while others remain unnoticed, suppressed, fragmented or dismissed.

Signals and attention form the beginning of the current Project OI operational model:

```text
Signals and experience
        ↓
Attention
        ↓
Context and interpretation
        ↓
Organisational orientation
```

A failure at this stage can prevent relevant knowledge, judgement and response from ever entering the organisational process.

---

## 2. Core Finding

An organisation does not respond to everything that occurs.

It responds to what its people, systems, routines and structures cause it to notice and treat as relevant.

The resulting principle is:

> **Organisational attention is not merely an individual cognitive act. It is an organisational allocation process shaped by roles, rules, relationships, incentives, technologies, routines and communication channels.**

This means that an organisation may possess relevant information without attending to it.

It may also direct disproportionate attention towards information that is:

- recent;
- vivid;
- senior-sponsored;
- easy to measure;
- politically safe;
- commercially urgent;
- already consistent with existing beliefs;
- or amplified by the design of its systems.

The question is therefore not only:

> What information does the organisation have?

It is also:

> What can become visible, to whom, under what conditions, with what priority, and through which pathway?

## 3. Key Definitions

### 3.1 Event

Something that occurs in the organisation or its environment.

An event exists whether or not the organisation notices it.

Examples:

- a customer delays payment;
- a defect is introduced;
- an employee raises a concern;
- a supplier misses a delivery;
- a competitor changes pricing;
- a workflow fails;
- a model begins producing lower-quality outputs.

### 3.2 Data Point

A recorded representation of an event or state.

A data point may be accurate, inaccurate, partial, duplicated or irrelevant.

### 3.3 Cue

A feature of an event or data point that an actor uses to begin interpretation.

A cue is selected rather than inherently meaningful.

### 3.4 Signal

A cue, event or pattern that may indicate a material condition, change, risk, opportunity or need for response.

A signal is relational. Its significance depends on:

- organisational purpose;
- context;
- thresholds;
- timing;
- risk;
- prior knowledge;
- and the consequences of missing it.

### 3.5 Weak Signal

An early, incomplete or ambiguous indication of a potentially significant development.

A weak signal may have:

- low confidence;
- low frequency;
- incomplete evidence;
- uncertain meaning;
- or limited organisational sponsorship.

Weak does not mean unimportant.

It means that significance is not yet fully established.

### 3.6 Noise

Information that consumes attention without materially improving orientation or response in the relevant context.

Noise is also contextual. Information irrelevant to one decision may be critical to another.

### 3.7 Attention

The selective allocation of limited cognitive and organisational processing capacity.

At the organisational level, attention includes:

- what is monitored;
- what is measured;
- what is discussed;
- what is escalated;
- what appears in dashboards;
- what leaders ask about;
- what systems notify;
- what meetings prioritise;
- and what is ignored.

### 3.8 Attention Structure

The organisational arrangement through which issues, answers and decision-makers are brought together.

Attention structures include:

- roles;
- reporting lines;
- meeting cadences;
- dashboards;
- thresholds;
- escalation pathways;
- inboxes;
- queues;
- workflows;
- incentives;
- policies;
- and software interfaces.

### 3.9 Attention Episode

A bounded period in which a signal competes for, gains, retains or loses organisational attention.

### 3.10 Attentional Debt

The accumulated organisational consequence of repeatedly deferring, fragmenting or suppressing signals that require interpretation or response.

This is a Project OI working term, not an established construct.

Attentional debt may later surface as:

- repeated incidents;
- customer churn;
- unplanned work;
- compliance exposure;
- employee frustration;
- strategic surprise;
- or dependence on heroic intervention.

## 4. Theoretical Foundations

### 4.1 Attention as a Scarce Resource

Herbert Simon argued that information abundance consumes a scarce resource: the attention of recipients.[^1]

The practical implication is that an information system should not be judged only by how much information it can deliver. It should also be judged by how effectively it helps allocate attention.

This becomes more important as organisations add:

- dashboards;
- communication platforms;
- AI assistants;
- automated alerts;
- workflow notifications;
- and enterprise search.

Each may improve access while also increasing demands on finite attention.

### 4.2 The Attention-Based View of the Firm

William Ocasio’s attention-based view proposes that organisational behaviour is shaped by how the firm channels and distributes the attention of decision-makers.[^2]

Three implications are especially important:

1. Decision-makers act on the issues and possible answers that receive their attention.
2. What receives attention depends on the situation and the organisation’s attention structures.
3. Rules, resources and relationships influence which issues, answers and people meet in organisational communications and procedures.

This makes attention a structural concern rather than simply a matter of personal discipline.

### 4.3 Organisations as Interpretation Systems

Daft and Weick describe organisations as interpretation systems.[^3]

Organisations differ in:

- how actively they scan their environments;
- how analysable they believe the environment to be;
- how they reduce equivocality;
- and how interpretation influences strategy and decision-making.

This suggests that sensing cannot be separated cleanly from assumptions about the environment.

An organisation that assumes its environment is stable may scan differently from one that expects discontinuity.

### 4.4 Sensemaking

Sensemaking research explains how circumstances are turned into an explicitly comprehended situation that can support action.[^4]

Important implications include:

- cues do not interpret themselves;
- ambiguity may support several plausible accounts;
- language and interaction help stabilise meaning;
- action can generate information;
- and interpretation is often provisional.

For Project OI, attention is a gateway into sensemaking.

A signal that never gains attention cannot become part of organisational orientation.

### 4.5 Weak Signals

Ansoff’s work on strategic surprise argues that organisations can sometimes respond progressively to incomplete indications before a threat or opportunity is fully understood.[^5]

This does not mean treating every anomaly as a crisis.

It means creating mechanisms through which ambiguous signals can:

- be retained;
- accumulate;
- gain context;
- be compared;
- and receive graduated attention as evidence develops.

### 4.6 High Reliability and Collective Mindfulness

High-reliability research emphasises practices such as:[^6]

- attention to anomalies and near misses;
- reluctance to simplify;
- sensitivity to current operations;
- resilience;
- and deference to relevant expertise.

These practices illustrate that reliable attention is not passive monitoring.

It requires an organisational disposition to notice evidence that challenges expectations and to involve expertise wherever it resides.

### 4.7 Information Overload

Research on information overload consistently shows that excessive information demands can impair decision-making, productivity and well-being.[^7][^8]

The design implication is not simply to reduce information volume.

Organisations need mechanisms for:

- filtering;
- prioritisation;
- relevance;
- timing;
- role-based routing;
- aggregation;
- and controlled interruption.

A quiet system can still hide important signals.

A noisy system can make every signal functionally invisible.

Recent research also extends overload from attention to information towards relational overload: uncertainty about whom to attend to in high-permeability workplaces.[^10]

## 5. Signals Are Not Inherently Important

A signal becomes organisationally significant through the interaction of several conditions.

### 5.1 Materiality

Would acting or failing to act plausibly affect:

- customers;
- employees;
- revenue;
- cost;
- safety;
- legal obligations;
- reputation;
- operations;
- strategy;
- or organisational capability?

### 5.2 Relevance

Does the signal relate to a current purpose, decision, risk, commitment or operating condition?

### 5.3 Novelty

Does it differ meaningfully from what the organisation expected?

### 5.4 Urgency

How quickly could the value of response decay?

### 5.5 Uncertainty

How confident is the organisation in the signal and its interpretation?

### 5.6 Reversibility

What happens if the organisation acts too early or too late?

### 5.7 Recurrence

Is this:

- an isolated event;
- a repeated event;
- an emerging pattern;
- or a chronic condition that has become normalised?

### 5.8 Source Credibility

Who or what generated the signal?

What is known about:

- accuracy;
- incentives;
- proximity;
- competence;
- data quality;
- and potential bias?

### 5.9 Corroboration

Does other evidence:

- support;
- contradict;
- qualify;
- or contextualise the signal?

### 5.10 Authority and Responsibility

Who is responsible for assessing the signal?

Who can decide whether attention should escalate?

## 6. The Signal-to-Attention Process

Project OI currently represents the process as:

```text
Occurrence
   ↓
Capture
   ↓
Recognition
   ↓
Qualification
   ↓
Routing
   ↓
Attention
   ↓
Contextualisation
   ↓
Orientation
```

### 6.1 Occurrence

Something changes or becomes observable.

### 6.2 Capture

The event enters a human or technical channel.

Examples:

- an employee observes it;
- a customer sends a message;
- a sensor detects it;
- a system logs it;
- an AI model identifies a pattern;
- or a metric crosses a threshold.

### 6.3 Recognition

Someone or something identifies the event as potentially meaningful.

Recognition can fail because the event:

- is not measured;
- occurs outside formal systems;
- is normalised;
- conflicts with expectations;
- or is visible only to someone with low organisational influence.

### 6.4 Qualification

The signal is assessed for materiality, urgency, confidence, novelty and relevance.

Qualification should not require complete interpretation.

Its purpose is to determine the appropriate attention pathway.

### 6.5 Routing

The signal reaches the people, systems or workflows able to evaluate it.

Routing must consider:

- expertise;
- responsibility;
- authority;
- sensitivity;
- workload;
- and conflicts of interest.

### 6.6 Attention

The signal receives actual organisational processing capacity.

A notification sent is not evidence of attention received.

### 6.7 Contextualisation

The signal is connected to:

- related evidence;
- organisational history;
- current commitments;
- previous incidents;
- customers;
- processes;
- risks;
- policies;
- and expertise.

### 6.8 Orientation

The organisation forms a usable account of what may be happening, why it matters and what should occur next.

## 7. Organisational Attention Failures

### 7.1 Blindness

The organisation has no mechanism for detecting the event.

### 7.2 Suppression

The signal is noticed but withheld because raising it is unsafe, unrewarded or politically costly.

### 7.3 Fragmentation

Different parts of the organisation possess pieces of the signal but no mechanism combines them.

### 7.4 Normalisation

Repeated anomalies become accepted as normal operating conditions.

### 7.5 Threshold Failure

The signal remains below a rigid threshold even though its context makes it material.

### 7.6 Escalation Failure

The signal is recognised locally but cannot reach appropriate authority or expertise.

### 7.7 Notification Saturation

The signal is routed into a channel already overwhelmed by low-value notifications.

### 7.8 Seniority Distortion

Attention follows hierarchy rather than evidence or expertise.

### 7.9 Metric Fixation

Measured indicators receive attention while unmeasured conditions remain invisible.

### 7.10 Premature Interpretation

The signal is assigned a familiar explanation before adequate context is gathered.

### 7.11 False Consensus

A system or meeting collapses disagreement into one summary and hides uncertainty.

### 7.12 Orphaned Attention

The organisation discusses the signal but assigns no owner, decision or next review point.

### 7.13 Attention Decay

The signal gains temporary visibility but disappears before resolution.

### 7.14 Automated Amplification

AI or automation repeatedly elevates a signal because of model design rather than material organisational relevance.

### 7.15 Algorithmic Suppression

A ranking or filtering system hides unusual but important evidence because it does not resemble prior high-priority cases.

## 8. Attention Is a Governance Question

Who or what controls organisational attention can influence:

- priorities;
- resource allocation;
- problem framing;
- accountability;
- and whose experience counts as evidence.

Attention design therefore has ethical and governance implications.

A responsible attention system should consider:

### Contestability

Can a participant challenge a priority, classification or suppression decision?

### Provenance

Can people see why the signal exists and where it came from?

### Proportionality

Does the required attention match the likely risk and value?

### Representation

Can frontline, customer and minority perspectives enter the system?

### Accountability

Who is responsible for unattended or downgraded signals?

### Transparency

Can affected users understand why something was routed, escalated or suppressed?

### Privacy

Does signal capture exceed legitimate organisational need?

### Manipulation Resistance

Can participants game priorities, urgency or visibility?

### Automation Boundaries

Which attention decisions may be automated, and which require human judgement?

## 9. SMB Context

SMBs often experience attention differently from large enterprises.

Potential strengths include:

- shorter communication paths;
- closer customer contact;
- greater contextual familiarity;
- faster informal escalation;
- and direct founder awareness.

Potential weaknesses include:

- founder bottlenecks;
- dependence on tacit knowledge;
- limited specialist capacity;
- informal records;
- weak separation of urgent and important work;
- fewer independent challenge mechanisms;
- and minimal analytical infrastructure.

The same informality that allows rapid response may also make attention fragile.

A signal may be acted on quickly when the founder is present and disappear entirely when that person is unavailable.

For an SMB, the aim should not be to reproduce enterprise reporting bureaucracy.

It should be to create lightweight structures that make material signals:

- visible;
- contextual;
- owned;
- reviewable;
- and connected to response.

## 10. Project OI Propositions

These propositions are literature-grounded hypotheses, not validated Project OI findings.

### P1 — Attention Precedes Organisational Orientation

> A signal cannot influence organisational orientation unless it enters an attention structure capable of retaining, routing and contextualising it.

### P2 — Information Quantity Is Not Attention Quality

> Increasing available information may reduce Organisational Intelligence where it increases attention demand faster than the organisation’s ability to filter and contextualise it.

### P3 — Attention Structures Shape Behaviour

> Organisational behaviour is influenced by the rules, roles, relationships and systems that determine which issues and possible responses receive attention.

### P4 — Weak Signals Require Graduated Commitment

> Weak signals are best handled through progressive attention, evidence gathering and reversible response rather than binary dismissal or full escalation.

### P5 — Dissent Is an Attention Resource

> Preserving competing interpretations can improve orientation where uncertainty is material and premature consensus would suppress useful evidence.

### P6 — Attention Requires Ownership

> Signals that receive visibility but no responsible owner, decision pathway or review point are unlikely to influence persistent organisational response.

### P7 — Expertise Should Influence Routing

> Relevant expertise, rather than hierarchy alone, should influence who evaluates and responds to a signal.

### P8 — Automation Can Create Attention Failure

> AI and automated systems may improve detection while simultaneously increasing noise, hiding uncertainty or misallocating organisational attention.

### P9 — Context Determines Priority

> Signal priority should reflect organisational context and potential consequence, not only frequency, recency or source seniority.

### P10 — Attention Must Connect Forward

> Attention contributes to Organisational Intelligence only when it can influence orientation, response or deliberate monitoring.

## 11. Observable Indicators

Potential indicators for later research include:

### Signal Capture

- material events can enter the organisation from multiple legitimate sources;
- frontline concerns can be raised;
- system anomalies are retained;
- weak signals can accumulate;
- and relevant external changes are monitored.

### Qualification

- priority reflects consequence and context;
- confidence and uncertainty are visible;
- novelty and recurrence are distinguished;
- and false urgency can be challenged.

### Routing

- responsible owners are identifiable;
- relevant expertise is locatable;
- escalation pathways are clear;
- and sensitive signals have protected channels.

### Attention

- high-priority signals receive timely review;
- notification volume is controlled;
- important signals are not repeatedly deferred;
- and attention decisions are visible.

### Contextualisation

- related evidence is linked;
- prior episodes are retrievable;
- assumptions are recorded;
- dissent is preserved;
- and source provenance is available.

### Continuity

- unresolved signals retain ownership;
- review dates are established;
- escalation can increase or decrease;
- and resolution is connected to downstream decisions and outcomes.

## 11A. Signals and Attention Quality

**Status:** Project OI final-synthesis addition introduced during the adversarial correction pass. These dimensions are candidates for inquiry and evidence design, not a validated scale.

Chapter 1 originally contained observable indicators but no standalone quality architecture. The integrated review identified eight qualities that should be assessed explicitly when making a bounded claim about Sense.[^9]

### 11A.1 Materiality Discrimination

Can the organisation distinguish potentially consequential signals from noise without requiring certainty before attention is allocated?

### 11A.2 Channel Coverage and Diversity

Can materially different signals enter through customers, employees, operations, partners, systems, external sources and affected stakeholders rather than only formal reporting channels?

### 11A.3 Timeliness

Can a signal reach attention within the period in which interpretation or response remains useful?

### 11A.4 Provenance and Attribution

Can the organisation identify where the signal came from, how it was transformed and which confidence or uncertainty attaches to it?

### 11A.5 Routing Clarity

Is responsibility visible for receiving, qualifying, escalating, dismissing or monitoring the signal?

### 11A.6 Attention Capacity and Saturation Control

Can the organisation manage signal volume, competing priorities and alert fatigue without allowing material conditions to disappear inside overload?

### 11A.7 Contestability of Suppression or Dismissal

Can an authorised or affected actor challenge the decision to ignore, downgrade or close a signal without relying solely on hierarchy or personal access?

### 11A.8 Follow-Through Into Contextualisation

Does an attended signal move into purpose-bound contextualisation, or does the organisation merely acknowledge it without creating a usable next step?

### Quality Boundary

None of these dimensions is standalone evidence of Organisational Intelligence. They support a bounded claim about Sense and should be combined with evidence that relevant signals are contextualised and used by later capabilities.

## 12. Practical Principles

### 12.1 Design for Attention, Not Maximum Visibility

Making everything visible makes nothing meaningfully visible.

### 12.2 Separate Capture From Interruption

A signal may need to be recorded without immediately interrupting someone.

### 12.3 Use Graduated Attention

Possible states include:

```text
Captured
→ Watching
→ Needs review
→ Material
→ Urgent
→ Governed response
→ Resolved
```

### 12.4 Preserve the Original Signal

Later interpretation should not overwrite the source event.

### 12.5 Attach Context Before Action

Where time permits, provide the minimum context needed to judge relevance and consequence.

### 12.6 Make Uncertainty Explicit

A low-confidence, high-consequence signal may deserve more attention than a high-confidence, low-consequence one.

### 12.7 Route to Expertise and Authority

The person who understands a signal may not be the person authorised to act.

Both relationships matter.

### 12.8 Protect Dissent and Escalation

The system should provide pathways for challenging suppression, priority and interpretation.

### 12.9 Record Attention Decisions

Where a material signal is deferred or dismissed, preserve:

- who decided;
- why;
- based on what evidence;
- and when it should be reconsidered.

### 12.10 Close the Loop

A signal should eventually connect to:

- a decision;
- a workflow;
- monitoring;
- a knowledge update;
- or an explicit determination that no further response is justified.

## 13. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Signals and attention are necessary but not sufficient.

An organisation may notice important events and still fail to:

- interpret them well;
- coordinate a response;
- learn from outcomes;
- or retain capability.

The Project OI relationship is:

```text
Signal quality
× Attention quality
× Contextualisation
= Orientation potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency: failure in any element can weaken the organisation’s ability to orient.

The higher-level principle is:

> **The intelligence of an organisation begins with what it is structurally capable of noticing, but is determined by what it can subsequently understand, coordinate and improve.**

## 15. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises multiple research traditions;
- does not establish a validated Project OI construct;
- does not prove the proposed failure taxonomy is complete;
- does not establish causal effects for the proposed indicators;
- and does not provide a validated attention assessment.

The concepts of **attentional debt**, **orphaned attention**, and the Project OI signal-to-attention lifecycle are working syntheses requiring later challenge and testing.
