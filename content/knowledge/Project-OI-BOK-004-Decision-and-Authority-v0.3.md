## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **decision and authority**: how an organisation converts context, evidence and expert judgement into a legitimate commitment that can guide coordinated action.

Its central question is:

> **How does an organisation decide what will happen, establish who may decide and act, preserve the basis of the decision, and remain accountable for what follows?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant organisational context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. and form a sufficiently useful orientation.

Orientation alone does not create organisational response.

The organisation must still:

- identify the decision;
- frame the issue;
- establish decision rights;
- generate and evaluate alternatives;
- preserve uncertainty and dissent;
- select a course of action;
- authorise execution;
- secure enough commitment for coordinated response;
- observe consequences;
- and retain what it learns.

```text
Signal and experience
        ↓
Attention
        ↓
Context
        ↓
Knowledge and expertise
        ↓
Organisational orientation
        ↓
Decision and authority
        ↓
Coordinated response
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what an organisational decision is;
- how a decision differs from an answer, recommendation, approval and action;
- how authority is allocated, delegated and exercised;
- how formal authority differs from effective control;
- how evidence, expertise, dissent and uncertainty should enter decisions;
- what makes a decision legitimate, executable, accountable and contestable;
- how decision process should be evaluated separately from outcome;
- how exceptions, escalation, urgency and reversibility affect decision governance;
- how AI may participate without manufacturing its own authority;
- and what stronger decision capability may contribute to Organisational Intelligence.

---

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **An organisational decision is not merely the production of an answer or the selection of an option. It is an attributable and authorised commitment that connects orientation to action under explicit conditions of evidence, authority, accountability and review.**

This creates several necessary distinctions:

```text
Information
        ≠
Orientation
        ≠
Recommendation
        ≠
Decision
        ≠
Authorisation
        ≠
Action
        ≠
Outcome
```

A model can produce an answer.

An expert can provide a recommendation.

A reviewer can identify a defect.

An approver can grant permission.

An authorised actor or mechanism can make a decision.

A person, team, workflow or system can execute the resulting action.

The world then produces consequences that may differ from what the organisation expected.

Conflating these stages produces recurring failures:

- AI output is treated as a decision;
- expert advice is treated as action authority;
- approval is treated as ownership;
- a meeting conclusion is treated as an executable commitment;
- a good outcome is treated as proof of a good decision process;
- or a poor outcome is treated as proof that the decision was irrational.

The Project OI position is:

> **Decision capability is the organisational capacity to establish the decision, place legitimate authority where it can use relevant knowledge, preserve evidence and dissent, commit to an executable course, and learn without rewriting the past through hindsight.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Issue

An **issue** is a condition, question, opportunity, risk or conflict that may require organisational attention and response.

An issue is not automatically a decision.

The organisation may need to determine:

- whether a decision is required;
- which decision is required;
- who owns it;
- and whether the issue should instead be monitored, investigated or resolved through an existing rule.

### 3.2 Decision

Project OI defines an **organisational decision** as:

> **An attributable commitment made by an authorised actor or mechanism that selects, approves, rejects, defers, conditions, delegates, revokes or deliberately preserves a course of action, allocation, rule or non-action for a defined organisational scope.**

The definition contains five deliberate elements.

#### Attributable

The organisation can identify:

- who or what made the decision;
- under which authority;
- and at what time.

#### Commitment

The decision changes what the organisation is expected, permitted or required to do.

#### Authorised

The decision right existed before or was validly conferred.

#### Bounded

The decision has a defined:

- scope;
- time;
- subject;
- condition;
- and consequence.

#### Action-Oriented

The decision can influence action, deliberate non-action, policy, resource allocation or future choice.

### 3.3 Choice

A **choice** is a selection among alternatives.

A choice becomes an organisational decision only where it is:

- made under legitimate authority;
- attributable;
- and connected to organisational commitment.

### 3.4 Recommendation

A **recommendation** is a proposed course of action supported by evidence, expertise, analysis or preference.

A recommendation may influence a decision.

It does not confer authority to commit the organisation.

### 3.5 Decision Right

A **decision right** is the bounded right to make a defined class of decisions for an organisational scope.

A decision right should identify:

- subject;
- holder;
- limits;
- conditions;
- required evidence;
- approvals;
- escalation;
- and revocation.

### 3.6 Formal Authority

**Formal authority** is the officially granted right to make a decision or direct action.

Aghion and Tirole describe formal authority as the right to decide.[^1]

### 3.7 Real Authority

**Real authority** is effective control over a decision in practice.

It may arise from:

- superior information;
- expertise;
- control of agenda or resources;
- urgency;
- workload;
- reputation;
- or the inability of the formal authority holder to review every case.

Formal and real authority may coincide.

They often do not.

### 3.8 Delegation

**Delegation** is the transfer of a bounded decision right or action authority from one authorised actor to another.

Delegation should specify:

- what is delegated;
- to whom;
- for which scope;
- under which thresholds;
- with which constraints;
- until when;
- and under what review and revocation conditions.

Delegation does not eliminate the organisation’s accountability for the authority design.

### 3.9 Decision Owner

A **Decision Owner** is the person or governance body accountable for ensuring that a decision is:

- properly framed;
- made under valid authority;
- connected to action;
- and reviewed where required.

The Decision Owner may be the decision-maker.

The roles may also differ.

### 3.10 Decision-Maker

A **decision-maker** is the authorised actor or mechanism that makes the specific decision.

### 3.11 Reviewer

A **Reviewer** evaluates:

- evidence;
- compliance;
- quality;
- risk;
- or fitness.

Review does not imply decision authority unless that authority is explicit.

### 3.12 Approver

An **Approver** holds a defined right to consent before a decision, action or publication becomes effective.

Approval may:

- permit;
- reject;
- condition;
- or return the matter for correction.

Approval does not necessarily make the Approver the Decision Owner.

### 3.13 Veto

A **veto** is a bounded authority to block a decision or action.

A veto should identify:

- the domain;
- trigger;
- evidence;
- and resolution path.

Unbounded veto creates hidden authority and decision congestion.

### 3.14 Authorisation

**Authorisation** is the governed conferral of permission to perform an action, use a resource, access information or exercise a decision right.

### 3.15 Action Authority

**Action authority** is the right to execute the organisational response.

The person or system authorised to act may differ from the person who decided.

### 3.16 Responsibility

**Responsibility** is an assigned duty to perform, support or ensure work.

Responsibility may be shared.

Shared responsibility should not be used to obscure decision ownership or accountability.

### 3.17 Accountability

Project OI uses **accountability** in the relational sense described by Bovens:

> an actor has an obligation to explain and justify conduct to a forum that may question, judge and impose consequences.[^2]

Accountability therefore requires:

- an actor;
- a forum;
- an obligation to explain;
- the possibility of judgement;
- and consequences or corrective response.

### 3.18 Commitment

**Commitment** is the organisational willingness or obligation to support and implement a decision.

Commitment does not require personal agreement.

It requires sufficient acceptance of the decision’s legitimacy and implementation obligations.

### 3.19 Escalation

**Escalation** is the governed transfer or referral of a matter where:

- authority is insufficient;
- risk exceeds the threshold;
- context is incomplete;
- conflict remains unresolved;
- or the current route cannot produce a legitimate decision.

Escalation is not inherently a failure.

An absent or unusable escalation path is.

### 3.20 Exception

An **exception** is an authorised and bounded deviation from a governing rule, policy, threshold or ordinary process.

An exception should preserve:

- reason;
- authority;
- scope;
- conditions;
- effective period;
- review;
- and expiry.

### 3.21 Deliberate Non-Action

**Deliberate non-action** is an attributable decision not to act at the present time.

It should still identify:

- rationale;
- owner;
- risk;
- monitoring;
- and review point.

Silence, neglect and delay are not deliberate non-action merely because nothing happened.

### 3.22 Decision Record

A **Decision Record** is the durable representation of:

- the decision;
- authority;
- material context;
- evidence;
- options;
- dissent;
- rationale;
- conditions;
- action ownership;
- and review point.

It should preserve organisational reasoning without requiring private mental process or chain-of-thought.

### 3.23 Decision-Time Context

**Decision-Time Context** is the material context that was available, believed and authorised when a decision was made.

It remains distinct from information learned later.

### 3.24 Contestability

**Contestability** is the ability of an affected or authorised actor to:

- question;
- challenge;
- appeal;
- correct;
- or request review

of a decision, its evidence, its authority or its application.

### 3.25 Reversibility

**Reversibility** is the degree to which a decision and its consequences can be undone, compensated, contained or changed at acceptable cost.

Reversibility is a property of the complete organisational effect, not only the software command.

### 3.26 Decision Quality

Decision quality has several dimensions:

- framing quality;
- evidence quality;
- process quality;
- authority quality;
- content quality;
- implementation quality;
- and outcome quality.

These dimensions are related but not interchangeable.

## 4. A Recommendation Is Not a Decision

The distinction can be represented as:

```text
Evidence and context
        ↓
Analysis or expert judgement
        ↓
Recommendation
        ↓
Decision under authority
        ↓
Authorisation
        ↓
Action
        ↓
Outcome
```

### Example

An AI system analyses customer churn and recommends a price concession.

The recommendation may be:

- well evidenced;
- commercially sensible;
- and accurately predicted.

It is not yet an organisational decision.

The organisation still needs to know:

- who may change the price;
- whether the customer contract permits it;
- whether Finance must approve;
- whether similar customers must be treated consistently;
- who will communicate the change;
- and what happens if the forecast is wrong.

### Why the Distinction Matters

When recommendation and decision are merged:

- the recommendation gains unearned authority;
- the decision-maker becomes invisible;
- accountability becomes diffuse;
- and later reviewers cannot determine whether the organisation accepted, modified or merely transmitted the advice.

### Project OI Principle

> **Every material recommendation should make its intended decision, decision authority and permitted next step visible.**

## 5. A Decision Is an Organisational Commitment

A decision is more than a statement of preference.

It changes the organisation’s expected behaviour.

### A Decision May

- allocate money;
- approve a customer exception;
- select a supplier;
- change a policy;
- accept a risk;
- authorise a release;
- reject a proposal;
- defer action;
- start a workflow;
- close an incident;
- delegate authority;
- or deliberately continue the current course.

### A Decision Should Produce

- a clear decision statement;
- a defined scope;
- an effective time;
- conditions;
- authorised action;
- ownership;
- and a review or completion point.

### Weak Decision Output

> We agreed that improving customer onboarding is important.

### Stronger Decision Output

> The Operations Director has approved a 60-day trial of the revised onboarding workflow for Australian retail customers, beginning 1 September, subject to Legal approval of the revised consent wording. The Customer Operations Lead owns implementation. Conversion, rework and complaint rates will be reviewed after 30 days. Expansion requires a new decision.

The second statement is:

- attributable;
- bounded;
- conditional;
- executable;
- and reviewable.

## 6. Decisions Are Bounded and Distributed

Herbert Simon’s bounded-rationality work rejects the assumption that decision-makers can possess complete information, unlimited computational capacity and stable knowledge of every alternative and consequence.[^3]

Organisational decisions are distributed because:

- information is incomplete;
- knowledge is specialised;
- consequences cross functions;
- authority is divided;
- and action is performed by people and systems other than the decision-maker.

Mintzberg, Raisinghani and Théorêt’s study of 25 strategic decisions also shows that consequential decisions rarely follow one neat linear sequence. They cycle through identification, development and selection, with interruptions, delays and feedback loops.[^4]

This creates two errors.

### Error 1 — Pretend the Decision Is Fully Rational

The organisation creates a formal template that implies:

- the problem was known;
- alternatives were complete;
- evidence was objective;
- and the decision followed in a clean sequence.

The record hides the actual uncertainty and iteration.

### Error 2 — Treat Ambiguity as a Reason Not to Govern

The organisation concludes that because decisions are messy, authority and evidence cannot be made explicit.

The result is informal power, lost rationale and retrospective storytelling.

### Project OI Position

> **Decision architecture should structure ambiguity without pretending to remove it.**

## 7. Authority Should Meet Knowledge, Consequence and Accountability

Decision rights should not be allocated through hierarchy alone.

Jensen and Meckling argue that knowledge relevant to all decisions cannot be transferred costlessly to one central decision-maker. Where knowledge is specific and expensive to move, decision rights may need to move closer to the person who possesses or can acquire that knowledge.[^5]

This creates a design tension.

### Centralise the Knowledge

Move information upward to the formal authority holder.

Benefits:

- consistency;
- broad organisational perspective;
- and control.

Costs:

- delay;
- information loss;
- overload;
- and weak use of local knowledge.

### Delegate the Decision

Move authority closer to the knowledge.

Benefits:

- speed;
- initiative;
- and contextual fit.

Costs:

- local optimisation;
- inconsistent treatment;
- agency risk;
- and weak coordination across consequences.

### The Decision-Rights Problem

The organisation must balance:

```text
Relevant knowledge
× consequence ownership
× legitimate authority
× accountability capacity
```

This is not a mathematical formula.

It expresses the design requirement.

### Practical Rule

Decision rights should normally sit where the organisation can achieve the best combination of:

- relevant and timely knowledge;
- understanding of consequences;
- aligned incentives;
- appropriate authority;
- and effective accountability.

## 8. Formal Authority and Real Authority Diverge

Aghion and Tirole distinguish formal authority from real authority.[^1]

The person with the official right to decide may not control the decision in practice.

Real authority may move to:

- the expert who controls information;
- the employee who frames the options;
- the system that filters what becomes visible;
- the person who controls the agenda;
- the approver who can delay indefinitely;
- or the AI system whose recommendation becomes the default.

### Example — Formal Manager, Real Model

A manager has formal authority to approve a refund.

The interface presents:

- one AI recommendation;
- one confidence score;
- and one prominent “Approve” button.

The evidence is hidden behind several clicks.

The manager technically decides.

The model and interface exercise much of the real authority.

### Shadow Authority

Project OI uses **shadow authority** as a working term for effective control over a decision that is not represented in the formal authority model.

Examples:

- an assistant who controls the executive’s brief;
- a technical specialist whose approval is not formally required but is always decisive;
- a founder whose informal consent is sought for every exception;
- or an algorithmic score that nobody is willing to override.

### Design Requirement

The organisation should compare:

```text
Who is formally authorised?
Who frames the issue?
Who controls the evidence?
Who generates the options?
Who can block?
Who can delay?
Who performs the action?
Who bears the consequence?
Who is held accountable?
```

Where these relationships differ, the authority design should be visible.

## 9. The Decision and Authority Chain

Project OI proposes the following working chain.

```text
Recognise the decision
        ↓
Frame the issue and scope
        ↓
Establish decision rights and constraints
        ↓
Assemble context, evidence and expertise
        ↓
Generate alternatives and criteria
        ↓
Surface uncertainty, dissent and affected interests
        ↓
Select a proportionate decision route
        ↓
Make and record the decision
        ↓
Authorise and coordinate action
        ↓
Observe consequences
        ↓
Review process, outcome and learning
```

### 9.1 Recognition

Determine whether a decision exists.

A decision may be hidden inside:

- an approval request;
- a problem statement;
- a status meeting;
- a recommendation;
- a budget discussion;
- or an unexamined default.

### 9.2 Framing

Define:

- the decision question;
- scope;
- time;
- affected parties;
- outcome sought;
- and what is outside the decision.

### 9.3 Authority

Establish:

- Decision Owner;
- decision-maker;
- approvers;
- reviewers;
- vetoes;
- action owner;
- and escalation.

### 9.4 Context and Evidence

Assemble:

- current state;
- history;
- sources;
- expertise;
- uncertainty;
- and constraints.

### 9.5 Alternatives

Include:

- realistic options;
- deliberate non-action;
- reversible experiments;
- conditional decisions;
- and staged commitments.

### 9.6 Dissent and Interests

Surface:

- unique information;
- competing interpretations;
- risks;
- affected stakeholders;
- and minority views.

### 9.7 Decision Route

Match governance to:

- risk;
- novelty;
- reversibility;
- urgency;
- uncertainty;
- and authority.

### 9.8 Decision

Make the commitment explicitly.

### 9.9 Action

Authorise:

- owner;
- resources;
- dependencies;
- communication;
- stop conditions;
- and review.

### 9.10 Consequences

Observe:

- intended outcomes;
- side effects;
- exceptions;
- and emerging evidence.

### 9.11 Review

Evaluate:

- decision process;
- implementation;
- outcome;
- assumptions;
- and future changes.

## 10. Theoretical Foundations

### 10.1 Bounded Rationality

Simon’s model of bounded rationality establishes that real decision-makers simplify, search selectively and often satisfice rather than optimise.[^3]

The Project OI implication is not that rigour is pointless.

It is that decision systems should make the bounds visible:

- missing information;
- time limits;
- attention;
- model limits;
- assumptions;
- and stopping rules.

### 10.2 Organisational Decision Process

Mintzberg and colleagues identify a dynamic structure in apparently unstructured strategic decisions, including recognition, diagnosis, search, design, evaluation, authorisation and interruption.[^4]

This supports explicit process without assuming one universal sequence.

### 10.3 Organisation as Information Processing

Galbraith’s information-processing view connects task uncertainty to organisational design.[^6]

As uncertainty increases, organisations can:

- reduce information-processing needs;
- increase information-processing capacity;
- create lateral relationships;
- or change authority and structure.

Decision architecture is therefore organisational design.

### 10.4 Decision Rights and Specific Knowledge

Jensen and Meckling argue that decision rights and relevant knowledge must be brought together while controlling the agency problems created by delegation.[^5]

This supports bounded delegation rather than either universal centralisation or unrestricted autonomy.

### 10.5 Formal and Real Authority

Aghion and Tirole show that formal allocation of authority affects information acquisition, initiative, communication and real control.[^1]

Authority design changes behaviour before any specific decision is made.

### 10.6 Decision Process and Effectiveness

Dean and Sharfman’s longitudinal study of 52 strategic decisions in 24 companies finds that process matters: procedural rationality and political behaviour relate to decision success after controlling for environmental favourability and implementation.[^7]

Decision quality is not determined only by the external environment.

### 10.7 Speed Under High Velocity

Eisenhardt’s study of eight microcomputer firms finds that fast decision-makers used more real-time information, considered more alternatives and used active conflict resolution rather than simply analysing less.[^8]

Speed can result from process design rather than premature closure.

### 10.8 Hidden Information

Stasser and Titus demonstrate that group discussion can favour information already shared by members, preventing unique information from being pooled effectively.[^9]

Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch’s meta-analysis of 72 studies similarly finds that information sharing predicts performance, cohesion, decision satisfaction and knowledge integration, while the usefulness of the information matters more than sheer openness.[^10]

A meeting does not guarantee that the organisation used the information it possessed.

### 10.9 Cognitive and Affective Conflict

Amason distinguishes task-focused cognitive conflict from relationship-focused affective conflict and finds different effects on decision quality, consensus and acceptance.[^11]

Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan find that dialectical inquiry and devil’s advocacy can improve recommendation and assumption quality relative to consensus-only approaches, although consensus may produce greater satisfaction and acceptance.[^12]

Dissent requires structure.

### 10.10 Voice and Psychological Safety

Morrison’s reviews show that employees may withhold suggestions, concerns and problem information even where the organisation would benefit from hearing them.[^13]

Edmondson’s study of 51 work teams links psychological safety to learning behaviour and performance through greater willingness to take interpersonal risks such as speaking up and discussing error.[^14]

Decision rights are incomplete where relevant evidence cannot travel upward.

### 10.11 Legitimacy and Procedural Justice

Tyler defines legitimacy as the belief that an authority or arrangement is appropriate, proper and just, creating voluntary deference beyond coercion or reward.[^15]

Decision legitimacy therefore affects:

- acceptance;
- implementation;
- and resilience under disagreement.

### 10.12 Accountability

Bovens frames accountability as an actor–forum relationship involving explanation, questioning, judgement and consequences.[^2]

Lerner and Tetlock’s review shows that accountability does not have one universal effect. It can increase cognitive complexity and reduce bias, but may also produce conformity or defensive reasoning depending on timing, audience and expectations.[^16]

Siegel-Jacobs and Yates distinguish accountability for process from accountability for outcomes.[^17]

Accountability must be designed, not merely demanded.

### 10.13 Escalation of Commitment

Staw shows how decision-makers can increase commitment to a failing course in an effort to justify or recover prior investment.[^18]

Decision review therefore requires explicit stop conditions and independence from the original commitment where appropriate.

### 10.14 Outcome Bias

Baron and Hershey show that people evaluate identical decision processes more favourably when outcomes are good and less favourably when outcomes are poor.[^19]

The organisation must assess what was known at the time, not only what happened later.

## 11. Framing the Decision

The way a decision is framed determines:

- which evidence appears relevant;
- which alternatives are considered;
- who seems authoritative;
- and what counts as success.

### Weak Frame

> Should we replace the supplier?

This assumes that supplier replacement is the focal choice.

### Stronger Frame

> What response should the organisation take to the third quality incident, given contractual obligations, continuity risk, customer impact and the unresolved root cause?

The stronger frame allows:

- remediation;
- suspension;
- replacement;
- monitoring;
- dual sourcing;
- and staged action.

### Framing Failures

- solution-first framing;
- symptom treated as cause;
- decision scope too narrow;
- affected stakeholders omitted;
- time horizon hidden;
- risk framed only financially;
- or one department’s vocabulary defining the issue.

### Nutt’s Decision Formulation Work

Nutt identifies several formulation processes, including issue, objective-directed and reframing approaches, and finds that formulation choices relate to adoption, merit and duration.[^20]

### Framing Practice

A decision brief should state:

```text
Decision question
Why a decision is required
Current situation
Scope
Decision owner
Outcome sought
Constraints
Affected parties
Time horizon
Known uncertainty
What is not being decided
```

## 12. Decision Rights and Delegation

A decision-right model should be specific enough to prevent ambiguity and simple enough to use.

### 12.1 Decision Class

What type of decision is being delegated?

Examples:

- customer refund;
- supplier suspension;
- hiring;
- release approval;
- pricing;
- data access;
- incident response;
- or AI action.

### 12.2 Scope

Which:

- product;
- customer;
- jurisdiction;
- team;
- system;
- amount;
- or risk class

does the right cover?

### 12.3 Thresholds

What limits apply?

Examples:

- financial value;
- reversibility;
- customer impact;
- data sensitivity;
- contractual consequence;
- safety;
- or public visibility.

### 12.4 Required Evidence

What must be present?

### 12.5 Required Review or Approval

Which other actors must contribute?

### 12.6 Exceptions

Which cases are excluded?

### 12.7 Escalation

What happens when the authority is insufficient?

### 12.8 Duration and Revocation

When does the delegation expire or require review?

### Delegation Guardrail

> **Delegation should transfer the decision right, not ambiguity about who remains accountable for the authority design.**

## 13. Evidence, Unique Information and Decision Support

A decision can be supported by many sources and still omit the one fact that changes the answer.

### Shared Information Bias

Groups tend to discuss information that:

- several people already know;
- confirms the emerging preference;
- or can be explained quickly.

Unique information remains with the person who holds it.

### Structured Elicitation

Decision systems should consider:

- independent input before group discussion;
- source-specific evidence collection;
- explicit “what do you know that others may not?” prompts;
- minority reports;
- domain-by-domain review;
- and delayed disclosure of initial preferences where anchoring risk is high.

### Evidence Roles

Evidence may:

- establish fact;
- estimate probability;
- define constraint;
- expose uncertainty;
- challenge an option;
- or indicate that no decision is yet justified.

### Source Diversity

More sources are not automatically better.

The decision should preserve:

- relevance;
- authority;
- independence;
- time;
- and limitations.

### Project OI Principle

> **Decision support should increase the availability of decision-relevant evidence, not merely increase the volume of material visible to the decision-maker.**

## 14. Dissent, Voice and Psychological Safety

A decision process needs challenge without turning disagreement into interpersonal conflict.

### Dissent Has Several Functions

- exposes unique information;
- tests assumptions;
- identifies affected interests;
- creates alternatives;
- reveals implementation barriers;
- and reduces false consensus.

### Dissent Can Also Fail

- become personal;
- delay action indefinitely;
- repeat positions without evidence;
- or become a performance of challenge with no ability to affect the decision.

### Voice Is Not the Same as Authority

People may have the right to:

- contribute;
- challenge;
- appeal;
- or review

without holding the final decision right.

### Psychological Safety Is Not Comfort

Psychological safety means that speaking up, admitting uncertainty and identifying error is interpersonally possible.

It does not mean:

- every view is correct;
- every challenge wins;
- or accountability disappears.

### Structured Challenge Methods

Possible methods include:

- devil’s advocacy;
- dialectical inquiry;
- premortem;
- independent red-team review;
- minority report;
- explicit disconfirming evidence;
- and “what would change our decision?” criteria.

### Decision Commitment After Dissent

A team can disagree during deliberation and still commit after a legitimate decision.

The process should distinguish:

```text
Right to contribute
Right to challenge
Right to decide
Obligation to support implementation
Right to appeal or reopen
```

## 15. Speed, Uncertainty and Reversibility

Fast decision-making is not the absence of process.

Eisenhardt’s work suggests that speed may come from:

- current information;
- multiple alternatives;
- clear priorities;
- active conflict resolution;
- and integration with execution.[^8]

### Decision Speed Should Match

- consequence;
- reversibility;
- uncertainty;
- information decay;
- opportunity cost;
- and escalation cost.

### Reversible Decisions

A reversible decision may use:

- bounded trial;
- small exposure;
- monitoring;
- and rapid review.

### Difficult-to-Reverse Decisions

Require stronger:

- evidence;
- authority;
- independent challenge;
- consequence analysis;
- and implementation planning.

### Urgency

Urgency may justify:

- temporary authority;
- shorter review;
- or emergency action.

It should not justify:

- hiding the decision-maker;
- erasing evidence;
- or making temporary authority permanent.

### Information-Generating Decisions

Under uncertainty, the best decision may be a reversible action designed to produce information.

Examples:

- pilot;
- limited rollout;
- parallel test;
- or staged commitment.

## 16. Legitimacy, Participation and Fair Process

People may support a decision they disagree with where they believe:

- the right authority decided;
- relevant evidence was considered;
- they or affected parties could contribute;
- the process was consistent;
- and the rationale was explained.

### Participation Has Different Meanings

- informed;
- consulted;
- contributor;
- reviewer;
- approver;
- co-decision-maker;
- consent holder;
- or veto holder.

Using the word “involved” hides these distinctions.

### Legitimacy Does Not Require Consensus

A legitimate decision can preserve disagreement.

It should explain:

- who decided;
- why;
- under which authority;
- which evidence mattered;
- which dissent remained;
- and how the decision may be reviewed.

### Implementation Benefit

Legitimacy can improve voluntary compliance and reduce the need for coercive control.[^15]

### Guardrail

> **Participation should be designed around information, affected interests, legitimacy and implementation—not added as ceremony after the decision is effectively complete.**

---

## 17. Accountability

Accountability operates before, during and after the decision.

### 17.1 Prospective Accountability

Before deciding, the actor knows:

- who will review;
- which criteria apply;
- and what must be explained.

This may improve care.

It may also produce conformity where the expected audience preference is known.[^16]

### 17.2 Process Accountability

The actor must justify:

- evidence;
- alternatives;
- method;
- uncertainty;
- and authority.

### 17.3 Outcome Accountability

The actor is evaluated against consequences.

Outcome accountability matters.

It is dangerous when it causes the organisation to punish reasonable decisions made under uncertainty because the result was poor.

### 17.4 Accountability Forum

The forum may be:

- manager;
- board;
- regulator;
- customer;
- review panel;
- team;
- or public.

Different forums require different evidence and may produce conflicting accountability pressures.

### 17.5 Consequences

Consequences may include:

- correction;
- learning;
- remediation;
- authority change;
- disciplinary response;
- or policy revision.

### 17.6 Accountability Versus Blame

Blame seeks a person to absorb the negative outcome.

Accountability seeks explanation, judgement, correction and learning.

The two may overlap.

They are not identical.

---

## 18. Decision Quality Is Not Outcome Quality

A good decision can produce a poor outcome.

A poor decision can produce a good outcome.

Uncertainty, external action and luck intervene.

### Four Separate Questions

#### Process Quality

Was the decision framed and governed appropriately?

#### Decision Quality

Was the chosen commitment reasonable given what was known?

#### Implementation Quality

Was the decision carried out as intended?

#### Outcome Quality

What consequences occurred?

### Outcome Bias

Knowing the outcome can make earlier evidence appear more obvious than it was.[^19]

### Decision Review Should Compare

- predictions;
- assumptions;
- confidence;
- expected consequences;
- actual implementation;
- and actual outcome.

### Decision-Time Reconstruction

The review should preserve:

```text
What was known?
What was believed?
What remained uncertain?
Which options existed?
Who held authority?
Why was this option selected?
What happened later?
```

### Guardrail

> **Do not rewrite the quality of a past decision solely through the outcome that followed.**

---

## 19. Decision, Commitment and Action

A decision can be valid and still fail to produce action.

### The Decision–Action Gap

Common causes include:

- unclear action owner;
- insufficient resources;
- hidden dependencies;
- disagreement after the decision;
- missing authorisation;
- no effective date;
- or no review point.

### A Complete Decision Output Should Include

```text
Decision
Decision owner
Decision-maker
Authority
Effective time
Scope
Conditions
Action owner
Required resources
Dependencies
Evidence
Dissent
Stop conditions
Review point
Communication
```

### Commitment Is Not Agreement

People may disagree and still support implementation where:

- the decision process was legitimate;
- obligations are clear;
- and appeal or review remains possible.

### Decision Drift

Decision drift occurs when implementation gradually changes the meaning of the decision without an explicit new decision.

Examples:

- trial becomes permanent;
- exception becomes standard practice;
- scope expands;
- or conditions are forgotten.

## 20. Exceptions, Escalation and Veto

### Exceptions

An exception should not silently rewrite the general rule.

It should preserve:

- reason;
- scope;
- authority;
- expiry;
- and whether the underlying policy needs review.

### Escalation

A healthy escalation path identifies:

- trigger;
- destination;
- required context;
- expected response time;
- temporary authority;
- and what work may continue.

### Veto

Veto rights are useful where:

- safety;
- security;
- legal;
- financial;
- or ethical constraints

require an independent block.

Veto rights become harmful when:

- scope is vague;
- response time is undefined;
- or the veto holder can block without explaining the basis.

### Approval Congestion

Too many approval layers may:

- reduce ownership;
- delay response;
- increase rubber stamping;
- and create formal authority without real review.

### Project OI Principle

> **Controls should preserve legitimate challenge and risk containment without turning every decision into an approval queue.**

## 21. AI in Organisational Decisions

AI can support decision-making through:

- retrieval;
- synthesis;
- prediction;
- option generation;
- scenario analysis;
- anomaly detection;
- recommendation;
- challenge;
- and record preparation.

AI can also influence decisions through:

- framing;
- ranking;
- defaults;
- confidence presentation;
- and interface prominence.

This means AI may exercise real authority without possessing formal authority.

### AI Cannot Create Its Own Authority

Any AI decision or action authority must originate from:

- policy;
- delegation;
- role;
- or an authorised workflow.

The system cannot expand its own scope because it predicts that the expansion would be useful.

### Algorithm Aversion and Appreciation

People may reject useful algorithmic advice after observing error, as shown by Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey.[^21]

Other experiments find that people can give more weight to identical advice when it is described as algorithmic, particularly among non-experts.[^22]

The organisational problem is therefore not simply trust or distrust.

It is **calibrated reliance**.

### Overreliance

Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos find that people can overrely on incorrect AI advice and that explanations alone may not solve the problem. Cognitive-forcing interventions reduced overreliance, but also reduced user satisfaction.[^23]

Convenience and decision quality may conflict.

### Human Oversight

“Human in the loop” is insufficient.

The organisation should identify:

- which human;
- with what expertise;
- at which stage;
- with which evidence;
- under which authority;
- and with what ability to change the result.

### NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework treats AI risk as an organisational lifecycle responsibility organised around Govern, Map, Measure and Manage.[^24]

The Project OI implication is that AI decision participation must be governed as part of the organisation’s decision architecture, not as an isolated model feature.

## 22. Human–AI Decision Modes

Project OI proposes the following working modes.

They are descriptive, not an approved universal governance model.

### Mode 0 — No AI Participation

The decision uses human and deterministic system inputs only.

### Mode 1 — Information Assistance

AI retrieves, organises or summarises material.

AI holds no decision right.

### Mode 2 — Recommendation

AI proposes an option.

An authorised human or governance body decides.

### Mode 3 — Challenge or Validation

AI tests:

- assumptions;
- consistency;
- policy fit;
- or missing evidence.

AI may block only where an explicit deterministic or delegated veto exists.

### Mode 4 — Bounded Automated Determination

The organisation delegates a narrow class of decision to a system under:

- explicit rules;
- thresholds;
- evidence;
- logging;
- exception;
- and appeal.

The organisation remains accountable for the delegation.

### Mode 5 — Authorised Action Execution

AI or automation executes a prior decision within explicit action authority.

### Mode 6 — Bounded Adaptive Decision and Action

An AI system selects and performs an action within an authorised policy envelope, budget, tool scope and stop conditions.

This mode requires the strongest:

- monitoring;
- traceability;
- contestability;
- rollback or compensation;
- and governance.

### Mode Guardrail

> **Increasing model capability does not automatically move a use case to a higher authority mode. Authority changes require organisational decisions.**

## 23. AI Decision Guardrails

### 23.1 Separate Source, Inference and Recommendation

The user should know whether the output is:

- retrieved fact;
- generated synthesis;
- prediction;
- recommendation;
- or authorised decision.

### 23.2 Preserve Model and Context Identity

Consequential use should preserve:

- model;
- version;
- relevant configuration;
- sources;
- time;
- and output.

### 23.3 Make Delegation Explicit

The system should identify:

- permitted decisions;
- thresholds;
- prohibited actions;
- approval;
- and escalation.

### 23.4 Support Contest and Appeal

Affected actors should have an appropriate path to:

- question;
- correct;
- or request review

of high-impact AI-supported or AI-made determinations.

### 23.5 Do Not Confuse Explanation With Oversight

A generated explanation may increase confidence without improving correctness.

### 23.6 Protect Independent Judgement

Where anchoring risk is high, the design may require:

- human assessment before AI advice;
- hidden recommendation until evidence review;
- or explicit counterargument.

### 23.7 Detect Silent Provider or Model Change

A provider change should not silently alter a decision policy.

### 23.8 Preserve Organisational Accountability

The organisation should not attribute responsibility to the model as a substitute for identifying:

- decision owner;
- system owner;
- policy owner;
- and action owner.

## 24. Decision and Authority Failure Modes

### 24.1 Decision Ambiguity

Nobody can state what decision is required.

### 24.2 Recommendation Substitution

A recommendation is treated as a decision.

### 24.3 Approval Substitution

Obtaining approval is treated as owning the decision.

### 24.4 Action Without Decision

A consequential action occurs without attributable commitment.

### 24.5 Wrong-Problem Framing

The decision answers a narrower or different question.

### 24.6 Authority Ambiguity

Several people believe another person owns the decision.

### 24.7 Shadow Authority

Real control sits outside the formal authority model.

### 24.8 Authority Without Knowledge

The decision-maker lacks access to relevant context or expertise.

### 24.9 Knowledge Without Authority

Relevant expert input cannot influence the decision.

### 24.10 Overcentralisation

Too many decisions move upward, creating overload and context loss.

### 24.11 Overdelegation

Authority is transferred without adequate controls, evidence or coordination.

### 24.12 Approval Congestion

Excess approvals create delay and rubber stamping.

### 24.13 Hidden Veto

A stakeholder can block but is not represented in the process.

### 24.14 Rubber-Stamp Review

The reviewer cannot realistically challenge the recommendation.

### 24.15 Hidden-Profile Failure

Unique information never enters deliberation.

### 24.16 Consensus Substitution

Agreement is treated as evidence of decision quality.

### 24.17 Suppressed Dissent

People with material concerns remain silent.

### 24.18 Affective Conflict

Challenge becomes interpersonal and degrades information use.

### 24.19 Analysis Paralysis

The organisation avoids commitment by continually seeking more evidence.

### 24.20 Premature Closure

The first acceptable option becomes the decision.

### 24.21 Urgency Laundering

A claimed emergency bypasses authority or evidence without later review.

### 24.22 Reversibility Blindness

A difficult-to-reverse action receives the same governance as a low-cost trial.

### 24.23 Exception Normalisation

Repeated exceptions silently become policy.

### 24.24 Escalation Failure

The current actor lacks authority and no usable route exists.

### 24.25 Decision Drift

Implementation changes the decision without a new decision.

### 24.26 Accountability Diffusion

Many participants exist, but no actor must explain and justify the result.

### 24.27 Outcome Bias

A good or bad outcome rewrites evaluation of the original process.

### 24.28 Escalation of Commitment

Prior investment causes continued support for a failing course.

### 24.29 Retrospective Reconstruction

The decision record is rewritten using later knowledge.

### 24.30 Rationale Loss

The organisation remembers what it decided but not why.

### 24.31 AI Authority Laundering

AI output is presented as an organisational decision.

### 24.32 Automation Bias

The human accepts AI advice without adequate independent judgement.

### 24.33 Algorithm Aversion

The organisation rejects useful system advice because it observed or remembers an error.

### 24.34 Explanation Theatre

A generated explanation creates confidence without meaningful review.

### 24.35 Human-Oversight Theatre

A nominal human reviewer lacks time, context, expertise or power.

### 24.36 Model-Change Drift

A model or provider changes while the decision policy appears unchanged.

### 24.37 Appeal Failure

Affected actors cannot challenge a consequential determination.

### 24.38 Decision Without Learning

The outcome never improves future authority, policy or judgement.

## 26. The Decision and Authority Process

Project OI proposes the following working process.

```text
Define the decision
        ↓
Identify decision owner and authority
        ↓
Set scope, criteria and constraints
        ↓
Assemble context, evidence and expertise
        ↓
Generate alternatives, including non-action
        ↓
Elicit dissent and affected interests
        ↓
Assess risk, reversibility and decision route
        ↓
Make and record the decision
        ↓
Authorise and coordinate action
        ↓
Monitor implementation and consequence
        ↓
Review process separately from outcome
        ↓
Update authority, policy and memory
```

### 26.1 Define

State the decision in one sentence.

### 26.2 Establish Authority

Identify:

- Decision Owner;
- decision-maker;
- reviewers;
- approvers;
- veto;
- action owner;
- and escalation.

### 26.3 Set Criteria

Define:

- objectives;
- constraints;
- thresholds;
- and success measures.

### 26.4 Assemble

Use OI-BOK-002 and OI-BOK-003 principles for context and expertise.

### 26.5 Generate

Include:

- alternatives;
- no action;
- staged commitment;
- and information-generating options.

### 26.6 Challenge

Surface:

- unique information;
- assumptions;
- dissent;
- and affected interests.

### 26.7 Select Route

Choose governance appropriate to the decision class.

### 26.8 Decide

Record the commitment and rationale.

### 26.9 Authorise and Coordinate

Translate the decision into:

- action;
- owner;
- resource;
- communication;
- and monitoring.

### 26.10 Monitor

Observe implementation and consequence.

### 26.11 Review

Separate:

- process;
- decision;
- implementation;
- and outcome.

### 26.12 Improve

Update:

- decision rights;
- policy;
- thresholds;
- context;
- and organisational memory.

## 27. Proportionate Decision Governance

### 27.1 Routine and Reversible

Examples:

- standard low-value refund;
- ordinary scheduling;
- approved operational adjustment.

Approach:

- delegated authority;
- current guidance;
- light evidence;
- and audit sampling.

### 27.2 Operational Exception

Examples:

- customer exception;
- supplier deviation;
- non-standard expense.

Approach:

- explicit exception authority;
- evidence;
- rationale;
- expiry;
- and review.

### 27.3 Material Cross-Functional Decision

Examples:

- supplier suspension;
- pricing change;
- significant release;
- organisational policy.

Approach:

- multi-domain evidence;
- decision owner;
- structured dissent;
- approval where required;
- and implementation plan.

### 27.4 High-Risk or Difficult-to-Reverse

Examples:

- safety;
- legal commitment;
- major investment;
- sensitive data use;
- public action.

Approach:

- strongest evidence;
- independent challenge;
- explicit authority;
- decision-time record;
- and review or appeal.

### 27.5 Emergency

Examples:

- live security incident;
- safety threat;
- material operational outage.

Approach:

- temporary emergency authority;
- bounded action;
- contemporaneous evidence;
- later review;
- and automatic expiry.

### 27.6 AI-Delegated Decision

Approach depends on:

- impact;
- confidence;
- evidence;
- exception handling;
- appeal;
- monitoring;
- and authority scope.

No AI mode should be classified as low risk merely because it is automated.

## 28. SMB Context

SMBs often make decisions quickly because:

- people are close to the issue;
- authority is concentrated;
- and communication paths are short.

This can create high practical intelligence.

It can also produce fragility.

### Common Strengths

- fast access to context;
- founder understanding;
- low coordination overhead;
- adaptable decisions;
- and direct customer knowledge.

### Common Weaknesses

- founder bottleneck;
- implicit decision rights;
- exceptions held in memory;
- unclear delegation;
- approval by interruption;
- limited rationale capture;
- and decisions that are difficult to reconstruct.

### Lightweight SMB Decision Practices

For material decisions, record:

```text
Decision:
Why now:
Decision owner:
Authority:
Evidence:
Options:
Key uncertainty:
Dissent:
Decision:
Action owner:
Effective date:
Conditions:
Review date:
```

### Lightweight Authority Map

The business may define:

- what employees can decide;
- what managers can approve;
- what must reach the founder;
- what can be handled through policy;
- and what triggers emergency escalation.

### Founder Guardrail

The objective is not to remove the founder from decisions.

It is to prevent every ambiguous case from depending on unrecorded founder memory and implicit consent.

## 29. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — An Answer Is Not a Decision

> An answer contributes to organisational response only where an authorised actor or mechanism converts it into a bounded commitment.

### P2 — Decisions Require Attribution

> Material decisions that cannot be attributed to an authority holder will produce weak accountability and historical reconstruction.

### P3 — Formal and Real Authority Differ

> Effective decision control will often reside with actors, information systems or defaults outside the formal authority model.

### P4 — Decision Rights Should Meet Knowledge

> Decision rights should be placed where relevant knowledge can be used at acceptable transfer and control cost.

### P5 — Authority Must Remain Bounded

> Delegation without scope, threshold, evidence, escalation and review will create hidden or inconsistent authority.

### P6 — Decision Process Matters

> Decision effectiveness depends partly on framing, evidence, procedural rationality, political behaviour and implementation, not only the external environment.

### P7 — Unique Information Requires Structure

> Group discussion alone will not reliably surface decision-relevant information held by only one or a few participants.

### P8 — Dissent Can Improve Decisions

> Task-focused dissent can improve assumption and option quality where psychological safety, competence and integration are sufficient.

### P9 — Consensus Is Not Decision Quality

> Agreement may improve acceptance while concealing missing evidence or weak challenge.

### P10 — Speed Need Not Mean Less Evidence

> Fast decisions can use more current information and alternatives where authority and process are well designed.

### P11 — Reversibility Should Shape Governance

> Decision process, evidence and approval should be proportionate to the difficulty of reversing the complete organisational effect.

### P12 — Legitimacy Supports Commitment

> People will be more likely to implement decisions they view as appropriately authorised and procedurally fair, even where they disagree with the outcome.

### P13 — Accountability Design Is Contingent

> Accountability can improve judgement or create conformity and defensive behaviour depending on timing, audience and evaluation criteria.

### P14 — Outcome Does Not Prove Process Quality

> Decision review should distinguish reasonable judgement under uncertainty from luck, implementation and later information.

### P15 — Exceptions Must Remain Exceptional

> An exception without scope, authority, time and expiry will drift into invisible policy.

### P16 — Escalation Is a Capability

> A clear escalation route allows local authority to remain bounded without forcing universal centralisation.

### P17 — AI Cannot Create Authority

> AI decision and action rights must originate from explicit organisational delegation and cannot expand through model capability alone.

### P18 — Human Oversight Must Be Specific

> Human oversight is meaningful only where the reviewer has relevant context, expertise, time, authority and the ability to alter the result.

### P19 — Consequential AI Needs Decision-Time Traceability

> The organisation should preserve the model, context, evidence, delegation and human intervention relevant to consequential AI-supported decisions.

### P20 — Review Should Improve Decision Architecture

> Decision outcomes should update authority, policy, thresholds, evidence requirements and future organisational context.

## 31. Practical Principles

### 31.1 Name the Decision

Do not let a decision remain hidden inside a meeting, recommendation or task.

### 31.2 Name the Authority

Identify who may decide, approve, veto, act and review.

### 31.3 Separate Knowledge From Authority

The expert informs.

The authorised actor decides.

### 31.4 Frame Before Evaluating

Do not optimise the first question without checking whether it is the right question.

### 31.5 Preserve Decision-Time Context

Record what was known, believed and uncertain at the time.

### 31.6 Elicit Unique Information

Do not assume open discussion will surface it.

### 31.7 Generate Real Alternatives

Include deliberate non-action, staged action and reversible experiments.

### 31.8 Design Dissent

Create challenge that is evidence-bearing and task-focused.

### 31.9 Match Governance to Reversibility

Use lighter process for bounded reversible choices and stronger process for difficult-to-reverse effects.

### 31.10 Do Not Use Urgency to Hide Authority

Emergency action should still be attributable, bounded and reviewed.

### 31.11 Make Exceptions Expire

An exception should not become invisible policy.

### 31.12 Make Decisions Executable

Every material decision needs action ownership, conditions and review.

### 31.13 Separate Process From Outcome

Do not reward luck or punish reasonable uncertainty through hindsight.

### 31.14 Design Accountability

Specify forum, criteria, timing and consequence.

### 31.15 Treat AI as a Governed Participant

State whether AI informs, recommends, validates, determines or acts.

### 31.16 Make Human Oversight Real

Give the human relevant evidence, time, expertise and power.

### 31.17 Preserve Contestability

Allow appropriate challenge, correction and appeal.

### 31.18 Learn Into the System

Use outcomes to improve:

- decision rights;
- policy;
- context;
- and future workflows.

## 32. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Decision and authority sit at the boundary between **Orient** and **Coordinate**.

Orientation creates a usable organisational view.

Decision converts that view into commitment.

Authority makes the commitment legitimate and actionable.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Orientation quality
× authority clarity
× decision legitimacy
× executability
= coordinated response potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Orientation Without Decision

```text
The organisation understands the issue
→ nobody commits
→ the condition persists
```

### Decision Without Authority

```text
A preferred action is selected
→ nobody may commit the organisation
→ implementation stalls or becomes informal
```

### Authority Without Knowledge

```text
The decision-maker can act
→ relevant context and expertise are missing
→ the organisation commits confidently to a weak response
```

### Decision Without Action

```text
The commitment is valid
→ ownership and resources are absent
→ nothing changes
```

### Action Without Learning

```text
The organisation responds
→ outcome is not connected to the decision
→ future decision capability remains unchanged
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when it can convert a grounded orientation into a legitimate and executable decision, preserve the basis and limits of that decision, and use consequences to improve how authority and judgement operate next time.**

## 33. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises decision science, organisation theory, governance, team research and emerging human–AI work;
- does not establish a validated Project OI decision model;
- does not prove that the proposed Decision and Authority Chain is complete;
- does not provide a universal method for allocating decision rights;
- and does not resolve every legal, ethical or cultural meaning of authority and accountability.

### Decision Diversity

Routine operational choices, strategic decisions, emergency decisions, legal determinations and moral decisions differ substantially.

### Level of Analysis

Individual judgement, team deliberation, organisational authority and institutional legitimacy are related but not interchangeable.

### Power

Formal models of authority may underrepresent:

- coercion;
- dependency;
- politics;
- identity;
- and structural inequality.

### Normative Criteria

A well-governed decision process can still serve an unethical organisational purpose.

### Process and Outcome

Empirical links among process, implementation and outcome are difficult to separate from environmental conditions and selection effects.

### Cultural and Legal Variation

Participation, hierarchy, consent, privacy, employment authority and accountability differ across jurisdictions and cultures.

### AI Evidence

Research on algorithm reliance, explanation and overreliance uses specific systems, tasks and populations. Model capability and interface design continue to change.

### Automation and Agency

Whether an AI system should be described as “making a decision” is partly a legal, philosophical and technical question. Project OI uses the term only where the organisation has delegated a bounded determination and remains accountable for the design.

### Measurement

Decision quality cannot be reduced safely to one score.

## 36. Editorial Guardrail

> **Project OI must not confuse a confident answer, expert recommendation, approval click or automated output with an organisational decision. A decision contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where authority, evidence, dissent, commitment, action, accountability and learning remain connected.**
