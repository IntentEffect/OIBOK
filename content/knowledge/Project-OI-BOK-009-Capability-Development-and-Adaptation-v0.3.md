## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **capability development and adaptation**: how organisational learning becomes durable organisational capacity, how capabilities are formed, distributed, exercised, maintained and retired, and how an organisation reconfigures those capabilities as its purpose, environment and operating conditions change.

Its central question is:

> **How does organisational learning become durable capability, and how does the organisation develop, retire or reconfigure that capability as conditions change?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant organisational context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. form a useful orientation;
6. make an attributable and authorised decision;
7. coordinate interdependent collective action;
8. execute authorised work reliably;
9. observe and evaluate consequences;
10. and preserve, reconstruct and interpret experience through organisational memory and learning.

Learning does not automatically change what the organisation can do.

A lesson may be:

- understood by one person;
- accepted by one team;
- recorded in a repository;
- incorporated into one decision;
- or applied once under unusually favourable conditions

without becoming durable organisational capability.

The organisation must still:

- identify the capability implication of the learning;
- determine whether the current capability should be preserved, improved, created, combined, distributed, scaled, retired or replaced;
- assess the people, knowledge, routines, authority, technology, relationships and resources required;
- design and test a coherent capability configuration;
- provide opportunities for practice and feedback;
- embed the capability in organisational work;
- distribute it beyond fragile individual dependency;
- preserve enough variation and slack for future adaptation;
- evaluate whether the capability works under relevant conditions;
- recognise decay, obsolescence and lock-in;
- remove or supersede capability that no longer fits;
- and reconfigure the wider organisational system without losing purpose, accountability or continuity.

```text
Validated organisational learning
        ↓
Capability implication
        ↓
Capability design and development
        ↓
Practice, distribution and institutionalisation
        ↓
Reliable deployment across relevant conditions
        ↓
Maintenance, renewal or retirement
        ↓
Organisational reconfiguration
        ↓
Changed future capacity to orient, coordinate and improve
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what an organisational capability is;
- how capability differs from a resource, skill, capacity, competence, routine, technology and performance;
- how learning differs from capability development;
- how capability emerges through people, processes, structures and relationships;
- how operational and dynamic capabilities differ;
- how capabilities form, mature, branch, renew, decay and retire;
- how learning becomes institutionalised without becoming rigid;
- how capabilities are transferred, replicated and scaled;
- how external knowledge becomes internal capability through absorptive capacity;
- how organisations balance exploitation and exploration;
- how experimentation supports capability search and development;
- how path dependence, competency traps and capability traps constrain adaptation;
- how resource allocation, authority and incentives shape reconfiguration;
- how adaptation differs from change volume, transformation rhetoric and short-term performance response;
- how resilience and adaptation relate without becoming synonyms;
- how AI can extend, substitute, distribute or erode capability;
- how provider and model dependence affect organisational capability;
- which failure modes prevent learning from becoming durable and responsible capability;
- and how adaptation completes the Organisational Intelligence feedback loop.

The final integration of the complete Organisational Intelligence construct belongs to OI-BOK-010.

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **An organisational capability is not a resource, individual skill, documented process, technology or isolated success. It is the sufficiently repeatable, coordinated and governable capacity of an organisational unit to perform a class of activity or produce a class of effect across relevant people, time and operating conditions through a configuration of knowledge, practice, roles, authority, relationships, routines, tools and resources.**

Capability development is therefore not the publication of a lesson or deployment of a tool.

Project OI defines **capability development** provisionally as:

> **The deliberate and emergent process through which an organisation creates, strengthens, distributes, combines and sustains the conditions required to perform a class of work or response reliably beyond one individual or episode.**

Project OI defines **organisational adaptation** provisionally as:

> **An evidence-linked change to the organisation’s capability configuration, resource allocation, authority, structures, routines, relationships or purpose that improves or preserves viable and responsible response under changed conditions.**

This creates several necessary distinctions:

```text
Feedback
        ≠
Lesson
        ≠
Learning
        ≠
Capability
        ≠
Capability change
        ≠
Adaptation
```

and:

```text
Resource
        ≠
Capacity
        ≠
Capability
        ≠
Performance
        ≠
Outcome
```

An organisation can:

- purchase a powerful tool without developing the capability to use or govern it;
- train individuals without changing organisational work;
- change a workflow without changing the authority or knowledge required to operate it;
- achieve strong performance through one expert, high effort or favourable conditions;
- replicate a process while losing the mechanism that made it effective;
- scale activity faster than the organisation can preserve quality;
- institutionalise an incorrect lesson;
- preserve a formerly valuable capability after its context has changed;
- invest continually in improvement while workload pressure prevents capability formation;
- adopt AI that increases immediate output while reducing human learning and organisational resilience;
- and change repeatedly without adapting coherently.

The Project OI position is:

> **Adaptation capability is the organisation’s capacity to convert credible learning into appropriately designed and distributed capability, evaluate that capability over time, and preserve, renew, retire or reconfigure it without losing the coherence, authority and memory required for responsible action.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Resource

A **resource** is an asset, input or means that may contribute to organisational work.

Examples:

- people;
- money;
- time;
- data;
- technology;
- facilities;
- reputation;
- intellectual property;
- and relationships.

A resource does not become a capability merely because the organisation possesses it.

### 3.2 Asset

An **asset** is a resource with current or potential value to the organisation.

Assets may be:

- tangible;
- intangible;
- owned;
- accessed;
- leased;
- or relational.

### 3.3 Human Capital

**Human capital** is the knowledge, skills, abilities and other attributes embodied in people that may support productive activity.

Human capital contributes to organisational capability.

It is not equivalent to organisational capability.

### 3.4 Skill

A **skill** is a learned ability to perform a task or class of tasks.

Skill is primarily an individual- or role-level construct unless aggregated and embedded through organisational arrangements.

### 3.5 Competence

**Competence** is sufficient ability to perform defined work to an expected standard under specified conditions.

### 3.6 Capacity

Project OI uses **capacity** for:

> **The amount or extent of work, load, change or response that an actor or system can accommodate within defined constraints.**

Capacity answers:

- how much;
- how fast;
- or under what load.

Capability answers:

- what can be performed;
- how;
- and with what reliability.

### 3.7 Organisational Capability

Project OI defines an **organisational capability** as:

> **A sufficiently repeatable and coordinated organisational capacity to perform a class of activity or produce a class of effect through an identifiable configuration of people, knowledge, practice, routines, authority, relationships, technology and resources.**

### 3.8 Capability Purpose

A **capability purpose** is the organisational need or class of outcome the capability exists to support.

### 3.9 Capability Boundary

A **capability boundary** identifies:

- the organisational unit;
- class of work;
- operating conditions;
- authority;
- and outcome

within which a capability claim is being made.

### 3.10 Capability Configuration

A **capability configuration** is the particular arrangement of:

- people;
- roles;
- skills;
- knowledge;
- routines;
- workflow;
- technology;
- authority;
- resources;
- relationships;
- and governance

through which a capability is enacted.

### 3.11 Capability Element

A **capability element** is one constituent of a capability configuration.

No single element should be assumed sufficient.

### 3.12 Microfoundation

A **microfoundation** is an individual- or interaction-level basis through which organisation-level capability emerges.

Microfoundations research emphasises that organisational capability cannot be explained adequately without considering the actions and interactions of individuals, processes and structures.[^9]

Microfoundations should not be used to deny the causal importance of organisational arrangements.

### 3.13 Capability System

A **capability system** is a set of interdependent capabilities and enabling arrangements that together produce a broader organisational response.

A capability can be strong locally and weak as part of the system.

### 3.14 Operational Capability

An **operational capability** is a capability used to perform current organisational work.

Winter describes ordinary or zero-level capabilities as those that permit an organisation to make a living in the short term.[^6]

Project OI uses **operational capability** rather than zero-level capability in ordinary prose.

### 3.15 Dynamic Capability

Project OI uses **dynamic capability** for:

> **A repeatable organisational capacity to create, extend, combine, modify, redeploy or retire operational capabilities and their supporting resource configurations.**

Teece, Pisano and Shuen describe dynamic capabilities in relation to integrating, building and reconfiguring internal and external competences under changing conditions.[^2]

Winter distinguishes dynamic capabilities from operational capabilities and from one-off ad hoc problem solving.[^6]

### 3.16 Ad Hoc Problem Solving

**Ad hoc problem solving** is a non-routinised response used to address a particular issue or change.

It may be effective.

It is not necessarily evidence that the organisation possesses a dynamic capability.

### 3.17 Capability Formation

**Capability formation** is the process through which a new capability begins to emerge from:

- prior experience;
- resources;
- knowledge;
- routines;
- roles;
- and organising choices.

### 3.18 Capability Development

**Capability development** is the process through which a capability becomes more:

- reliable;
- distributed;
- integrated;
- governable;
- and fit for its intended conditions.

### 3.19 Capability Deployment

**Capability deployment** is the use of a capability in organisational work.

### 3.20 Capability Utilisation

**Capability utilisation** is the degree to which available capability is actually used.

Unused capability may decay.

Overutilised capability may become fragile.

### 3.21 Capability Readiness

**Capability readiness** is the condition in which the required capability configuration can perform the relevant class of work responsibly.

### 3.22 Capability Reliability

**Capability reliability** is the consistency with which a capability performs acceptably across stated conditions and time.

### 3.23 Capability Resilience

**Capability resilience** is the capacity of a capability configuration to:

- absorb;
- recover;
- or extend performance

under disruption without losing its essential purpose and integrity.

### 3.24 Capability Distribution

**Capability distribution** is the spread of required knowledge, practice, authority and supporting arrangements across the actors and units that need to exercise or sustain the capability.

### 3.25 Capability Concentration

**Capability concentration** is the degree to which performance depends on one or a small number of:

- people;
- systems;
- suppliers;
- models;
- or relationships.

### 3.26 Capability Redundancy

**Capability redundancy** is deliberate overlap sufficient to preserve continuity, challenge and recovery.

### 3.27 Capability Owner

A **Capability Owner** is the role accountable for the capability’s:

- purpose;
- design;
- currentness;
- performance;
- development;
- and retirement.

The Capability Owner may not perform the work directly.

### 3.28 Capability Steward

A **Capability Steward** supports the maintenance, coordination or improvement of one or more capability elements without necessarily owning the complete capability.

### 3.29 Capability Evidence

**Capability evidence** is observable evidence that the organisation can perform the class of activity or effect claimed under relevant conditions.

Evidence may include:

- repeated performance;
- successful transfer;
- performance after turnover;
- recovery;
- outcome;
- and demonstrable use beyond one expert.

### 3.30 Capability Claim

A **capability claim** is a statement about what the organisation can perform.

A claim should identify:

- scope;
- conditions;
- evidence;
- and limitations.

### 3.31 Capability Gap

A **capability gap** is a material difference between the capability required and the capability currently available.

A gap may concern:

- existence;
- capacity;
- quality;
- distribution;
- authority;
- integration;
- or resilience.

### 3.32 Capability Dependency

A **capability dependency** is reliance on another capability, resource, actor or external party for performance.

### 3.33 Capability Fragility

**Capability fragility** is the susceptibility of a capability to material loss or failure under:

- absence;
- change;
- disruption;
- or overload.

### 3.34 Capability Debt

Project OI uses **capability debt** as a working term for accumulated:

- skill gaps;
- outdated routines;
- hidden dependencies;
- weak ownership;
- deferred maintenance;
- and unsupported workarounds

that reduce the organisation’s future ability to perform or adapt.

### 3.35 Capability Maturity

Project OI uses **capability maturity** cautiously for the degree to which a capability is:

- established;
- repeatable;
- understood;
- governable;
- and resilient

under stated conditions.

The term does not imply one universal linear maturity scale.

### 3.36 Capability Lifecycle

A **capability lifecycle** is the evolution of a capability across formation, development, maturity and later branching, renewal, retrenchment, redeployment, replication or retirement.

Helfat and Peteraf use the lifecycle concept to describe capability founding, development and maturity followed by several possible altered paths.[^7]

### 3.37 Capability Branching

**Capability branching** occurs where an existing capability develops into:

- renewed;
- replicated;
- redeployed;
- recombined;
- or otherwise altered forms.

### 3.38 Capability Renewal

**Capability renewal** is the substantive update of an existing capability so that it remains fit under changed conditions.

### 3.39 Capability Extension

**Capability extension** expands the:

- scope;
- scale;
- population;
- or condition

under which a capability can perform.

### 3.40 Capability Enrichment

**Capability enrichment** strengthens or expands an existing capability using additional knowledge, practice, resource or technology.

### 3.41 Capability Pioneering

**Capability pioneering** is the creation of materially new capability rather than incremental improvement of an existing one.

### 3.42 Capability Transfer

**Capability transfer** is the movement of capability-related knowledge and arrangements from one actor or unit to another.

Transfer is not complete merely because documentation moved.

### 3.43 Capability Replication

**Capability replication** is the deliberate recreation of a capability in another unit or setting using a source template and adaptation process.

Winter and Szulanski treat replication as a process involving exploration of the business model followed by exploitation through replication, not simple copying.[^16]

### 3.44 Capability Scaling

**Capability scaling** is the expansion of a capability’s:

- volume;
- reach;
- frequency;
- or organisational coverage

while maintaining acceptable performance and governance.

### 3.45 Capability Portability

Project OI uses **capability portability** as a working term for the degree to which a capability can be moved across:

- people;
- units;
- technologies;
- providers;
- or locations

without losing its essential function, authority and evidence.

### 3.46 Capability Decay

**Capability decay** is the gradual reduction in capability quality or availability through:

- disuse;
- turnover;
- knowledge loss;
- deferred maintenance;
- or changed context.

### 3.47 Capability Erosion

**Capability erosion** is the loss of capability through repeated substitution, reduced practice, outsourcing, automation or removal of supporting conditions.

### 3.48 Capability Drift

**Capability drift** occurs where the enacted capability changes gradually while the organisation’s description, governance or assumptions remain static.

### 3.49 Capability Obsolescence

**Capability obsolescence** is the condition in which a capability no longer fits:

- current purpose;
- environment;
- technology;
- risk;
- or stakeholder need.

### 3.50 Capability Retirement

**Capability retirement** is the governed withdrawal of a capability from ordinary organisational use.

### 3.51 Capability Divestment

**Capability divestment** is the removal, sale, outsourcing or release of capability-related resources and arrangements.

Divestment may or may not eliminate the organisational need for the capability.

### 3.52 Institutionalisation

**Institutionalisation** is the embedding of learning or practice into sufficiently durable organisational arrangements such as:

- routines;
- roles;
- structures;
- systems;
- authority;
- incentives;
- and shared expectations.

Crossan, Lane and White describe institutionalising as one of four processes linking individual, group and organisational learning.[^8]

### 3.53 Embedment

**Embedment** is the degree to which capability is incorporated into the organisation’s actual work and relationships.

### 3.54 Standardisation

**Standardisation** is the creation of common rules, interfaces or practices to improve consistency and transfer.

Standardisation can strengthen exploitation and inhibit exploration where applied indiscriminately.

### 3.55 Resource Orchestration

**Resource orchestration** is the deliberate structuring, bundling and leveraging of resources to create and use capability.[^19]

### 3.56 Resource Reallocation

**Resource reallocation** is the movement of:

- money;
- people;
- attention;
- authority;
- technology;
- and capacity

among capabilities or opportunities.

### 3.57 Absorptive Capacity

Cohen and Levinthal define **absorptive capacity** as the organisation’s ability to recognise the value of new external information, assimilate it and apply it to commercial ends.[^10]

Project OI uses the broader formulation:

> **The organisational capacity to recognise, acquire, assimilate, transform and apply relevant external knowledge under current purpose and context.**

### 3.58 Potential Absorptive Capacity

**Potential absorptive capacity** concerns the organisation’s ability to:

- acquire;
- and assimilate

external knowledge.

### 3.59 Realised Absorptive Capacity

**Realised absorptive capacity** concerns the ability to:

- transform;
- and exploit or apply

that knowledge.[^11]

### 3.60 Exploration

**Exploration** includes:

- search;
- variation;
- experimentation;
- discovery;
- and development of new alternatives.

### 3.61 Exploitation

**Exploitation** includes:

- refinement;
- efficiency;
- implementation;
- replication;
- and use of existing knowledge and capability.

March argues that adaptive processes can favour exploitation because its benefits are nearer and more certain, producing short-term efficiency and possible long-term self-destruction.[^12]

### 3.62 Organisational Ambidexterity

**Organisational ambidexterity** is the ability to pursue exploration and exploitation through a suitable organisational arrangement rather than allowing one to eliminate the other.[^15]

### 3.63 Experiment

An **experiment** is a bounded intervention designed to produce evidence about:

- a mechanism;
- capability;
- option;
- or condition.

### 3.64 Pilot

A **pilot** is a limited implementation used to test a capability under selected operational conditions.

A pilot is not necessarily an experiment unless comparison and evidence are designed explicitly.

### 3.65 Prototype

A **prototype** is an early representation or implementation used to explore:

- design;
- feasibility;
- and interaction.

A prototype may borrow capability from expert operators or manual support.

### 3.66 Sandbox

A **sandbox** is a bounded environment that limits the consequence of experimentation.

### 3.67 Variation

**Variation** is the production or presence of alternative practices, ideas, configurations or behaviours.

### 3.68 Selection

**Selection** is the retention or amplification of some variations over others.

### 3.69 Retention

Within capability evolution, **retention** is the preservation and repeated use of selected capability elements or configurations.

### 3.70 Path Dependence

**Path dependence** is a process in which prior choices and self-reinforcing mechanisms progressively narrow the range of available future action.

Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch describe path dependence through a progression toward organisational lock-in.[^17]

### 3.71 Self-Reinforcement

**Self-reinforcement** occurs where use of a capability increases the incentives, knowledge, infrastructure or coordination advantages supporting further use.

### 3.72 Lock-In

**Lock-in** is a condition in which alternatives become difficult to select or implement despite evidence that the current path may no longer be desirable.

### 3.73 Competency Trap

A **competency trap** occurs where positive experience with an existing capability reinforces its use and reduces search for potentially superior alternatives.

### 3.74 Capability Trap

Repenning and Sterman use **capability trap** to describe a self-reinforcing pattern in which pressure to improve current performance diverts resources from process improvement, worsening capability and reinforcing the belief that improvement investment is unaffordable.[^18]

### 3.75 Adaptation

Project OI defines **adaptation** as:

> **An evidence-linked change to organisational capability or configuration that improves or preserves viable and responsible response under changed conditions.**

### 3.76 Organisational Reconfiguration

**Organisational reconfiguration** is the deliberate alteration of:

- capability;
- resource;
- structure;
- authority;
- relationship;
- routine;
- and technology

across the organisational system.

### 3.77 Strategic Renewal

**Strategic renewal** is a material change to the organisation’s:

- direction;
- business model;
- capability base;
- or relationship with its environment.

### 3.78 Transformation

Project OI uses **transformation** only for substantial and multi-dimensional organisational change.

The term should not be used automatically for every programme or technology adoption.

### 3.79 Adaptation Coherence

Project OI uses **adaptation coherence** as a working term for the degree to which changes across capability, authority, resources, identity and purpose remain mutually compatible.

### 3.80 Borrowed Capability

Project OI uses **borrowed capability** as a working term for performance enabled materially by an external:

- provider;
- expert;
- platform;
- model;
- partner;
- or temporary support arrangement

that the organisation cannot reproduce, govern or sustain independently.

Borrowed capability may be strategically useful.

It should not be represented as fully internal capability.

### 3.81 Capability Augmentation

**Capability augmentation** occurs where technology or external support extends an existing human or organisational capability while retaining meaningful internal understanding and control.

### 3.82 Capability Substitution

**Capability substitution** occurs where one capability, technology or provider performs work previously dependent on another capability.

Substitution can reduce cost and increase performance while also creating:

- skill erosion;
- dependency;
- and recovery risk.

### 3.83 Capability Opacity

Project OI uses **capability opacity** as a working term for the degree to which the organisation cannot explain or inspect:

- how a capability performs;
- where it depends;
- why it fails;
- or what would be required to reproduce it.

### 3.84 Capability Sovereignty

Project OI uses **capability sovereignty** cautiously for the degree to which the organisation retains legitimate control over:

- purpose;
- authority;
- essential knowledge;
- operational continuity;
- portability;
- and exit

for a capability.

It does not imply that all capability must be owned internally.

## 4. Learning Is Not Capability

Chapter 8 established that learning occurs where experience changes organisationally available understanding or future response and persists beyond the immediate individual or episode.

Capability requires more.

### 4.1 Individual Learning

One employee understands a better method.

### 4.2 Group Learning

A team changes its shared response.

### 4.3 Organisational Availability

The learning becomes accessible across relevant units.

### 4.4 Institutionalisation

The learning changes:

- roles;
- routines;
- authority;
- training;
- tools;
- or structures.

### 4.5 Capability

The organisation can perform the relevant class of work:

- repeatedly;
- under stated conditions;
- beyond one person;
- with acceptable quality;
- and with sufficient governance and resilience.

### Example — Incident Review

Lesson:

> Deployment checks did not detect capacity incompatibility.

Capability implication:

> The organisation requires a reliable pre-release capacity-qualification capability.

Capability development may require:

- a new test;
- environment;
- responsibility;
- data;
- release gate;
- exception authority;
- and learning feedback.

Changing one checklist without creating the required environment or authority may not create the capability.

### Project OI Principle

> **Learning becomes capability only where the organisation can enact the changed understanding reliably through appropriate people, routines, authority, technology and resources.**

## 5. Capability Is Not Performance, Resource or Technology

### 5.1 Capability and Performance

Strong performance may result from:

- favourable demand;
- luck;
- one expert;
- temporary effort;
- or external support.

Capability should be inferred from repeated and appropriately bounded evidence, not one outcome.

### 5.2 Capability and Resource

A company can possess:

- an AI model;
- skilled employees;
- a database;
- and a budget

without possessing the capability to produce a governed AI-supported decision.

### 5.3 Capability and Skill

Skill may reside in one person.

Capability requires organisational availability and integration.

### 5.4 Capability and Routine

A routine may be one capability element.

A routine can also be obsolete, performed mechanically or disconnected from outcome.

### 5.5 Capability and Technology

Deploying technology changes the capability configuration.

It does not establish that users:

- can use it;
- understand it;
- govern it;
- recover from failure;
- or sustain the work.

### 5.6 Capability and Capacity

A team may have the capability to perform complex review but insufficient capacity for current volume.

A larger team may have capacity but lack capability.

### 5.7 Capability and Outcome

A capability enables a class of response.

The environment and execution still influence outcome.

### Project OI Principle

> **Capability claims should identify the organisational configuration and evidence that make the performance reproducible rather than attributing capability to the most visible resource or latest success.**

## 6. Capability as a Sociotechnical Configuration

Organisational capability emerges from a configuration rather than one component.

### 6.1 People

- expertise;
- skill;
- judgement;
- motivation;
- and availability.

### 6.2 Knowledge

- explicit guidance;
- tacit understanding;
- context;
- evidence;
- and memory.

### 6.3 Practice and Routine

- repeated action;
- coordination;
- exception handling;
- and repair.

### 6.4 Roles and Authority

- ownership;
- decision rights;
- action authority;
- escalation;
- and accountability.

### 6.5 Technology and Tools

- execution;
- representation;
- automation;
- observability;
- and access.

### 6.6 Resources

- time;
- money;
- data;
- capacity;
- infrastructure;
- and suppliers.

### 6.7 Relationships

- trust;
- coordination;
- knowledge access;
- and partner contribution.

### 6.8 Governance

- purpose;
- constraints;
- evaluation;
- and retirement.

### 6.9 Outcome Connection

- what the capability exists to achieve;
- and how consequence returns as feedback.

### Configuration Principle

A strong component can be neutralised by a missing complement.

Examples:

- skilled employees without authority;
- automation without reliable data;
- policy without operational capacity;
- or strong AI output without human and organisational integration.

### Microfoundations and Emergence

Felin and colleagues emphasise individuals, processes and structures as microfoundations of routines and capabilities.[^9]

Project OI retains a multilevel position:

```text
Individual and interaction-level elements
        ↓
are organised through roles, routines and structures
        ↓
produce an organisation-level capability
        ↓
which changes the conditions and behaviour of individuals
```

The capability should not be reduced either to individual traits or to an abstract organisation-level label.

## 7. The Capability Lifecycle

Helfat and Peteraf’s capability lifecycle provides a useful basis for understanding capability evolution.[^7]

Project OI adapts it as follows.

### 7.1 Need or Opportunity Recognition

The organisation identifies:

- a gap;
- changed condition;
- or opportunity.

### 7.2 Founding

A capability begins through a combination of:

- prior knowledge;
- people;
- resources;
- and organising choices.

### 7.3 Development

The organisation gains experience and improves:

- coordination;
- routine;
- skill;
- and supporting infrastructure.

### 7.4 Maturity

The capability becomes more:

- repeatable;
- predictable;
- and efficient

within its established domain.

### 7.5 Branching

Possible paths include:

- renewal;
- extension;
- replication;
- redeployment;
- recombination;
- retrenchment;
- and retirement.

### 7.6 Decline

Use or performance may decline through:

- changed need;
- underinvestment;
- knowledge loss;
- or superior alternatives.

### 7.7 Retirement or Transformation

The organisation may:

- withdraw;
- replace;
- outsource;
- preserve for contingency;
- or convert

the capability.

### Lifecycle Is Not Universal

Capabilities can:

- remain immature;
- skip stages;
- be acquired externally;
- or disappear abruptly.

### Lifecycle Evidence

A lifecycle view should preserve:

- original purpose;
- development history;
- operating assumptions;
- capability evidence;
- current owner;
- dependencies;
- and retirement criteria.

## 8. Theoretical Foundations

### 8.1 Evolutionary Theory and Routines

Nelson and Winter treat organisational routines as important repositories of organisational knowledge and as the basis for evolutionary variation, selection and retention.[^1]

Capabilities emerge historically through:

- repeated action;
- learning;
- and selection.

They are therefore path dependent.

### 8.2 Dynamic Capabilities

Teece, Pisano and Shuen argue that organisational advantage under rapid change depends on processes of coordination and reconfiguration shaped by asset positions and paths.[^2]

Teece later develops microfoundations for:

- sensing;
- seizing;
- and reconfiguring.[^5]

Project OI does not adopt that three-part model as a substitute for Sense–Know–Choose–Mobilise–Learn–Adapt.

It uses dynamic-capability research to explain the reconfiguration work within **Adapt**.

### 8.3 Specific Dynamic Processes

Eisenhardt and Martin argue that dynamic capabilities are identifiable processes such as:

- product development;
- strategic decision-making;
- and alliancing,

with different properties in moderately dynamic and high-velocity environments.[^4]

This guards against treating dynamic capability as a vague synonym for successful change.

### 8.4 Operational and Dynamic Capabilities

Winter distinguishes operational capabilities from capabilities that create, extend or modify them and notes that organisations may use ad hoc problem solving instead of investing in a repeatable dynamic capability.[^6]

A small organisation may rationally choose occasional expert-led reconfiguration rather than maintain a permanent transformation function.

### 8.5 Deliberate Learning

Zollo and Winter identify:

- experience accumulation;
- knowledge articulation;
- and knowledge codification

as learning mechanisms through which dynamic capabilities evolve.[^3]

Their relative value depends on task:

- frequency;
- homogeneity;
- and causal ambiguity.

### 8.6 Organisational Learning and Institutionalisation

Crossan, Lane and White’s 4I framework connects:

- intuiting;
- interpreting;
- integrating;
- and institutionalising

across individual, group and organisational levels.[^8]

Chapter 8 owned learning.

Chapter 9 owns the capability consequences of institutionalisation.

### 8.7 Absorptive Capacity

Cohen and Levinthal show that external knowledge is useful only where the organisation possesses prior related knowledge and the ability to recognise, assimilate and apply it.[^10]

Zahra and George distinguish potential and realised absorptive capacity.[^11]

External access is not equivalent to internal capability.

### 8.8 Exploration and Exploitation

March describes a fundamental allocation problem between:

- exploration of new possibilities;
- and exploitation of existing certainties.[^12]

Returns to exploitation are usually:

- nearer;
- more certain;
- and easier to connect to action,

creating a tendency to underinvest in exploration.

### 8.9 Learning Myopia

Levinthal and March identify myopia concerning:

- distant time;
- distant place;
- and failure.[^13]

Capability development can optimise current work while making the organisation less able to respond later.

### 8.10 Organisational Ambidexterity

O’Reilly and Tushman describe organisational ambidexterity as the ability to exploit mature capabilities and explore new ones.[^15]

The literature contains several arrangements:

- structural separation;
- temporal separation;
- contextual ambidexterity;
- and leadership integration.

No one form is universally superior.

### 8.11 Capability Traps

Repenning and Sterman show how short-term performance pressure and delayed improvement returns can produce a self-confirming capability trap.[^18]

The organisation diverts effort from improvement to current output.

Capability declines.

Managers interpret the decline as evidence that employees need more control or current effort.

Improvement becomes even harder.

### 8.12 Path Dependence

Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch explain organisational path dependence through:

- preformation;
- self-reinforcing development;
- and lock-in.[^17]

A capability can become entrenched because:

- infrastructure;
- skill;
- coordination;
- identity;
- and incentives

all reinforce continued use.

### 8.13 Resource Orchestration

Sirmon and colleagues emphasise managerial actions that:

- structure;
- bundle;
- and leverage

resources across organisational breadth, depth and lifecycle.[^19]

Capability change requires synchronisation across these actions.

### 8.14 Replication

Winter and Szulanski show that replication involves both exploration of the source template and exploitation through repeated use.[^16]

Replication without understanding can reproduce visible form and lose the active mechanism.

## 24. AI and Human–AI Capability Development

AI can change organisational capability through several mechanisms.

### 24.1 Capability Augmentation

AI may extend human capability through:

- retrieval;
- drafting;
- analysis;
- and guidance.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond find that a generative AI assistant increased productivity in one customer-support setting, with larger gains among less-experienced workers and evidence of some learning.[^22]

The result demonstrates one pathway for distributing expert-derived patterns.

It does not establish general organisational capability.

### 24.2 Capability Substitution

AI may perform work previously dependent on human skill.

Substitution can:

- increase availability;
- and reduce cost.

It can also reduce:

- practice;
- internal knowledge;
- and recovery capacity.

### 24.3 Capability Borrowing

A business may perform sophisticated work because a model provider supplies much of the underlying reasoning and knowledge.

The organisation should distinguish:

```text
What the organisation can do independently
What it can do with the provider
What it can govern
What it can verify
What it can recover if access changes
```

### 24.4 Capability Distribution

AI can make guidance available across roles and locations.

It may reduce expert bottlenecks.

### 24.5 Capability Homogenisation

One model may cause:

- common language;
- and common solution patterns.

This can improve consistency and reduce diverse exploration.

### 24.6 Capability Erosion

Algorithmic recommendation can weaken experiential learning under some conditions.[^23]

### 24.7 Capability Recombination

The cybernetic-teammate experiment found that individuals with AI matched the performance of human teams without AI on realistic product-innovation tasks in one firm and that AI reduced functional differences in proposals.[^24]

This suggests that AI can reconfigure how expertise is combined.

It does not prove that AI replaces human teams or organisational coordination generally.

### 24.8 Coding Capability

A 2026 study combining three company-run field experiments across 4,867 developers reports an estimated 26.08 per cent increase in completed tasks for developers with access to an AI coding assistant, with substantial uncertainty across experiments and stronger adoption and gains among less-experienced developers.[^25]

Task completion is not a complete measure of code quality, maintainability or organisational development capability.

### 24.9 Capability Opacity

The organisation may achieve performance without understanding:

- the mechanism;
- model limitations;
- or data dependency.

### 24.10 Provider and Model Drift

Capability can change when the provider changes:

- model;
- policy;
- price;
- context window;
- or tool behaviour.

### 24.11 Capability Sovereignty

For critical capability, assess:

- portability;
- data control;
- alternate path;
- verification;
- and exit.

### 24.12 Human Development

Decide which human capabilities must remain because they are required for:

- oversight;
- exception;
- innovation;
- and recovery.

### 24.13 AI Capability Owner

An AI-enabled capability needs ownership for:

- purpose;
- model and provider;
- data;
- workflow;
- authority;
- human roles;
- evaluation;
- and retirement.

### Project OI Principle

> **AI adoption creates organisational capability only where model performance is integrated with human skill, authority, workflow, evidence, continuity and the capacity to verify, adapt or exit the arrangement.**

## 26. Proportionate Capability and Adaptation Modes

### 26.1 Preserve and Maintain

Use where the capability remains fit.

Focus:

- reliability;
- refresh;
- and continuity.

### 26.2 Incremental Improvement

Use where the purpose remains stable and evidence identifies bounded weakness.

### 26.3 Capability Enrichment

Add:

- knowledge;
- technology;
- scope;
- or resilience.

### 26.4 Capability Transfer

Use a proven mechanism in another unit with local adaptation.

### 26.5 Capability Scaling

Expand reach or volume after reliability and governance evidence.

### 26.6 Capability Pioneering

Create materially new capability where current configurations cannot meet the need.

### 26.7 Capability Recombination

Combine existing capabilities into a new response.

### 26.8 Capability Renewal

Redesign a mature capability under changed conditions.

### 26.9 Capability Retrenchment

Reduce scope or scale while preserving the most important function.

### 26.10 Capability Retirement

Remove obsolete or harmful capability and dependencies.

### 26.11 Emergency Adaptation

Use temporary configuration under crisis, with later review and expiry.

### 26.12 AI-Enabled Reconfiguration

Change human, model, workflow and authority arrangements together.

### Mode Principle

> **Select the adaptation mode according to the capability need, evidence, environment, path dependence and organisational consequence—not according to the visibility or novelty of the proposed technology.**

## 30. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Learning Is Not Capability

> Organisational learning becomes capability only where changed understanding is enacted reliably through organisational arrangements beyond one individual or episode.

### P2 — Capability Is Configurational

> Organisational capability emerges from the integration of people, knowledge, routines, authority, relationships, technology and resources rather than from any one element.

### P3 — Capability Is Distinct From Performance

> Strong performance in one episode is insufficient evidence of repeatable organisational capability.

### P4 — Capability Claims Require Boundaries

> Capability should be stated relative to a class of work, organisational unit, operating conditions and evidence.

### P5 — Institutionalisation Requires Practice and Authority

> Documentation and training will not institutionalise learning where actors lack representative practice, resources or authority.

### P6 — Institutionalisation Can Preserve Error

> The durability of a routine or system does not establish the validity of the lesson embedded within it.

### P7 — Capability Development Uses Several Learning Mechanisms

> Experience accumulation, knowledge articulation, codification and practice contribute differently depending on task frequency, variability and causal ambiguity.

### P8 — Capability Has a Lifecycle

> Organisational capabilities form, develop, mature and then branch through renewal, replication, redeployment, retrenchment or retirement.

### P9 — Distribution Reduces Some Fragility

> Capability distributed across appropriate actors and reservoirs will be less dependent on individual availability, but may require stronger coordination and consistency mechanisms.

### P10 — Transfer Requires Recontextualisation

> Capability transfer succeeds where the receiving unit can enact and govern the mechanism under local conditions rather than merely copy the source artefact.

### P11 — Scaling Changes Capability Requirements

> Increased volume or scope creates new coordination, control, resource and failure conditions that may invalidate evidence from a small pilot.

### P12 — External Knowledge Requires Absorptive Capacity

> Access to external knowledge or experts becomes organisational capability only where the organisation can recognise, assimilate, transform and apply it.

### P13 — Exploitation Tends to Outcompete Exploration

> Shorter and more certain returns from current capability create a systematic tendency to underinvest in future alternatives.

### P14 — Exploration Without Institutionalisation Is Incomplete

> Experimentation contributes to adaptation only where selection and integration can convert useful variation into operating capability.

### P15 — Capability Traps Are Self-Reinforcing

> Operational pressure can reduce improvement investment, weaken capability and create evidence that appears to justify further short-term pressure.

### P16 — Path Dependence Narrows Adaptation

> Prior investments, routines, metrics, identity and coordination can make obsolete capability difficult to replace even where evidence favours change.

### P17 — Capability Requires Maintenance

> Capability will decay where knowledge, practice, technology, ownership and resources are not refreshed.

### P18 — Automation Can Erode Capability

> Technology may improve immediate performance while reducing human learning, internal understanding or recovery capacity.

### P19 — Capability Retirement Is a Core Adaptive Function

> Organisational adaptation depends on the ability to retire obsolete routines, tools, authority and dependencies rather than only add new capability.

### P20 — Adaptation Is Not Change Volume

> Organisational change contributes to Adapt only where it is evidence-linked and improves or preserves responsible future response capacity.

### P21 — Adaptation Must Remain Coherent

> Local capability changes can reduce organisation-level intelligence where they damage shared purpose, dependencies, authority or identity.

### P22 — Resilience and Adaptation Differ

> Resilience preserves viable performance through disturbance, while adaptation changes the capability system for future conditions.

### P23 — AI Can Distribute and Reconfigure Capability

> AI may make expert-derived performance and cross-functional contribution more widely available under some task and organisational conditions.

### P24 — AI Can Create Borrowed Capability

> Performance dependent on a model provider should not be represented as fully internal organisational capability where verification, portability or continuity are weak.

### P25 — AI Capability Requires Organisational Integration

> Model access becomes organisational capability only where it is integrated with human roles, authority, workflow, evidence, outcome evaluation and lifecycle governance.

### P26 — Capability Sovereignty Is Conditional

> Organisations need not own every capability element but should retain sufficient control over critical purpose, authority, continuity, evidence and exit.

### P27 — Adaptation Closes the OI Loop

> Adaptation strengthens Organisational Intelligence where learning changes future capacity to sense, know, choose, mobilise and improve.

## 32. Practical Principles

### 32.1 Define What the Organisation Must Be Able to Do

Do not begin with a course, tool or reorganisation.

### 32.2 Bound the Capability Claim

State:

- scope;
- condition;
- quality;
- and evidence.

### 32.3 Design the Complete Configuration

Include people, practice, authority, technology and resources.

### 32.4 Convert Lessons Into Capability Requirements

A lesson is not the design.

### 32.5 Provide Practice With Feedback

Awareness does not create performance.

### 32.6 Embed Capability in Normal Work

Do not leave it inside training or project documentation.

### 32.7 Give the Capability an Owner

Own development, currentness and retirement.

### 32.8 Align Authority

People must be able to perform and adapt within bounds.

### 32.9 Preserve Source Evidence and Applicability

Do not institutionalise a decontextualised lesson.

### 32.10 Test Before Scaling

A demonstration is not production capability.

### 32.11 Scale the Governance With the Work

Volume creates new risk and dependency.

### 32.12 Transfer Mechanisms, Not Only Artefacts

Require receiving evidence.

### 32.13 Maintain Critical Capability

Use, refresh and rehearse it.

### 32.14 Create Proportionate Redundancy

Avoid one-point dependency where consequence is material.

### 32.15 Protect Exploration

Current delivery will consume unprotected improvement capacity.

### 32.16 Close Experiments

Select, integrate or stop.

### 32.17 Monitor Capability Traps

Treat chronic pressure and deferred improvement as a reinforcing system.

### 32.18 Review Path Dependence

Ask which investments and metrics make alternatives difficult.

### 32.19 Retire Obsolete Capability

Do not allow workarounds to keep it alive indefinitely.

### 32.20 Preserve Historical Memory During Retirement

Removal should not destroy accountability.

### 32.21 Reallocate Real Resources

A strategic priority without people, budget or authority is not reconfiguration.

### 32.22 Preserve Adaptation Coherence

Test effects on the wider capability system.

### 32.23 Distinguish Resilience From Adaptation

Recovery and future reconfiguration are different decisions.

### 32.24 Treat AI as a Capability Element

Do not treat the model as the complete capability.

### 32.25 Protect Human Oversight and Recovery Skills

Decide deliberately which capabilities must remain internal.

### 32.26 Assess Borrowed Capability

State what depends on the provider and what the organisation can reproduce.

### 32.27 Test Portability and Exit

Especially for critical AI and cloud-enabled capability.

### 32.28 Evaluate Capability Outcomes

Capability activity is not proof of improved response.

### 32.29 Revisit Capability Claims After Change

A new provider, scale or population may invalidate prior evidence.

### 32.30 Close the Learning-to-Adaptation Loop

Show how feedback changed future organisational capacity.

## 33. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Capability development and adaptation complete **Improve** and close the operational Organisational Intelligence loop.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Learning quality
× capability integration
× lifecycle governance
× adaptation coherence
= improved future response potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Learning Without Capability

```text
The organisation understands
→ work and authority remain unchanged
→ future response repeats the old pattern
```

### Capability Without Distribution

```text
One team or expert can perform
→ the organisation depends on their availability
→ capacity remains fragile
```

### Capability Without Maintenance

```text
The organisation once performed reliably
→ skills, tools and context decay
→ capability claim remains while capability disappears
```

### Exploration Without Exploitation

```text
The organisation generates alternatives
→ none becomes normal work
→ innovation does not alter capability
```

### Exploitation Without Exploration

```text
Current capability becomes efficient
→ environment changes
→ the organisation cannot respond
```

### Change Without Adaptation

```text
Tools, structures and roles change
→ evidence and capability do not improve
→ organisational coherence declines
```

### AI Without Capability Integration

```text
The model produces strong output
→ human roles, authority, continuity and learning are weak
→ performance is borrowed and fragile
```

### Adaptation Without Memory

```text
The organisation reconfigures
→ rationale and prior learning disappear
→ future adaptation repeats old discovery
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when credible learning can be converted into durable, distributed and governable capability, when obsolete capability can be retired, and when the organisation can reconfigure its capability system without losing the purpose, memory, authority and coherence required for responsible future action.**

## 34. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises evolutionary theory, organisational learning, resource-based theory, dynamic capabilities, absorptive capacity, ambidexterity, path dependence, routine research and emerging AI evidence;
- does not establish a validated Project OI capability construct;
- does not prove that the proposed Capability Development and Adaptation Process is complete;
- and does not provide one universal method for capability assessment or reconfiguration.

### 34.1 Capability Construct Ambiguity

Capability is used differently across:

- strategy;
- operations;
- human resources;
- public management;
- and technology.

### 34.2 Performance Circularity

Dynamic-capability research has been criticised where capability is inferred from success and then used to explain success.

Project OI therefore requires capability evidence distinct from favourable outcome.

### 34.3 Level of Analysis

Individual skill, team capability, organisational capability and ecosystem capability should not be conflated.

### 34.4 Microfoundation Debate

Scholars disagree about the appropriate relationship between individual-level explanation and organisation-level routine and capability.

### 34.5 Dynamic-Capability Boundary

Not every change process is a dynamic capability.

Ad hoc response may be more economical for infrequent change.[^6]

### 34.6 Lifecycle Variation

Not every capability follows a stable lifecycle.

### 34.7 Ambidexterity

The evidence does not support one universal arrangement for balancing exploration and exploitation.

### 34.8 Path Dependence

Observed persistence may result from:

- rational complementarity;
- power;
- uncertainty;
- or true path dependence.

### 34.9 Capability Measurement

Repeatability, transfer, resilience and outcome are difficult to combine without construct inflation.

### 34.10 Capability Retirement

Research gives less attention to capability retirement than formation and development.

### 34.11 SMB Applicability

Many dynamic-capability studies concern large firms and competitive advantage.

SMBs may use more episodic and person-dependent adaptation.

### 34.12 Responsible Purpose

A strong capability can support harmful organisational goals.

Capability quality does not establish ethical legitimacy.

### 34.13 AI Generalisability

The cited AI field experiments concern specific:

- firms;
- tasks;
- models;
- and time periods.

### 34.14 Skill Erosion

Evidence that algorithmic tools can weaken experiential learning comes from one healthcare setting and should not be universalised.[^23]

### 34.15 Provider Dynamics

AI provider capability, pricing and policy continue to change rapidly.

### 34.16 Borrowed Capability

Borrowed capability is a Project OI working concept requiring empirical and conceptual challenge.

### 34.17 Capability Sovereignty

Capability sovereignty is a provisional design concept and may overlap with resilience, strategic autonomy and resource dependence.

### 34.18 Adaptation Boundary

Adaptation can be:

- reactive;
- anticipatory;
- or environment-shaping.

The chapter does not provide a complete typology.

### 34.19 Final Construct Integration

Purpose, governance, ethics, measurement, human–AI integration and full construct boundaries remain for Chapter 10.

## 37. Editorial Guardrail

> **Project OI must not confuse learning, training, documentation, technology adoption, isolated performance or change activity with organisational capability or adaptation. Capability contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where learning becomes a reliable and governable organisational configuration, and adaptation contributes only where evidence-linked capability change improves or preserves responsible future response without losing coherence, memory, authority or the capacity to act.**
