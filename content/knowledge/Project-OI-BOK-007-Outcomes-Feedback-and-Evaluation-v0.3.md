## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **outcomes, feedback and evaluation**: how an organisation observes the consequences of its response, distinguishes output from outcome, assesses implementation and contribution, recognises unintended and uneven effects, and produces sufficiently credible feedback for organisational learning.

Its central question is:

> **How does an organisation observe and evaluate the consequences of its response without confusing activity, output, implementation, metric and outcome?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant organisational context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. form a useful orientation;
6. make an attributable and authorised decision;
7. coordinate interdependent collective action;
8. and execute authorised work reliably under real operating conditions.

Execution produces:

- outputs;
- state changes;
- interactions;
- and operational evidence.

It does not establish that the desired outcome occurred.

The organisation must still:

- define which consequences matter;
- identify affected stakeholders;
- distinguish immediate output from intermediate and longer-term outcome;
- preserve the theory connecting action to consequence;
- determine whether the intervention was implemented as intended;
- establish a credible baseline or comparison;
- observe intended and unintended effects;
- distinguish attribution from contribution;
- assess measurement validity and uncertainty;
- detect metric gaming and behavioural reactivity;
- evaluate process, implementation, outcome, impact and sustainability proportionately;
- and transfer credible evaluative feedback into organisational memory and learning.

```text
Authorised execution
        ↓
Output and changed state
        ↓
Consequences across stakeholders and time
        ↓
Observation and measurement
        ↓
Interpretation and evaluation
        ↓
Feedback suitable for organisational learning
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what output, outcome, effect, impact and value mean;
- how outcome differs from completion, implementation and organisational performance;
- how feedback differs from data, monitoring and learning;
- why outcomes must be evaluated against purpose, stakeholder and time;
- how implementation fidelity affects outcome interpretation;
- how theories of change and logic models support but do not prove causality;
- how counterfactual reasoning, attribution and contribution differ;
- how delayed, noisy, selective and politically shaped feedback can mislead;
- how measures change organisational behaviour;
- how targets can corrupt the measures used to evaluate them;
- how process, implementation, outcome and impact evaluation differ;
- how evaluation should remain useful, credible, proportionate and contestable;
- how human–AI systems should be evaluated beyond model accuracy;
- which failure modes prevent consequence from becoming trustworthy feedback;
- and how evaluation capability contributes to Organisational Intelligence.

The durable retention and interpretation of feedback belongs to OI-BOK-008.

Capability development and reconfiguration belong to OI-BOK-009.

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **An outcome is not an output or a metric. It is a consequential change experienced by an organisational entity, stakeholder, system or environment after action, interpreted relative to purpose, expectation, baseline and alternative explanation.**

Feedback is therefore not whatever data arrives after action.

Project OI defines **organisational feedback** provisionally as:

> **Evidence about the state, effects and consequences of organisational response that can be connected credibly enough to prior purpose, decision, execution and context to support evaluation and future learning.**

Evaluation is not the mechanical comparison of a target with a number.

Project OI defines **evaluation** provisionally as:

> **A governed and evidence-bearing process of making a reasoned judgement about the relevance, coherence, implementation, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, fairness, sustainability or other material qualities of an organisational intervention, decision, system or response.**

This creates several necessary distinctions:

```text
Activity
        ≠
Output
        ≠
Implementation
        ≠
Outcome
        ≠
Impact
        ≠
Evaluation
        ≠
Learning
```

An organisation can:

- complete every planned activity and achieve no useful outcome;
- miss a target while creating a valuable unintended benefit;
- meet a target by degrading another stakeholder outcome;
- implement a sound intervention badly;
- implement a weak intervention faithfully;
- observe improvement that would have occurred anyway;
- produce a good outcome through luck or external conditions;
- produce a poor outcome despite a reasonable decision and strong execution;
- collect accurate metrics that do not measure the intended construct;
- and conduct an excellent evaluation that is ignored.

The Project OI position is:

> **Evaluation capability is the organisational capacity to define material outcomes, observe consequences across time and stakeholders, test the pathway from action to result, preserve uncertainty and alternative explanation, and convert evidence into a credible judgement that can support learning without rewriting the past.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Activity

An **activity** is work performed by a person, team, workflow, system, partner or AI.

Activity measures what was done.

It does not establish what changed.

### 3.2 Input

An **input** is a resource consumed, allocated or made available for an intervention or operation.

Examples:

- money;
- time;
- labour;
- data;
- infrastructure;
- expertise;
- and model usage.

### 3.3 Output

An **output** is an immediate product, service, artefact, state transition or completed action produced through execution.

Examples:

- report issued;
- customer refunded;
- application released;
- supplier reviewed;
- message sent;
- recommendation generated;
- or workflow completed.

Outputs are usually closer to organisational control than outcomes.

### 3.4 Deliverable

A **deliverable** is an agreed output that satisfies a defined obligation or acceptance condition.

A deliverable can be accepted without producing its intended outcome.

### 3.5 Result

**Result** is a broad term that may refer to:

- output;
- outcome;
- impact;
- or implementation consequence.

Project OI should avoid using it where a more precise term is available.

### 3.6 Consequence

A **consequence** is any state change, experience or effect associated with action, inaction or environmental interaction.

Consequences may be:

- intended or unintended;
- anticipated or unanticipated;
- beneficial, harmful or mixed;
- direct or indirect;
- immediate or delayed;
- reversible or persistent;
- and visible or hidden.

Merton’s work on unanticipated consequences demonstrates that purposive action may produce effects that were not predicted because of limited knowledge, error, immediate interests and other factors.[^1]

### 3.7 Intended Consequence

An **intended consequence** is an effect included within the purpose or expected result of the decision or intervention.

### 3.8 Unintended Consequence

An **unintended consequence** is an effect that was not part of the actor’s purpose.

An unintended consequence may still have been anticipated.

### 3.9 Unanticipated Consequence

An **unanticipated consequence** is an effect the organisation did not predict or recognise before action.

Unanticipated and unintended should not be treated as synonyms.

### 3.10 Outcome

Project OI defines an **outcome** as:

> **A consequential change in the state, behaviour, experience, capability, relationship or condition of an organisational entity, stakeholder, system or environment following an intervention or response.**

An outcome may be:

- intended;
- unintended;
- positive;
- negative;
- mixed;
- intermediate;
- final;
- immediate;
- delayed;
- direct;
- or indirect.

### 3.11 Intermediate Outcome

An **intermediate outcome** is a change expected to contribute to a later outcome.

Examples:

- employee adoption;
- reduced processing delay;
- increased customer understanding;
- improved data quality;
- or changed decision behaviour.

### 3.12 End Outcome

An **end outcome** is the later or more final condition the intervention is intended to influence.

The term does not imply that effects have ceased.

### 3.13 Effect

An **effect** is a change associated causally or plausibly with an intervention, action or condition.

The strength of the causal claim should remain explicit.

### 3.14 Impact

Project OI uses **impact** for:

> **A material, broader or longer-term positive or negative effect on people, organisations, systems or environments, including effects beyond the immediate scope of execution.**

The OECD evaluation criteria define impact in relation to higher-level, significant effects and include intended, unintended, positive and negative consequences.[^2]

The term should not be used merely to make an output sound important.

### 3.15 Benefit

A **benefit** is a valued positive consequence for a specified stakeholder or purpose.

Benefit is not objective without a value frame.

### 3.16 Harm

A **harm** is a material negative consequence affecting:

- wellbeing;
- rights;
- safety;
- opportunity;
- resources;
- trust;
- or organisational viability.

### 3.17 Value

**Value** is the significance or worth assigned to an outcome relative to:

- stakeholder;
- purpose;
- values;
- cost;
- and alternative use.

### 3.18 Externality

An **externality** is a consequence experienced by a party or system not fully represented in the decision, transaction or evaluation boundary.

### 3.19 Stakeholder Outcome

A **stakeholder outcome** is an outcome assessed from the perspective of a defined affected group or actor.

Different stakeholders may experience the same intervention differently.

### 3.20 Distributional Outcome

A **distributional outcome** concerns how benefits, harms, burdens and opportunities are distributed among:

- groups;
- roles;
- regions;
- customers;
- employees;
- or other populations.

Average improvement may conceal concentrated harm.

### 3.21 Organisational Performance

**Organisational performance** is a broad judgement about how well an organisation is achieving relevant objectives or obligations.

March and Sutton caution that organisational performance is causally complex, competitively unstable and often evaluated using retrospective accounts that do not support strong causal inference.[^3]

Performance is not one outcome.

### 3.22 Implementation

**Implementation** is the process through which a decision, policy, intervention or system is put into use.

### 3.23 Implementation Fidelity

**Implementation fidelity** is the degree to which an intervention or programme is delivered as intended.[^4]

Fidelity should not be interpreted as literal procedural conformity where local adaptation is authorised and necessary.

### 3.24 Implementation Outcome

An **implementation outcome** is an effect of the deliberate effort to introduce or use an intervention.

Proctor and colleagues distinguish implementation outcomes such as:

- acceptability;
- adoption;
- appropriateness;
- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- implementation cost;
- penetration;
- and sustainability

from service and client outcomes.[^5]

### 3.25 Implementation Quality

**Implementation quality** is the degree to which implementation creates the conditions necessary for the intervention to operate as intended and responsibly.

### 3.26 Process

A **process** is the sequence or pattern through which work and interaction occur.

### 3.27 Process Outcome

A **process outcome** is a result concerning how work was carried out.

Examples:

- timeliness;
- participation;
- decision quality;
- fairness;
- or handoff integrity.

### 3.28 Implementation Failure

An **implementation failure** occurs where the intended intervention was not delivered, adopted, sustained or used adequately.

### 3.29 Theory of Change

A **theory of change** is an explicit account of:

- how;
- why;
- for whom;
- and under which conditions

an intervention is expected to produce outcomes.

Theory-based evaluation examines implementation conditions and the mechanisms connecting activity to outcome.[^6]

### 3.30 Logic Model

A **logic model** is a structured representation connecting:

```text
Inputs
→ Activities
→ Outputs
→ Intermediate outcomes
→ End outcomes
```

A logic model is a hypothesis and communication device.

It is not proof that the arrows are causal.

### 3.31 Mechanism

A **mechanism** is a process or interaction through which an intervention is expected to contribute to an outcome.

### 3.32 Assumption

An **assumption** is a condition treated as true or sufficiently likely within the outcome pathway.

### 3.33 Context Condition

A **context condition** is an environmental or organisational condition affecting whether the mechanism operates and which outcome occurs.

### 3.34 Baseline

A **baseline** is the state or measured condition before the relevant intervention or comparison period.

A baseline is not automatically a counterfactual.

### 3.35 Benchmark

A **benchmark** is a reference point based on:

- prior performance;
- another unit;
- an external standard;
- or an expected level.

### 3.36 Comparator

A **comparator** is the alternative group, condition, period or treatment used to interpret the observed outcome.

### 3.37 Counterfactual

A **counterfactual** is the unobserved outcome that would have occurred for the same unit, group or system under a different condition.

Holland’s causal-inference framework emphasises that causal effects require comparison between potential outcomes that cannot both be observed for the same unit at the same time.[^7]

### 3.38 Causal Effect

A **causal effect** is the difference between outcomes under alternative conditions attributable to the treatment, action or intervention within a defined causal model.

### 3.39 Attribution

**Attribution** is the assignment of causal responsibility for an observed outcome to an intervention, actor or condition.

Strong attribution requires a defensible counterfactual or equivalent causal design.

### 3.40 Contribution

**Contribution** is a credible claim that an intervention was one material cause among several influencing an outcome.

Contribution analysis develops a reasoned performance story by testing a theory of change and alternative explanations rather than claiming sole attribution where that claim is not supportable.[^8]

### 3.41 Correlation

**Correlation** is an association between variables.

Correlation does not establish causation.

### 3.42 Confounder

A **confounder** is a factor associated with both the intervention and outcome that can distort causal inference.

### 3.43 Selection Effect

A **selection effect** occurs where the units receiving an intervention differ systematically from those that do not.

### 3.44 Regression to the Mean

**Regression to the mean** is the tendency for unusually high or low measurements to be followed by less extreme measurements, even without intervention.

### 3.45 Spillover

A **spillover** is an effect of an intervention on actors or units outside the direct treatment or execution boundary.

### 3.46 Displacement

**Displacement** occurs where improvement in one area shifts:

- cost;
- workload;
- delay;
- risk;
- or harm

to another area.

### 3.47 Feedback

**Feedback** is information returned about state, action or consequence that can influence future behaviour or understanding.

Feedback is not learning automatically.

### 3.48 Feedback Signal

A **feedback signal** is a specific piece of information about:

- progress;
- state;
- output;
- outcome;
- deviation;
- or impact.

### 3.49 Feedback Delay

A **feedback delay** is the time between:

- action;
- consequence;
- observation;
- and organisational availability.

### 3.50 Feedback Distortion

**Feedback distortion** occurs where the information returned differs materially from the underlying consequence because of:

- measurement;
- aggregation;
- filtering;
- incentives;
- delay;
- selection;
- or interpretation.

### 3.51 Feedback Completeness

Project OI uses **feedback completeness** as a working term for the degree to which material positive, negative, intended, unintended and distributional consequences are represented sufficiently for evaluation.

Complete feedback in an absolute sense is impossible.

### 3.52 Outcome Observability

Project OI defines **outcome observability** as:

> **The organisation’s capacity to detect and interpret material consequences across relevant stakeholders, systems and time horizons from available evidence.**

### 3.53 Monitoring

**Monitoring** is the repeated collection and review of selected information about:

- implementation;
- output;
- state;
- risk;
- or outcome.

Monitoring does not itself make an evaluative judgement.

### 3.54 Measurement

**Measurement** is the assignment of values to attributes according to explicit rules.

### 3.55 Measure

A **measure** is the operational method used to represent an attribute.

### 3.56 Metric

A **metric** is a quantified measure used to track, compare or evaluate some aspect of performance, process, output or outcome.

### 3.57 Indicator

An **indicator** is an observable sign used as evidence of a condition or construct that may not be measured directly.

### 3.58 Target

A **target** is a desired or required value for a measure or indicator.

### 3.59 Threshold

A **threshold** is a value or condition that triggers:

- attention;
- decision;
- escalation;
- or intervention.

### 3.60 Leading Indicator

A **leading indicator** is intended to provide earlier evidence of a future outcome or risk.

### 3.61 Lagging Indicator

A **lagging indicator** reflects an outcome or condition after it has occurred.

### 3.62 Proxy

A **proxy** is a measurable variable used to represent a less directly observable construct.

### 3.63 Measurement Validity

**Measurement validity** concerns whether a measure represents the intended construct and supports the intended interpretation.

### 3.64 Measurement Reliability

**Measurement reliability** concerns the consistency or stability of a measure under defined conditions.

A reliable measure can be invalid.

### 3.65 Data Provenance

**Data provenance** is the traceable origin and transformation history of evaluative data.

### 3.66 Disaggregation

**Disaggregation** is the separation of aggregate data by relevant:

- population;
- role;
- customer;
- region;
- time;
- condition;
- or other dimension.

### 3.67 Evaluative Judgement

An **evaluative judgement** is a reasoned conclusion about the merit, worth, significance or fitness of an intervention, decision, system or response against explicit criteria and evidence.

### 3.68 Formative Evaluation

**Formative evaluation** is conducted to improve:

- design;
- implementation;
- or operation

while change remains possible.

### 3.69 Summative Evaluation

**Summative evaluation** assesses overall merit, effectiveness or result at a defined stage or end point.

### 3.70 Process Evaluation

**Process evaluation** examines:

- what was done;
- how;
- for whom;
- and under which conditions.

### 3.71 Outcome Evaluation

**Outcome evaluation** assesses whether and how outcomes changed.

### 3.72 Impact Evaluation

**Impact evaluation** assesses the causal effects of an intervention, usually through explicit counterfactual reasoning.

### 3.73 Evaluation Independence

**Evaluation independence** is the degree to which evaluative judgement is protected from inappropriate influence by:

- delivery;
- ownership;
- interest;
- or hierarchy.

Independence does not require evaluators to be organisationally isolated.

### 3.74 Evaluation Use

**Evaluation use** is the application of evaluation:

- findings;
- process;
- or reasoning

to decisions, accountability, learning or improvement.

### 3.75 Metric Gaming

**Metric gaming** is behaviour intended to improve the measured result without producing the intended underlying outcome.

### 3.76 Goal Displacement

**Goal displacement** occurs where attention shifts from the original purpose to the measure, procedure or target used to represent it.

### 3.77 Campbell-Type Distortion

Project OI uses **Campbell-type distortion** for the pattern in which greater use of a quantitative indicator for decision or control creates greater pressure to corrupt the indicator and the process it is intended to monitor.

Campbell described this dynamic in the context of planned social change and high-stakes indicators.[^9]

### 3.78 Goodhart-Type Effect

Project OI uses **Goodhart-type effect** for the loss of a useful statistical or behavioural relationship when the measure becomes an object of control.

Goodhart’s original observation concerned monetary management.[^10]

The popular formulation that a measure loses quality when turned into a target is associated with later generalisation, including Strathern’s discussion of audit systems.[^11]

### 3.79 Reactivity

**Reactivity** is change in behaviour produced by being:

- observed;
- measured;
- ranked;
- or evaluated.

Espeland and Sauder show how public rankings can change expectations and organisational behaviour through self-fulfilling prophecy and commensuration.[^12]

## 4. Output Is Not Outcome

The simplest evaluation error is treating delivery as consequence.

### 4.1 Output

> A new customer-onboarding workflow was published.

### 4.2 Implementation Outcome

> Eighty per cent of staff adopted the workflow and could use it within the required time.

### 4.3 Intermediate Outcome

> Rework and customer clarification decreased.

### 4.4 End Outcome

> Customers completed onboarding faster with fewer complaints and no increase in compliance risk.

### 4.5 Impact

> The organisation improved customer access, reduced operating cost and changed the capability required from frontline staff.

Each statement answers a different question.

### Output Measures Are Useful

Outputs can indicate:

- delivery;
- volume;
- pace;
- and immediate completion.

They are often available sooner and more directly than outcomes.

### Output Measures Become Dangerous Where

- the organisation assumes the mechanism worked;
- activity volume is rewarded;
- the affected stakeholder is absent;
- or the output is easy to count but weakly connected to purpose.

### Example — AI Assistant

Output:

- 10,000 answers generated.

Implementation outcome:

- staff used the assistant in 65 per cent of eligible cases.

Service outcome:

- average handling time fell.

Stakeholder outcome:

- customers received faster responses.

Potential harm:

- incorrect exceptions increased for one customer group.

Capability outcome:

- newer staff improved, while experienced staff contributed fewer novel solutions.

A count of generated answers cannot represent the complete outcome.

### Project OI Principle

> **Outputs should be evaluated as products of execution and inputs to an outcome pathway, not as proof that the organisational purpose was achieved.**

## 5. Outcome Is Not Improvement

A changed condition is not necessarily an improvement.

### 5.1 Improvement Requires a Value Frame

A shorter service time may be positive for:

- customers;
- cost;
- and throughput.

It may be negative if:

- quality falls;
- complex cases are avoided;
- or employee workload becomes unsafe.

### 5.2 Improvement for Whom?

A system may improve average performance while:

- concentrating harm;
- excluding difficult users;
- or transferring work to an unmeasured group.

### 5.3 Improvement Over Which Horizon?

Short-term gains may produce:

- future debt;
- capability erosion;
- or delayed risk.

### 5.4 Improvement Relative to What?

Possible comparisons include:

- baseline;
- target;
- prior period;
- peer;
- counterfactual;
- standard;
- or stakeholder expectation.

### 5.5 Improvement in Which Dimension?

The OECD criteria distinguish:

- relevance;
- coherence;
- effectiveness;
- efficiency;
- impact;
- and sustainability.[^2]

A response can be effective but inefficient.

It can produce a useful outcome while conflicting with another policy.

It can work briefly and fail to sustain.

### Project OI Principle

> **Outcome evaluation should state the stakeholder, value criterion, time horizon and comparison through which change is being judged as improvement.**

## 6. Feedback Is Not Learning

Feedback closes no loop by itself.

### Feedback May Be

- unavailable;
- delayed;
- incomplete;
- ambiguous;
- politically filtered;
- misinterpreted;
- or ignored.

Sterman describes organisational learning as a feedback process in which decisions change the world and information about that world can revise decisions and mental models. He also identifies major barriers, including delayed and ambiguous feedback, dynamic complexity, misperception and weak inquiry.[^13]

### Data Without Interpretation

```text
Outcome data arrives
→ no one connects it to the decision or mechanism
→ organisational understanding remains unchanged
```

### Interpretation Without Retention

```text
Review identifies the cause
→ rationale is not preserved
→ future actors repeat the same inference work
```

### Retention Without Change

```text
Lesson is documented
→ policy, workflow, authority and practice remain unchanged
→ organisational capacity does not improve
```

### Project OI Boundary

Chapter 7 owns:

- outcome observation;
- feedback quality;
- evaluation;
- and evaluative judgement.

Chapter 8 owns:

- reflection;
- memory;
- learning;
- and durable change in understanding or response.

### Project OI Principle

> **Feedback becomes organisationally useful only where it is connected to prior purpose and action, interpreted credibly and handed into a learning process capable of changing future response.**

## 7. Outcome Pathways and Theory of Change

Outcomes are rarely caused by one action through one direct path.

### 7.1 Outcome Pathway

A working outcome pathway may be represented as:

```text
Inputs
        ↓
Activities
        ↓
Outputs
        ↓
Implementation outcomes
        ↓
Intermediate outcomes
        ↓
End outcomes
        ↓
Broader impacts
```

### 7.2 Mechanisms

The pathway should identify why one stage is expected to influence the next.

Example:

```text
Source-grounded AI guidance
        ↓
reduces search time and exposes stronger examples
        ↓
staff resolve routine cases more consistently
        ↓
customer delay and rework decline
```

### 7.3 Assumptions

The pathway may assume:

- staff trust the guidance appropriately;
- sources remain current;
- complex cases escalate;
- and workload saved is not replaced by extra demand.

### 7.4 Context Conditions

The same mechanism may produce different outcomes under:

- role;
- skill;
- workload;
- customer complexity;
- incentive;
- and technology conditions.

### 7.5 Theory of Implementation Versus Theory of Action

Weiss cautions that theory-based evaluation can confuse:

- how an intervention is implemented;
- with how the intervention is expected to cause change.[^6]

Both are needed.

### 7.6 Logic Model Limits

A logic model may imply:

- certainty;
- linearity;
- and control.

The arrows remain hypotheses.

### 7.7 Negative and Unintended Pathways

Outcome models should include:

- harm;
- displacement;
- adaptation;
- gaming;
- and capability erosion.

### 7.8 Multiple Pathways

One outcome may be produced through several mechanisms.

One intervention may produce several outcomes.

### Project OI Principle

> **An outcome model should make the expected mechanism, assumptions and alternative pathways visible without treating the diagram as causal proof.**

## 8. Theoretical Foundations

### 8.1 Unanticipated Consequences

Merton’s analysis establishes that purposive action can produce consequences not anticipated by the actor because of:

- ignorance;
- error;
- immediate interests;
- and the interaction between prediction and behaviour.[^1]

Outcome evaluation must therefore search beyond intended indicators.

### 8.2 Planned Social Change and Indicator Corruption

Campbell argues that planned social interventions are difficult to evaluate because social conditions continue to change and because the measures used for control can become corrupted under pressure.[^9]

This is central to Project OI because measurement changes organisational behaviour.

### 8.3 Causal Inference

Holland formalises causal inference through potential outcomes and the fundamental problem that the same unit cannot be observed simultaneously under both intervention and non-intervention conditions.[^7]

Outcome observation does not establish causal effect by itself.

### 8.4 Theory-Based Evaluation

Weiss argues that evaluation gains explanatory power by examining:

- implementation;
- mechanisms;
- and conditions

rather than asking only whether outcomes changed.[^6]

### 8.5 Contribution Analysis

Mayne develops contribution analysis for settings where several influences affect outcomes and sole attribution is not credible.[^8]

The method tests:

- the theory of change;
- implementation;
- other influences;
- and the strength of the contribution story.

### 8.6 Implementation Fidelity

Carroll and colleagues show that outcome interpretation depends on whether an intervention was implemented as intended and on moderators such as participant responsiveness and facilitation strategies.[^4]

### 8.7 Implementation Outcomes

Proctor and colleagues distinguish implementation outcomes from service and client outcomes.[^5]

This prevents the organisation from interpreting weak adoption as evidence that the underlying intervention is ineffective.

### 8.8 Evaluation Criteria

The OECD DAC criteria provide a widely used set of evaluative dimensions:

- relevance;
- coherence;
- effectiveness;
- efficiency;
- impact;
- and sustainability.[^2]

The OECD guidance stresses contextual and thoughtful use rather than mechanical application.

### 8.9 Performance Measurement Purposes

Behn identifies different purposes for performance measures, including:

- evaluation;
- control;
- budgeting;
- motivation;
- promotion;
- celebration;
- learning;
- and improvement.[^14]

No one measure is suitable for every purpose.

### 8.10 Organisational Performance Complexity

March and Sutton caution that organisational performance is difficult to explain because of:

- causal complexity;
- competitive instability;
- and retrospective reconstruction.[^3]

### 8.11 Measurement Reactivity

Espeland and Sauder show that measures can recreate social worlds by changing how actors understand and respond to evaluation.[^12]

Metrics are not passive windows.

### 8.12 Complex Feedback

Sterman shows how:

- delays;
- nonlinearity;
- side effects;
- and weak mental models

interfere with feedback and learning.[^13]

### 8.13 Utilisation-Focused Evaluation

Patton and Campbell-Patton argue that evaluation should be designed for identified users and intended uses rather than assuming findings will be used automatically.[^15]

### 8.14 Sociotechnical AI Evaluation

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework treats AI risk as an organisational lifecycle responsibility through:

- Govern;
- Map;
- Measure;
- and Manage.[^16]

NIST’s ARIA programme explicitly extends evaluation beyond laboratory model performance into realistic sociotechnical settings where people interact with AI systems.[^17]

## 9. Evaluation Questions and Criteria

An evaluation should begin with the judgement required.

### 9.1 Purpose Questions

- What was the intervention intended to achieve?
- For whom?
- Over which period?
- Under which constraints?

### 9.2 Relevance Questions

- Did the response address the real need?
- Did the need change?

### 9.3 Coherence Questions

- Did the response support or conflict with other organisational policies and actions?

### 9.4 Implementation Questions

- Was the intervention delivered?
- Was it used?
- Was it adapted?
- Did the adaptation preserve the mechanism?

### 9.5 Effectiveness Questions

- Were intended outcomes achieved?
- For which groups and conditions?

### 9.6 Efficiency Questions

- Were the outcomes proportionate to:

  - cost;
  - time;
  - effort;
  - and alternative use?

### 9.7 Impact Questions

- What broader positive, negative, intended and unintended consequences occurred?

### 9.8 Sustainability Questions

- Did the outcome persist?
- Did the organisation retain the capability and resources required?

### 9.9 Fairness Questions

- How were benefit, harm and burden distributed?
- Who was excluded from observation?

### 9.10 Learning Questions

- Which assumptions were supported?
- Which require revision?

The last questions prepare the handoff into Chapter 8.

## 10. Stakeholder and Distributional Outcomes

An organisation often evaluates from the perspective of the actor who commissioned or funded the work.

That boundary may be too narrow.

### 10.1 Stakeholder Map

For material interventions, identify:

- direct users;
- customers;
- employees;
- managers;
- partners;
- regulators;
- affected communities;
- and future stakeholders.

### 10.2 Conflicting Outcomes

One stakeholder’s benefit may be another’s cost.

Example:

- customer wait time falls;
- frontline workload and emotional strain rise.

### 10.3 Average Outcome

Average improvement may conceal:

- subgroup decline;
- exclusion;
- or unequal access.

### 10.4 Distributional Analysis

Disaggregate where relevant by:

- customer type;
- role;
- location;
- experience;
- disability;
- language;
- risk;
- and other legitimate dimensions.

### 10.5 Voice and Evidence

Stakeholder experience should not be represented only through internal metrics.

Use:

- direct feedback;
- observation;
- complaint;
- qualitative evidence;
- and outcome data.

### 10.6 Missing Stakeholder

A group that cannot complain or access the system may disappear from the evaluation.

### 10.7 Future Consequence

Some outcomes affect:

- future employees;
- future customers;
- or environmental systems

that cannot participate directly.

### Project OI Principle

> **Evaluation should identify who experiences the consequence, who defines value and whose outcome remains outside the current measurement boundary.**

## 11. Implementation Fidelity and Outcome Decomposition

A poor outcome can arise because:

1. the intervention theory was weak;
2. implementation was weak;
3. context prevented the mechanism;
4. an external condition overwhelmed the effect;
5. measurement was weak;
6. or the outcome occurred differently across groups.

### 11.1 Intervention Failure

The intervention was implemented adequately.

The expected outcome did not occur.

### 11.2 Implementation Failure

The intervention was not implemented sufficiently.

Outcome evidence cannot establish whether the underlying intervention would have worked.

### 11.3 Contextual Mismatch

The intervention worked under some conditions and not others.

### 11.4 Adaptation

Local adaptation may:

- improve fit;
- or remove the active mechanism.

### 11.5 Implementation Outcomes

Consider:

- acceptability;
- adoption;
- appropriateness;
- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- cost;
- penetration;
- and sustainability.[^5]

### 11.6 Implementation Evidence

Preserve:

```text
Who used it
How often
Under which conditions
Which components were delivered
Which adaptations occurred
Which resources were available
Which users were excluded
```

### 11.7 Project OI Principle

> **Outcome evaluation should separate whether the intervention was used, whether its mechanism operated and whether the desired consequence occurred.**

## 12. Attribution and Contribution

Organisations often ask:

> Did our action cause the outcome?

The answer may require different levels of claim.

### 12.1 Descriptive Claim

> The outcome changed after implementation.

### 12.2 Associational Claim

> Use of the intervention was associated with the outcome.

### 12.3 Contribution Claim

> The intervention plausibly made a material contribution alongside other factors.

### 12.4 Causal Attribution Claim

> The intervention caused the measured difference relative to a defensible counterfactual.

### 12.5 Attribution Challenges

- self-selection;
- concurrent change;
- maturation;
- seasonality;
- regression to the mean;
- measurement change;
- spillover;
- and external events.

### 12.6 Contribution Story

A credible contribution story should show:

1. a plausible theory of change;
2. implementation evidence;
3. expected mechanisms;
4. observed outcomes;
5. alternative explanations;
6. supporting and contradictory evidence;
7. and remaining uncertainty.[^8]

### 12.7 Shared Causation

Several teams or systems may contribute to one outcome.

Attributing the outcome to the most visible intervention can create:

- credit distortion;
- blame distortion;
- and weak learning.

### 12.8 Project OI Principle

> **Match the strength of the causal statement to the design and evidence rather than treating organisational confidence as attribution.**

## 13. Counterfactuals and Alternative Explanations

The observed post-action state does not reveal what would have happened otherwise.

### 13.1 Experimental Counterfactual

Random assignment can support causal inference where:

- ethical;
- practical;
- and implemented correctly.

### 13.2 Quasi-Experimental Counterfactual

Possible methods include:

- matched comparison;
- interrupted time series;
- regression discontinuity;
- difference-in-differences;
- and synthetic control.

Method selection requires specialist expertise.

### 13.3 Natural Comparison

The organisation may compare:

- another site;
- customer segment;
- team;
- period;
- or staged rollout.

### 13.4 Theory-Based Alternative Explanation

Where experimental design is not feasible, test:

- expected mechanism;
- rival explanation;
- timing;
- and pattern.

### 13.5 Before–After Limits

Before–after comparison may confuse intervention effects with:

- trend;
- season;
- novelty;
- and external change.

### 13.6 Benchmark Limits

A peer may differ in:

- context;
- population;
- and resources.

### 13.7 Counterfactual Humility

Some organisational decisions cannot support strong causal attribution.

The evaluation can still improve judgement by making alternative explanations visible.

## 14. Measurement Design

### 14.1 Start With the Construct

Define what is being evaluated before selecting available data.

### 14.2 Link Measure to Purpose

A measure useful for:

- control

may be poor for:

- learning;
- accountability;
- or impact evaluation.[^14]

### 14.3 Preserve the Data-Generating Process

Ask:

- Who creates the data?
- Under which incentives?
- Which cases enter?
- Which are missing?
- Has the definition changed?
- Does the measurement process alter behaviour?

### 14.4 Validity

Does the measure represent:

- the construct;
- population;
- time;
- and decision

for which it is being used?

### 14.5 Reliability

Would the measurement be sufficiently consistent under equivalent conditions?

### 14.6 Sensitivity and Specificity

Can the measure detect meaningful change without responding excessively to irrelevant variation?

### 14.7 Baseline

A baseline should use:

- comparable definition;
- population;
- and data quality.

### 14.8 Leading and Lagging Indicators

Leading indicators support earlier action.

They are often proxies and require validation.

Lagging indicators provide later confirmation.

They may arrive too late for operational correction.

### 14.9 Measure Portfolio

Use a portfolio balancing:

- output;
- implementation;
- outcome;
- quality;
- risk;
- and distribution.

### 14.10 Qualitative Evidence

Qualitative evidence can reveal:

- mechanism;
- experience;
- and unintended effect

not visible in aggregate metrics.

### 14.11 Data Provenance

Evaluative data should preserve:

- source;
- definition;
- transformation;
- exclusions;
- and time.

### 14.12 Uncertainty

Report:

- confidence interval;
- data limitation;
- model assumption;
- and judgement uncertainty

where appropriate.

### Project OI Principle

> **Measure design should begin from the outcome and judgement required, not from the data that happens to be easy to collect.**

## 15. Feedback Delay, Noise and Dynamic Complexity

### 15.1 Delay

Consequences may appear:

- immediately;
- after weeks;
- or after years.

### 15.2 Competing Time Horizons

A decision may improve short-term throughput and degrade:

- retention;
- trust;
- or capability

later.

### 15.3 Noise

Random variation can make a stable process appear to improve or decline.

### 15.4 Oscillation

Delayed feedback can cause overcorrection.

### 15.5 Hidden Accumulation

Operational debt, customer dissatisfaction or skill erosion may accumulate before crossing a visible threshold.

### 15.6 Feedback Compression

A dashboard may compress a complex outcome into one current value and remove:

- sequence;
- context;
- and distribution.

### 15.7 Misattributed Delay

The latest action may receive credit or blame for consequences produced by earlier action.

### 15.8 Review Cadence

Review timing should match:

- mechanism;
- feedback delay;
- and decision window.

### 15.9 Project OI Principle

> **Evaluation should represent the temporal structure of consequence rather than treating all feedback as immediate and independent.**

## 16. Metrics Change Behaviour

A measure becomes part of the organisational system once people know it matters.

### 16.1 Reactivity

People change behaviour in response to:

- evaluation;
- ranking;
- visibility;
- and reward.[^12]

### 16.2 Metric Gaming

Examples:

- classify difficult cases as ineligible;
- close work before dependency acceptance;
- reduce reported incidents;
- optimise model benchmark performance;
- or shift work outside the measured boundary.

### 16.3 Goal Displacement

The metric becomes the objective.

### 16.4 Goodhart-Type Effect

A previously useful relationship can deteriorate when used directly for control.[^10]

### 16.5 Campbell-Type Distortion

High stakes increase pressure to corrupt the indicator and underlying process.[^9]

### 16.6 Audit Expansion

Strathern argues that audit practices can develop their own cultural and organisational effects beyond monitoring.[^11]

### 16.7 Commensuration

Different qualities are translated into a common metric.

This supports comparison.

It can erase difference.

### 16.8 Indicator Churn

When one indicator becomes gamed, organisations add more measures.

Complexity increases without restoring understanding.

### 16.9 Guardrails

- use multiple measures;
- include qualitative review;
- rotate audit;
- inspect data-generating behaviour;
- preserve outcome purpose;
- and avoid direct high-stakes use of weak proxies.

### Project OI Principle

> **Every material metric should be evaluated both as evidence and as an intervention that changes the behaviour it measures.**

## 17. Evaluation Independence, Credibility and Use

### 17.1 Independence

The people delivering the intervention possess valuable context.

They also have:

- interests;
- identity;
- and reputational stakes.

### 17.2 External Independence

External evaluators can increase credibility.

They may lack:

- local context;
- access;
- or implementation understanding.

### 17.3 Functional Independence

Evaluation may be conducted internally with protections such as:

- separate reporting line;
- transparent methods;
- pre-specified criteria;
- and independent review.

### 17.4 Credibility

Credibility depends on:

- competence;
- method;
- evidence;
- transparency;
- independence;
- and stakeholder trust.

### 17.5 Evaluation Use

An evaluation can be:

- rigorous;
- relevant;
- and ignored.

Utilisation-focused evaluation begins with intended users and uses.[^15]

### 17.6 Misuse

Evaluation may be used:

- symbolically;
- selectively;
- punitively;
- or to legitimise a predetermined decision.

### 17.7 Evaluation Timing

Evaluation should occur while the organisation can still:

- change;
- stop;
- scale;
- or redesign.

### 17.8 Evaluation Ownership

The intervention owner should not be the only actor who decides:

- the question;
- criteria;
- and interpretation.

### 17.9 Project OI Principle

> **Evaluation should be independent enough to challenge the intervention, connected enough to understand it and designed clearly enough to be used.**

## 23. Outcomes, Feedback and Evaluation Failure Modes

Work volume is treated as outcome.

### 23.2 Output Substitution

Deliverables are treated as evidence of impact.

### 23.3 Completion Substitution

A terminal workflow state is treated as proof of success.

### 23.4 Implementation–Outcome Collapse

The organisation cannot distinguish whether the intervention failed or implementation failed.

### 23.5 Outcome–Impact Inflation

A small immediate change is described as broad impact.

### 23.6 Result Ambiguity

“Result” is used without identifying output, outcome or impact.

### 23.7 Purpose Drift

Outcome criteria change after the intervention begins.

### 23.8 Retrospective Targeting

The organisation selects the target after observing the data.

### 23.9 Baseline Absence

No pre-intervention state is preserved.

### 23.10 Baseline Incomparability

Definitions or populations change across periods.

### 23.11 Counterfactual Blindness

Post-action improvement is attributed to the intervention automatically.

### 23.12 Correlation–Causation Error

Association is described as effect.

### 23.13 Attribution Inflation

The organisation claims sole causation in a multi-causal system.

The organisation refuses to make any useful causal judgement because perfect attribution is impossible.

### 23.15 Selection Bias

Participants differ systematically from non-participants.

### 23.16 Regression-to-the-Mean Error

An extreme baseline improves naturally and is credited to the intervention.

### 23.17 Concurrent-Change Confounding

Several changes occur at once.

### 23.18 Spillover Blindness

Effects outside the target group are ignored.

### 23.19 Displacement

Cost, delay or harm moves outside the measured boundary.

Affected groups are absent from the outcome model.

### 23.24 Unintended-Consequence Blindness

Short-term review misses later effect.

### 23.26 Immediate-Interest Bias

Current gain obscures long-term cost.

Outcome is judged without knowing what was implemented.

### 23.28 Adaptation Misclassification

Legitimate local adaptation is treated as implementation failure, or harmful drift is treated as flexibility.

The organisation knows whether a number changed but not why.

The diagram is treated as causal proof.

The metric does not represent the intended construct.

The measure is consistent and wrong.

Definitions and transformations cannot be reconstructed.

The indicator name remains while its calculation changes.

### 23.36 Dashboard Compression

Complex and distributed outcomes collapse into one number.

### 23.37 Leading-Indicator Overconfidence

A weak proxy is treated as guaranteed future outcome.

### 23.38 Lagging-Indicator Paralysis

Evidence arrives after useful intervention is possible.

### 23.39 Metric Gaming

Actors optimise the number rather than the outcome.

### 23.40 Goal Displacement

The target becomes the purpose.

### 23.41 Goodhart-Type Collapse

A useful relationship deteriorates under control pressure.

### 23.42 Campbell-Type Corruption

High stakes corrupt both indicator and process.

### 23.43 Ranking Reactivity

Public comparison changes behaviour and organisational identity.

### 23.44 Indicator Proliferation

More metrics are added instead of improving understanding.

### 23.45 Positive-Only Reporting

Negative and unintended outcomes are suppressed.

### 23.46 Evaluation Capture

The intervention owner controls questions, evidence and conclusion.

### 23.47 External-Evaluator Detachment

Independent evaluation lacks operational context.

### 23.48 Evaluation Theatre

The organisation performs evaluation to demonstrate accountability without allowing findings to affect decisions.

### 23.49 Evaluation Delay

Findings arrive after the relevant decision window.

Credible findings are not routed into decision or learning.

### 23.51 Evaluation Misuse

### 23.52 Outcome Bias

### 23.57 Feedback Laundering

## 25. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Output Is Not Outcome

> Organisational activity and deliverables should not be treated as evidence that the intended consequential change occurred.

### P2 — Outcome Requires a Stakeholder and Value Frame

> Whether a change is an improvement depends on who experiences it, which purpose and value criteria apply and over which time horizon.

### P3 — Feedback Is Not Learning

> Data about consequence contributes to Organisational Intelligence only where it is connected to prior action, interpreted credibly and transferred into a learning process.

### P4 — Outcome Pathways Are Hypotheses

> Logic models and theories of change support explanation and testing but do not establish causality by representation alone.

### P5 — Implementation Must Be Distinguished From Intervention Effect

> Weak outcomes cannot be interpreted adequately without knowing whether and how the intervention was implemented.

### P6 — Causal Claims Require Counterfactual Discipline

> Observed change after action is insufficient evidence that the action caused the change.

### P7 — Contribution Can Be More Credible Than False Attribution

> In multi-causal organisational settings, a tested contribution claim may be more useful and honest than an unsupported claim of sole causation.

### P8 — Unintended and Unanticipated Differ

> Organisations should distinguish effects not intended from effects not anticipated because the governance and learning implications differ.

### P9 — Average Outcomes Can Conceal Harm

> Aggregate improvement should not be treated as responsible Organisational Intelligence where material subgroup or external harms remain hidden.

### P10 — Measurement Purpose Determines Measure Fitness

> No single performance measure will support control, accountability, learning, motivation and impact evaluation equally well.

### P11 — Validity Precedes Dashboarding

> A precise and timely metric is weak evidence where it does not represent the intended construct.

### P12 — Feedback Delay Distorts Learning

> Long, variable and hidden delays increase the probability that organisations will attribute outcomes to the wrong actions or overcorrect.

### P13 — Measures Are Organisational Interventions

> Material measures change behaviour, priorities and identity and should be evaluated for those effects.

### P14 — Target Pressure Can Corrupt Evidence

> The more consequential a weak proxy becomes for control or reward, the more likely it is to lose validity and distort the underlying process.

### P15 — Evaluation Requires Independence and Context

> Evaluation quality will decline where the evaluator is captured by delivery interests or detached from implementation reality.

### P16 — Evaluation Use Must Be Designed

> Credible findings do not influence organisational response automatically.

### P17 — Process and Outcome Quality Differ

> Good outcomes can follow weak processes and poor outcomes can follow reasonable decisions under uncertainty.

### P18 — Outcome Observation Should Be Multi-Method

> Quantitative, qualitative, operational and stakeholder evidence will often be necessary to understand organisational consequences.

### P19 — Feedback Completeness Is Bounded

> Organisations cannot observe every consequence but should make material exclusions, blind spots and stakeholder gaps explicit.

### P20 — AI Evaluation Must Match the Claim Boundary

> Model metrics support claims about models, while organisational outcome claims require evaluation of the complete human–AI sociotechnical system.

### P21 — Post-Deployment AI Evaluation Is Necessary

> Consequential AI outcomes may change through model updates, data drift, human adaptation and organisational context even where the nominal use case remains unchanged.

### P22 — AI Self-Evaluation Is Insufficient for Consequential Use

> A system should not be the sole judge of the outcome or safety of its own material actions.

### P23 — Evaluation Should Preserve Decision-Time Expectations

> Outcome review should compare actual consequence with the purpose, assumptions and predictions recorded before the outcome was known.

### P24 — Evaluation Quality Shapes Learning Quality

> Organisational learning will be weak where outcome evidence is invalid, delayed, distorted, politically filtered or causally overclaimed.

### P25 — Evaluation Is a Bridge Capability

> Outcomes, feedback and evaluation connect mobilisation to learning but do not themselves constitute durable organisational learning.

## 26. Observable Indicators

Potential indicators for later research include:

### Outcome Definition

- material interventions have explicit outcome statements;
- output and outcome are distinct;
- and stakeholder and time horizon are named.

### Theory and Assumptions

- theory of change exists;
- critical assumptions are visible;
- and negative pathways are considered.

### Implementation

- adoption;
- fidelity;
- adaptation;
- feasibility;
- and actual exposure

are known.

### Baseline and Comparison

- baseline is preserved;
- comparator is justified;
- and definitions remain comparable.

### Measurement Quality

- validity and reliability are reviewed;
- data provenance exists;
- missingness is visible;
- and metric definitions are stable.

### Stakeholder Coverage

- outcomes are disaggregated where material;
- affected groups can contribute evidence;
- and externalities are considered.

### Feedback Timing

- delay from action to consequence and observation is understood;
- and review cadence matches the mechanism.

### Causal Discipline

- descriptive, contribution and attribution claims are distinguished;
- alternative explanations are documented;
- and confidence is explicit.

### Metric Behaviour

- gaming;
- classification change;
- target pressure;
- and work displacement

are monitored.

### Evaluation Governance

- evaluator independence;
- evaluation use;
- decision deadline;
- and contestability

are visible.

### AI Evaluation

- model and version are traceable;
- workflow and human effects are measured;
- subgroup outcomes are examined;
- and post-deployment drift is monitored.

### Learning Handoff

- evaluation package includes:

  - purpose;
  - expectation;
  - implementation;
  - outcome;
  - uncertainty;
  - and unresolved question

for Chapter 8.

These remain candidate evidence categories.

They should not be collapsed into one evaluation-maturity score without empirical validation.

## 27. Practical Principles

### 27.1 Name the Outcome Before Acting

Do not wait until after implementation to decide what success means.

### 27.2 Separate Input, Activity, Output and Outcome

Use precise terms.

### 27.3 Identify Who Experiences the Outcome

Include affected stakeholders and excluded groups.

### 27.4 State the Time Horizon

Short-term and long-term outcomes may conflict.

### 27.5 Record the Theory of Change

Make mechanism and assumptions testable.

### 27.6 Preserve the Baseline

Do not rely on retrospective memory.

### 27.7 Match Causal Claim to Design

Use contribution language where attribution is not supported.

### 27.8 Measure Implementation

Do not interpret outcome without knowing what was delivered and used.

### 27.9 Use Multiple Evidence Forms

Metrics alone may miss mechanism, experience and harm.

### 27.10 Disaggregate Material Outcomes

Averages can conceal inequality.

### 27.11 Track Unintended and Negative Consequences

Do not monitor only the expected success path.

### 27.12 Inspect the Data-Generating Process

Metrics inherit incentives and omissions.

### 27.13 Treat Measures as Interventions

Expect behavioural response.

### 27.14 Do Not Turn Weak Proxies Into High-Stakes Targets

Reduce Goodhart-type and Campbell-type distortion.

### 27.15 Use Different Measures for Different Purposes

Control, accountability and learning require different evidence.

### 27.16 Preserve Definitions and Provenance

A trend is meaningless where the measure changed silently.

### 27.17 Represent Feedback Delay

Avoid crediting the most recent action automatically.

### 27.18 Make Evaluation Independent Enough to Challenge

Avoid both capture and context-free judgement.

### 27.19 Design for Use

State who will use the evaluation and which decision it supports.

### 27.20 Separate Process, Implementation and Outcome Judgement

Do not compress them into one score.

### 27.21 Evaluate Human–AI Systems in Context

Model accuracy is not organisational outcome.

### 27.22 Preserve Model and workflow Identity

Consequential AI evidence must remain attributable.

### 27.23 Use Independent Evaluation for Material AI Effects

Avoid sole self-assessment.

### 27.24 State Uncertainty and Blind Spots

Credibility increases when limits remain visible.

### 27.25 Hand Evaluation Into Learning

Do not let the report become the terminal state.

## 28. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Outcomes, feedback and evaluation form the bridge from **Mobilise** to **Improve**.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Outcome observability
× measurement integrity
× evaluative credibility
× feedback usability
= learning potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Execution Without Outcome Observation

```text
The organisation acts
→ consequences remain invisible
→ response cannot be evaluated
```

### Outcome Without Baseline

```text
A condition changes
→ no credible comparison exists
→ causal interpretation is weak
```

### Measurement Without Validity

```text
The metric is precise
→ it represents the wrong construct
→ organisational confidence increases while understanding falls
```

### Evaluation Without Independence

```text
The owner judges the intervention
→ inconvenient evidence is filtered
→ accountability and learning weaken
```

### AI Evaluation Without System Context

```text
The model scores well
→ human behaviour and stakeholder outcomes are absent
→ organisational risk remains unknown
```

### Feedback Without Learning Handoff

```text
The report is completed
→ evidence and assumptions are not integrated into memory
→ future behaviour remains unchanged
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when it can observe the material consequences of its response, evaluate those consequences with proportionate causal and measurement discipline, preserve affected perspectives and uncertainty, and transfer credible feedback into the processes that revise future understanding and action.**

## 29. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises programme evaluation, causal inference, implementation science, performance measurement, sociology of quantification, system dynamics and emerging AI evaluation;
- does not establish a validated Project OI evaluation construct;
- does not prove that the proposed Outcomes, Feedback and Evaluation Process is complete;
- and does not provide one universal evaluation method.

### 29.1 Outcome Diversity

Customer, employee, financial, operational, safety, social and environmental outcomes require different evidence.

### 29.2 Causal Inference

Strong causal inference may be:

- unethical;
- infeasible;
- or disproportionate

for some organisational decisions.

### 29.3 Contribution Analysis

A credible contribution story remains judgement and can be biased by selective evidence.

### 29.4 Theory of Change

Outcome pathways may become:

- overly linear;
- retrospective;
- or politically convenient.

### 29.5 Measurement Validity

Many organisational constructs are difficult to measure directly.

### 29.6 Goodhart and Campbell Effects

The likelihood and severity of metric corruption depend on:

- stakes;
- controllability;
- observability;
- and organisational culture.

### 29.7 Stakeholder Value Conflict

No evaluation method can eliminate normative disagreement about which outcome matters.

### 29.8 Evaluation Use

Useful evaluation may still be rejected because of:

- power;
- timing;
- identity;
- and organisational interest.

### 29.9 Outcome Bias

Evaluators may judge the process through known consequences even when instructed not to.

### 29.10 AI Evaluation

AI systems, models and deployment conditions continue to change rapidly.

Current testing and evaluation guidance is evolving.

### 29.11 NIST AI RMF Status

The AI RMF is voluntary, and NIST states that version 1.0 is being revised as of 2026.[^16]

### 29.12 ARIA

ARIA is an emerging programme developing sociotechnical testing approaches, not a completed universal standard.[^17]

### 29.13 Data Availability

Organisations often measure what systems already record, producing systematic blind spots.

### 29.14 Learning Boundary

This chapter produces evaluative feedback.

It does not establish that the organisation will:

- remember;
- reflect;
- revise;
- or institutionalise

that feedback.
