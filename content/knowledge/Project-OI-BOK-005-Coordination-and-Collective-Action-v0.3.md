## 1. Purpose

This chapter examines **coordination and collective action**: how an organisation turns an authorised decision into aligned, interdependent work across people, teams, systems, partners and AI.

Its central question is:

> **How does an organisation transform a legitimate commitment into coherent collective action while preserving context, authority, accountability and the ability to adapt?**

The previous chapters established that an organisation must:

1. notice material signals;
2. direct attention;
3. assemble relevant context;
4. locate and integrate knowledge and expertise;
5. form a useful orientation;
6. and make an attributable and authorised decision.

A decision does not execute itself.

The organisation must still:

- translate the decision into shared commitments;
- identify the work required;
- expose dependencies;
- allocate roles, resources and authority;
- coordinate timing and sequence;
- preserve context across handoffs;
- maintain a usable view of current state;
- manage shared resources and conflicting demands;
- repair coordination when the situation changes;
- and integrate distributed contributions into one organisational response.

```text
Signal and experience
        ↓
Attention
        ↓
Organisational context
        ↓
Knowledge and expertise
        ↓
Organisational orientation
        ↓
Decision and authority
        ↓
Coordination and collective action
        ↓
Execution, consequence and feedback
```

This chapter seeks to establish:

- what coordination is;
- how coordination differs from communication, collaboration, cooperation, orchestration and execution;
- how collective action emerges from interdependent contributions;
- how decisions become shared commitments;
- how dependencies shape organisational design;
- how plans, standards, roles, routines, schedules, meetings, shared representations and mutual adjustment coordinate work;
- how handoffs preserve context, evidence, authority and unresolved conditions;
- how work crosses professional, team, organisational and technological boundaries;
- how coordination changes under uncertainty, urgency and surprise;
- how local autonomy remains aligned with organisational purpose;
- how people and AI coordinate without hiding authority or accountability;
- which failure modes prevent organisations from acting coherently on what they know;
- and how coordination capability contributes to Organisational Intelligence.

## 2. Core Finding

The central finding is:

> **Coordination is not the volume of communication or the allocation of tasks. It is the organisational capability to manage dependencies among interdependent contributions so that authorised work remains mutually intelligible, appropriately timed, accountable, adaptable and capable of producing one coherent response.**

This creates several distinctions:

```text
Communication
        ≠
Coordination
        ≠
Collaboration
        ≠
Collective action
        ≠
Execution
        ≠
Outcome
```

People can communicate continuously while:

- working from different assumptions;
- competing for the same resource;
- waiting on invisible dependencies;
- duplicating effort;
- acting under incompatible authority;
- or producing outputs that cannot be integrated.

Tasks can be assigned while:

- prerequisites remain unmet;
- the receiver lacks context;
- the handoff strips away rationale;
- several actors believe someone else owns the dependency;
- or local completion damages the wider outcome.

An organisation can be busy without acting collectively.

The Project OI position is:

> **Coordination capability is the organisational capacity to make interdependence visible, establish the conditions required for aligned contribution, manage variation and exception, and connect distributed work into a legitimate organisational outcome.**

## 3. Working Definitions

### 3.1 Activity

An **activity** is a unit of work performed by a person, team, system, workflow, partner or AI.

An activity may be independent or interdependent.

### 3.2 Task

A **task** is a bounded activity with:

- purpose;
- expected output;
- owner;
- constraints;
- and completion condition.

Completing a task does not prove that the wider dependency has been satisfied.

### 3.3 Dependency

A **dependency** exists where the performance, timing, resources, state, output or legitimacy of one activity affects another.

Malone and Crowston define coordination as managing dependencies among activities.[^1]

Common dependencies include:

- shared resources;
- producer–consumer relationships;
- prerequisites;
- simultaneity;
- task–subtask relationships;
- shared customers;
- common reputation;
- shared authority;
- information;
- and outcome integration.

### 3.4 Interdependence

**Interdependence** is the condition in which two or more actors or activities affect one another’s ability to perform or achieve the intended outcome.

Interdependence may be:

- pooled;
- sequential;
- reciprocal;
- intensive;
- temporal;
- resource-based;
- informational;
- interpretive;
- or authority-based.

### 3.5 Coordination

Project OI defines **coordination** as:

> **The ongoing management of dependencies among authorised and interdependent contributions so that actors can understand how their work connects, anticipate relevant actions, adjust when conditions change and integrate their outputs into a coherent organisational response.**

The definition combines:

- dependency management;
- interaction;
- shared conditions;
- authority;
- adaptation;
- and outcome integration.

### 3.6 Coordination Mechanism

A **coordination mechanism** is a structure, practice, artefact, relationship or rule used to manage one or more dependencies.

Examples:

- plans;
- schedules;
- standards;
- roles;
- meetings;
- routines;
- workflow rules;
- queues;
- shared representations;
- integrators;
- liaison roles;
- escalation paths;
- and mutual adjustment.

### 3.7 Integrative Condition

An **integrative condition** is an organisational condition that allows coordination mechanisms to produce coordinated activity.

Okhuysen and Bechky identify three:

- accountability;
- predictability;
- and common understanding.[^2]

### 3.8 Accountability

Within coordination, **accountability** means that actors can identify:

- who is responsible for which contribution;
- what others may expect;
- and where explanation or correction is required.

This use complements the broader actor–forum accountability defined in OI-BOK-004.

### 3.9 Predictability

**Predictability** is the ability to form sufficiently reliable expectations about:

- who will act;
- when;
- under which conditions;
- and with which output.

Predictability does not require the environment to be deterministic.

It requires the organisation to make relevant commitments, states and variations visible.

### 3.10 Common Understanding

**Common understanding** is sufficient shared comprehension of:

- the objective;
- relevant roles;
- dependencies;
- state;
- vocabulary;
- and constraints

to allow interdependent action.

It does not require identical beliefs or complete consensus.

### 3.11 Shared Commitment

A **shared commitment** is the sufficiently distributed understanding that:

- a decision is effective;
- specified actors have obligations;
- dependencies must be managed;
- and the organisation will support the resulting coordinated response.

Shared commitment can exist despite disagreement with the decision.

### 3.12 Collective Action

Project OI defines **collective action** as:

> **The connected and mutually adjusted contributions of multiple actors or systems toward an accepted organisational purpose or authorised commitment.**

Collective action is more than aggregate activity.

The contributions must be sufficiently connected that the overall result depends on their relationship.

### 3.13 Cooperation

**Cooperation** is the willingness of actors to support one another or contribute toward compatible aims.

Cooperation can make coordination easier.

Cooperative intent does not itself manage dependencies.

### 3.14 Collaboration

**Collaboration** is joint work in which participants contribute to a shared activity, problem or output.

Collaboration often involves direct interaction and co-creation.

Coordination can occur without intensive collaboration through standards, roles, interfaces or schedules.

### 3.15 Communication

**Communication** is the exchange or representation of information, meaning or intent.

Communication is one coordination mechanism.

More communication is not necessarily better coordination.

### 3.16 Orchestration

**Orchestration** is the deliberate arrangement and direction of several activities, actors or capabilities toward a desired sequence or outcome.

It usually implies a coordinating actor or mechanism.

Coordination may also emerge through mutual adjustment without one orchestrator.

### 3.17 Execution

**Execution** is the performance of authorised work.

Execution is an input to collective action.

Execution quality can be high locally while coordination quality is poor across the system.

### 3.18 Alignment

**Alignment** is the degree to which objectives, contributions, authority and incentives are mutually compatible.

Alignment reduces some coordination demands.

It does not eliminate dependencies or change.

### 3.19 Role

A **role** is an expected pattern of contribution, responsibility, authority and relationship within work.

Roles help actors anticipate one another.

A job title is not a complete role definition.

### 3.20 Interface

An **interface** is the governed point at which actors, activities, systems or organisational units exchange:

- information;
- work;
- resources;
- authority;
- or outputs.

Interfaces can be human, organisational or technical.

### 3.21 Handoff

A **handoff** is the transfer of primary responsibility, work state or next action from one actor or mechanism to another.

A handoff is not simply a notification.

It must preserve enough context and authority for the receiving actor to continue responsibly.

### 3.22 Synchronisation

**Synchronisation** is the alignment of timing, state or readiness among interdependent activities.

### 3.23 Sequencing

**Sequencing** is the ordering of dependent activities.

### 3.24 Mutual Adjustment

**Mutual adjustment** is coordination through ongoing reciprocal communication and adaptation among actors.

It is especially important where work cannot be specified fully in advance.

### 3.25 Explicit Coordination

**Explicit coordination** uses deliberate communication, assignment, plans, schedules or commands to align work.

### 3.26 Implicit Coordination

**Implicit coordination** occurs where actors anticipate one another’s needs and adjust without direct requests at every step.

It depends on sufficiently accurate shared or compatible knowledge of:

- the task;
- team;
- situation;
- and expected actions.[^3]

### 3.27 Boundary Object

A **boundary object** is an artefact that retains enough common identity to support coordination across groups while allowing legitimate local interpretation.

### 3.28 Boundary Spanner

A **boundary spanner** is an actor or role that connects groups, translates across domains, manages dependencies or represents one unit to another.

### 3.29 Shared State

**Shared state** is a sufficiently current and inspectable representation of:

- work progress;
- dependencies;
- decisions;
- exceptions;
- and relevant conditions.

It is not necessarily one database or screen.

### 3.30 Exception

Within coordination, an **exception** is a condition in which the ordinary coordination mechanism no longer fits the situation.

### 3.31 Repair

**Coordination repair** is the process of restoring coherent action after:

- surprise;
- failure;
- changed conditions;
- missing information;
- or broken dependency.

### 3.32 Coordination Load

Project OI uses **coordination load** as a working term for the time, attention and interaction required to manage dependencies.

Coordination load may be necessary.

Excessive load can reduce the capacity available for substantive work.

### 3.33 Coordination Debt

Project OI uses **coordination debt** as a working term for accumulated unresolved dependencies, unclear ownership, brittle workarounds and inconsistent interfaces that make future collective action harder and more expensive.

The term requires later comparison and operationalisation.

### 3.34 Local Optimisation

**Local optimisation** occurs where an actor, team or system improves its own measure or completion state while degrading the wider organisational outcome.

### 3.35 Coordination Failure

A **coordination failure** occurs where interdependent contributions do not combine as required because dependencies, roles, information, timing, authority, adjustment or integration are inadequate.

## 4. Communication Is Not Coordination

Communication can support coordination by creating:

- awareness;
- common understanding;
- predictability;
- and repair.

It can also create noise, delay and ambiguity.

### High Communication, Weak Coordination

A team may have:

- daily meetings;
- active chat channels;
- extensive status reports;
- and frequent notifications

while still failing to identify:

- which task is blocked;
- whose output is required;
- what decision changed;
- which version is current;
- or who owns the exception.

### Low Communication, Strong Coordination

A mature team may coordinate through:

- stable roles;
- clear interfaces;
- shared state;
- predictable routines;
- and implicit adjustment.

This can reduce the need for constant explicit communication.

### Communication Quality

Gittell’s relational-coordination model emphasises communication that is:

- frequent;
- timely;
- accurate;
- and oriented toward problem solving,

supported by:

- shared goals;
- shared knowledge;
- and mutual respect.[^4]

The important property is not volume.

It is whether communication enables interdependent work.

### Project OI Principle

> **Communication should be evaluated by the dependencies it resolves and the coordinated action it enables, not by message volume or meeting frequency.**

## 5. Task Assignment Is Not Coordination

Assigning a task can establish responsibility.

It does not necessarily establish:

- readiness;
- dependencies;
- authority;
- resources;
- integration;
- or acceptable completion.

### Example

A product team assigns Legal to review a customer-consent change.

The task record may include:

- owner;
- due date;
- and document.

Coordination still fails if:

- the product decision is not explicit;
- Legal receives only the wording rather than the complete customer flow;
- Engineering proceeds before review;
- Marketing uses an earlier version;
- the relevant jurisdiction is missing;
- or nobody knows whether Legal advice is review input or approval authority.

### Task Completion Versus Dependency Satisfaction

A task can be marked complete while the receiving activity remains blocked.

The relevant test is:

> **Did the output satisfy the dependency for the next actor and the wider organisational outcome?**

### Project OI Principle

> **Work assignment should make the dependency, expected interface and integration condition visible—not only the local task.**

## 6. Collective Action Is Connected Contribution

An organisation does not act collectively merely because many people are active.

Collective action requires that contributors understand or reliably enact how their work relates to a wider system.

Weick and Roberts describe collective mind as a pattern of **heedful interrelating** in which actors:

- contribute their own actions;
- represent the system of connected actions;
- and subordinate or adapt their contribution to that system.[^5]

The term “subordinate” does not require passive obedience.

It means that actors shape their local work with awareness of:

- others’ contributions;
- the larger objective;
- and the consequences of their action for the system.

### Heedless Activity

```text
Each actor completes the assigned task
→ dependencies are treated as someone else’s concern
→ outputs conflict or fail to integrate
```

### Heedful Collective Action

```text
Each actor performs a distinct contribution
→ understands relevant dependencies
→ anticipates effects on others
→ adjusts when the shared situation changes
→ contributes to one coherent outcome
```

### Collective Action Conditions

Project OI proposes that collective action depends on at least:

- accepted purpose or commitment;
- visible interdependence;
- role and authority clarity;
- sufficient common understanding;
- predictable contribution;
- usable shared state;
- adaptation and repair;
- and outcome integration.

This is a working synthesis, not a validated universal model.

## 7. From Decision to Shared Commitment

An authorised decision creates an organisational commitment.

Coordination requires that commitment to become usable by the actors who must implement it.

### The Decision Translation Problem

The decision-maker may know:

- why the decision was made;
- what alternatives were rejected;
- which conditions apply;
- and which risks remain.

The implementation group may receive only:

- a task;
- deadline;
- or summary statement.

The decision loses:

- rationale;
- constraints;
- authority;
- and review conditions

as it moves into work.

### A Coordination-Ready Decision Should Include

```text
Decision
Purpose
Scope
Effective time
Decision owner
Authority
Action owner
Expected outcome
Dependencies
Resources
Conditions
Exceptions
Stop conditions
Review point
Communication obligations
```

### Shared Commitment Does Not Mean Consensus

Participants may disagree with the decision.

They need to understand:

- that it is legitimate;
- what obligations follow;
- which concerns remain open;
- and how future review or appeal works.

### Commitment Failure

A decision may be valid but lack collective commitment because:

- affected actors were not informed;
- the rationale is not credible;
- dependencies are impossible;
- authority is contested;
- or incentives reward different outcomes.

### Project OI Principle

> **Coordination begins when a legitimate decision becomes a sufficiently shared and actionable commitment.**

## 8. Theoretical Foundations

### 8.1 Coordination as Dependency Management

Malone and Crowston’s coordination theory defines coordination as managing dependencies among activities.[^1]

Their framework identifies dependencies such as:

- shared resources;
- producer–consumer relationships;
- simultaneity constraints;
- and task–subtask relationships.

The value of this view is that coordination becomes diagnosable.

Instead of asking:

> Why are the teams not communicating?

the organisation can ask:

> Which dependency exists, and which mechanism currently manages it?

### 8.2 Coordination and Process Design

Crowston applies coordination theory to process design, arguing that process alternatives can be explored by identifying dependencies and the mechanisms used to manage them.[^6]

This is particularly relevant to AI and automation.

Automating an activity without understanding its dependencies can:

- accelerate local work;
- increase resource contention;
- remove human repair;
- or produce outputs that downstream actors cannot use.

### 8.3 Interdependence

Thompson’s classic model distinguishes:

- pooled;
- sequential;
- and reciprocal interdependence.[^7]

Later work extends coordination to more intensive and complex forms.

The central insight remains:

> **Different interdependence structures require different coordination.**

### 8.4 Coordination Modes

Van de Ven, Delbecq and Koenig examine impersonal, personal and group modes of coordination and show that coordination demands change with work characteristics such as uncertainty and interdependence.[^8]

Adler’s study of the design–manufacturing interface distinguishes:

- standards;
- schedules;
- mutual adjustment;
- and teams,

with the appropriate mix changing across phases and types of uncertainty.[^9]

### 8.5 Integrative Conditions

Okhuysen and Bechky review coordination mechanisms and propose that they work by creating:

- accountability;
- predictability;
- and common understanding.[^2]

This is one of the strongest organising frameworks for this chapter.

A meeting is not coordinating merely because it occurs.

It coordinates where it improves one or more of those conditions.

### 8.6 Relational Coordination

Gittell’s research shows that coordination can be relational as well as structural.

Boundary spanners, routines and team meetings can strengthen coordination through shared goals, shared knowledge, mutual respect and high-quality communication.[^4]

Routines do not necessarily substitute for interaction.

They may improve the quality of interaction.

### 8.7 Fast-Response Coordination

Faraj and Xiao’s trauma-centre study distinguishes:

- expertise coordination practices;
- and dialogic coordination practices.[^10]

Expertise coordination includes:

- protocols;
- community-of-practice structuring;
- plug-and-play teaming;
- and knowledge sharing.

Dialogic coordination includes:

- epistemic contestation;
- joint sensemaking;
- cross-boundary intervention;
- and protocol breaking.

The implication is not that high reliability comes from following or breaking protocol.

It comes from knowing which mode the situation requires and coordinating the transition.

### 8.8 Heedful Interrelating

Weick and Roberts argue that reliable collective performance depends on how heedfully actors connect their contributions to the system of actions around them.[^5]

Coordination is therefore both structural and enacted.

### 8.9 Role-Based Coordination

Bechky’s study of film crews shows that temporary organisations can coordinate through durable role systems, visible work and local negotiation even where the team itself is temporary.[^11]

Roles carry expectations across projects.

They are also enacted and corrected in the local situation.

### 8.10 Implicit Coordination and Team Cognition

Rico and colleagues model implicit coordination through team situation models that help members anticipate needs and adjust without explicit requests at every step.[^3]

Implicit coordination can reduce communication overhead.

It depends on sufficient accuracy and shared experience.

### 8.11 Knowledge Boundaries

Carlile identifies syntactic, semantic and pragmatic knowledge boundaries and corresponding processes of:

- transfer;
- translation;
- and transformation.[^12]

Cross-boundary coordination fails where organisations treat a pragmatic conflict of interests and consequences as a simple information-transfer problem.

### 8.12 Distributed Work

Hinds and Bailey explain why geographical distribution and technology-mediated interaction can increase task, process and relationship conflict in distributed teams.[^13]

Distance affects:

- awareness;
- context;
- timing;
- interpretation;
- and repair.

### 8.13 Human–AI Teaming

Dell’Acqua and colleagues’ field experiment with 791 professionals at Procter & Gamble found that individuals using generative AI matched the performance of human teams without AI on realistic product-innovation tasks. AI also reduced functional differences between commercial and technical specialists and changed participants’ reported emotional experience.[^14]

The later peer-reviewed version was published in 2026.

The result demonstrates that AI can reproduce some benefits of human collaboration in one setting.

It does not establish that AI can own organisational authority, coordinate every task type or replace human teams generally.

## 9. Types of Interdependence

### 9.1 Pooled Interdependence

Actors contribute separately to a shared organisational result.

Examples:

- branches contributing revenue;
- researchers contributing findings to one programme;
- or teams using one shared brand.

Direct workflow dependency may be low.

Coordination is still required for:

- shared standards;
- resources;
- reputation;
- and aggregate outcome.

### 9.2 Sequential Interdependence

One activity produces an input required by another.

Examples:

- sales handoff to delivery;
- design before build;
- approval before release;
- or evidence collection before decision.

Key coordination needs include:

- readiness;
- sequencing;
- quality criteria;
- notification;
- and acceptance.

### 9.3 Reciprocal Interdependence

Actors exchange inputs and adjust repeatedly.

Examples:

- product and engineering;
- clinician and diagnostic team;
- researcher and statistician;
- or customer service and billing during an exception.

Mutual adjustment becomes more important.

### 9.4 Intensive Interdependence

Several specialists interact dynamically around one case, incident or problem.

Examples:

- emergency response;
- complex customer resolution;
- security incident;
- or strategic design.

The required order and contribution cannot be specified completely in advance.

### 9.5 Shared-Resource Interdependence

Actors require the same constrained:

- person;
- budget;
- machine;
- environment;
- data source;
- model quota;
- or approval capacity.

Resource allocation becomes a coordination mechanism.

### 9.6 Temporal Interdependence

Activities must occur:

- before;
- after;
- at the same time;
- within a window;
- or at a particular cadence.

### 9.7 Informational Interdependence

One actor’s work depends on information held or produced by another.

### 9.8 Interpretive Interdependence

Actors depend on one another to create a usable interpretation.

### 9.9 Authority Interdependence

Work depends on:

- approval;
- delegation;
- veto;
- or authorisation

held elsewhere.

### 9.10 Outcome Interdependence

Separate contributions affect one shared outcome.

Local success may not translate into system success.

### Interdependence Mapping

For material work, ask:

```text
What must be shared?
What must happen first?
What must happen together?
Who needs whose output?
Which resources are constrained?
Which interpretation must be integrated?
Which authority is external to the task?
Which outcome connects the actors?
```

## 10. Coordination Mechanisms

No single mechanism is universally superior.

The appropriate combination depends on:

- interdependence;
- novelty;
- uncertainty;
- scale;
- speed;
- and consequence.

### 10.1 Standards

Standards define:

- formats;
- quality;
- interfaces;
- thresholds;
- and expected practice.

They reduce the need for repeated negotiation.

They can become rigid or stale.

### 10.2 Plans

Plans specify:

- objectives;
- activities;
- sequence;
- ownership;
- and expected results.

They create predictability.

They are resources for action, not complete substitutes for situated adjustment.

### 10.3 Schedules

Schedules coordinate timing and resource use.

They can create false certainty where readiness and variation are hidden.

### 10.4 Roles

Roles establish expected contributions and relationships.

They enable both explicit and implicit coordination.

### 10.5 Routines

Routines encode recurring patterns of action.

They can improve reliability and learning.

They can also preserve obsolete assumptions.

### 10.6 Meetings

Meetings can:

- build common understanding;
- resolve dependencies;
- integrate knowledge;
- and make commitments visible.

They can also become status theatre.

### 10.7 Shared Representations

Examples:

- plans;
- boards;
- maps;
- models;
- case files;
- timelines;
- and dashboards.

They coordinate where they preserve a sufficiently shared view of state and relationships.

### 10.8 Liaison and Integrator Roles

Boundary spanners, project leads, product managers, incident commanders and similar roles can connect specialised units.

They become bottlenecks where every dependency must pass through one person.

### 10.9 workflow and Automation

workflows can encode:

- sequence;
- assignment;
- waits;
- approvals;
- and exception paths.

Automation can remove coordination effort.

It can also remove human repair and hide dependencies.

### 10.10 Queues and Markets

Queues, priorities, budgets and bidding can allocate scarce resources.

They embed value and fairness decisions.

### 10.11 Mutual Adjustment

Actors coordinate through direct reciprocal adaptation.

It is flexible and context-sensitive.

It can produce high communication load and weak traceability.

### 10.12 Escalation

Escalation transfers a dependency or exception to a route with greater:

- authority;
- expertise;
- or capacity.

### 10.13 Redundancy

Overlap, backup and cross-checking can improve resilience.

Excess redundancy creates duplication and ambiguity.

## 11. Accountability, Predictability and Common Understanding

Okhuysen and Bechky’s three integrative conditions provide a practical way to assess coordination mechanisms.[^2]

### 11.1 Accountability

Actors should know:

- what they own;
- what contribution is expected;
- who depends on it;
- and where failure or change must be reported.

Accountability is weakened by:

- shared ownership;
- vague roles;
- hidden work;
- and unrecorded delegation.

### 11.2 Predictability

Actors need enough expectation to plan and adjust.

Predictability can come from:

- routines;
- stable roles;
- schedules;
- interfaces;
- visible state;
- and reliable commitments.

Predictability is not the absence of surprise.

It includes predictable ways to respond to surprise.

### 11.3 Common Understanding

Actors need sufficient shared comprehension of:

- purpose;
- terms;
- dependencies;
- and current state.

Common understanding is weakened by:

- incompatible vocabularies;
- stale representations;
- and hidden assumptions.

### Mechanism Test

For every recurring coordination mechanism, ask:

```text
What accountability does this create?
What becomes predictable?
What common understanding does it establish?
Which dependency does it manage?
What happens when the mechanism no longer fits?
```

## 12. Roles, Visibility and Mutual Expectations

Roles reduce coordination load by allowing people to anticipate:

- who does what;
- which questions belong where;
- and what quality or behaviour to expect.

Bechky’s film-production research shows that role systems can support rapid coordination in temporary organisations because role expectations persist across projects and are negotiated in the local setting.[^11]

### Role Clarity

A useful role should identify:

- contribution;
- responsibility;
- authority;
- dependencies;
- escalation;
- and boundaries.

### Role Rigidity

Roles fail where:

- actors refuse necessary cross-boundary intervention;
- changing conditions make the role insufficient;
- or role protection becomes more important than outcome.

### Role Collision

Two actors may believe they own the same contribution.

### Role Vacuum

No actor owns the dependency.

### Work Visibility

Coordination improves where relevant activity and state are visible to those who depend on them.

Visibility should be:

- purpose-bound;
- permission-aware;
- and focused on coordination need.

Universal visibility can create surveillance and overload.

## 13. Shared State and Common Ground

Coordination requires some shared representation of what is happening.

### Shared State May Include

- current objective;
- decision;
- work status;
- dependencies;
- active blockers;
- resource state;
- exceptions;
- latest evidence;
- and next commitments.

### Shared Does Not Mean Identical

Different roles may need different views.

The underlying state and relationships should remain compatible.

### Common-Ground Failure

Two teams may use the same word differently.

Examples:

- done;
- approved;
- ready;
- risk;
- customer;
- or released.

The shared board displays agreement while the teams interpret the state differently.

### Boundary Objects

Carlile shows how boundary objects can support:

- representation;
- learning;
- and transformation

across specialised groups.[^12]

The artefact is useful where it makes consequences and differences workable.

It is not neutral automatically.

### Shared-State Drift

Shared-state drift occurs where:

- the representation remains unchanged;
- while the real work, assumptions or authority change.

## 14. Handoffs

Handoffs are high-risk coordination boundaries.

The sender possesses more context than the artefact usually communicates.

The receiver may see:

- output without rationale;
- task without purpose;
- status without uncertainty;
- approval without conditions;
- or evidence without interpretation.

### Handoff Integrity

Project OI uses **handoff integrity** as a working term for the degree to which a handoff preserves the information and conditions required for the receiving actor to continue responsibly.

A context-bearing handoff should include:

```text
Purpose
Decision or commitment
Completed work
Current state
Expected receiving contribution
Material rationale
Dependencies
Evidence
Known exceptions
Uncertainty
Authority
Next responsibility
Escalation
Review point
```

### Handoff Acceptance

The receiver should be able to:

- confirm readiness;
- reject incomplete input;
- identify a gap;
- or request clarification.

### Handoff Completion

The handoff is not complete when the sender transmits.

It is complete when the receiving dependency is accepted or explicitly failed.

### Handoff Repair

Where a handoff fails, the organisation should determine whether the cause was:

- source quality;
- interface;
- timing;
- role;
- authority;
- or shared-language failure.

## 15. Cross-Boundary Coordination

Organisational boundaries can be:

- functional;
- professional;
- hierarchical;
- geographical;
- organisational;
- contractual;
- cultural;
- or technological.

### Syntactic Boundary

Actors lack a shared language or format.

Coordination need:

- common syntax;
- standards;
- and transfer.

### Semantic Boundary

Actors interpret the same information differently.

Coordination need:

- translation;
- examples;
- and shared understanding.

### Pragmatic Boundary

Actors have different interests, consequences or priorities.

Coordination need:

- negotiation;
- transformation;
- authority;
- and adjustment.

Carlile’s framework distinguishes transfer, translation and transformation across these boundary types.[^12]

### Boundary-Spanning Risk

Boundary spanners can become:

- translators;
- negotiators;
- context carriers;
- and relationship builders.

They can also become:

- bottlenecks;
- hidden authorities;
- or single points of failure.

### Cross-Organisational Work

Partners may differ in:

- authority;
- incentives;
- vocabulary;
- data access;
- and accountability.

Coordination requires explicit interfaces and escalation rather than assumed internal norms.

## 16. Explicit and Implicit Coordination

### Explicit Coordination

Useful when:

- participants are unfamiliar;
- work is novel;
- consequences are high;
- or dependencies are changing.

Examples:

- direct request;
- assignment;
- briefing;
- checklist;
- status update;
- and command.

### Implicit Coordination

Useful when:

- roles are stable;
- shared experience is strong;
- the situation is familiar;
- and team situation models are accurate.[^3]

Examples:

- anticipating information needs;
- adjusting timing;
- offering backup;
- and reallocating work without request.

### Risks of Implicit Coordination

- assumptions remain untested;
- new members are excluded;
- context changes unnoticed;
- and silence is mistaken for agreement.

### Risks of Explicit Coordination

- communication overload;
- micromanagement;
- delay;
- and reduced initiative.

### Adaptive Mix

Teams should increase explicit coordination when:

- novelty rises;
- membership changes;
- shared state becomes uncertain;
- or failure consequences increase.

## 17. Coordination Under Urgency and Surprise

Urgency changes the cost of communication, delay and error.

### Protocols

Protocols create rapid predictability.

They are valuable for known conditions.

### Expertise Coordination

Faraj and Xiao identify practices that mobilise distributed expertise quickly.[^10]

### Dialogic Coordination

Novel events may require:

- contesting interpretations;
- joint sensemaking;
- cross-boundary intervention;
- and controlled protocol breaking.[^10]

### Surprise

Bechky and Okhuysen’s later work on SWAT teams and film crews shows how organisations use roles, routines, knowledge and resource recombination to respond to unexpected events.[^15]

### Emergency Coordination Guardrail

Emergency coordination should preserve:

- incident authority;
- shared state;
- role flexibility;
- evidence;
- and later review.

Urgency should not erase accountability.

## 18. Local Autonomy and Organisational Alignment

Coordination does not require central control of every action.

Local actors often possess the best context.

### Local Autonomy Is Useful Where

- variation is frequent;
- knowledge is local;
- response speed matters;
- and effects are bounded.

### Central Coordination Is Useful Where

- shared resources are constrained;
- consistency matters;
- consequences cross units;
- or local actions create common risk.

### Guardrails for Local Autonomy

Local authority should operate within:

- purpose;
- policy;
- scope;
- thresholds;
- evidence;
- and escalation.

### Local Optimisation

Examples:

- sales closes work delivery cannot perform;
- engineering improves speed while support complexity rises;
- one team clears its queue by sending incomplete work downstream;
- or an AI agent completes its assigned objective by consuming another team’s resource budget.

### Project OI Principle

> **Autonomy should be bounded by visible dependencies and shared organisational consequence, not merely local task completion.**

## 19. Coordination Load and Coordination Debt

Coordination consumes:

- attention;
- time;
- meetings;
- monitoring;
- and relationship work.

The objective is not to eliminate coordination cost.

It is to spend it where interdependence requires it.

### Coordination Load Increases With

- number of dependencies;
- uncertainty;
- role ambiguity;
- membership change;
- distance;
- resource scarcity;
- incompatible systems;
- and weak shared representations.

### Coordination Debt

Coordination debt can accumulate through:

- undocumented workarounds;
- duplicated systems;
- unclear ownership;
- hidden approval paths;
- brittle integrations;
- stale plans;
- and repeated manual repair.

### Symptoms

- more meetings with less clarity;
- repeated escalation;
- status chasing;
- founder intervention;
- inconsistent handoffs;
- duplicated work;
- and inability to explain why work is blocked.

### Reducing Coordination Debt

The organisation may:

- remove unnecessary dependencies;
- clarify ownership;
- standardise interfaces;
- improve shared state;
- redesign workflow;
- and retain learning from repeated repair.

## 20. Human–AI Coordination

AI can participate as:

- information source;
- drafter;
- planner;
- specialist assistant;
- recommendation system;
- coordinator;
- workflow actor;
- or autonomous AI agent within a bounded authority.

The coordination question is not only what the AI can do.

It is how its contribution connects to human and organisational work.

### 20.1 Role Clarity

The system should make visible:

- what the AI owns;
- what it proposes;
- what it may change;
- what requires human action;
- and when it must stop.

### 20.2 Shared State

Humans and AI need compatible representations of:

- objective;
- current state;
- commitments;
- permissions;
- tool outcomes;
- and unresolved conditions.

### 20.3 Handoff Integrity

AI-to-human and human-to-AI handoffs should preserve:

- purpose;
- evidence;
- work completed;
- uncertainty;
- authority;
- and next action.

### 20.4 Work Visibility

AI activity should be visible at the level required for coordination and audit.

Private chain-of-thought is not required.

### 20.5 AI as Cybernetic Teammate

The P&G field experiment suggests that AI can help individuals reproduce some benefits of human teamwork and bridge functional expertise in product-development tasks.[^14]

The result creates design opportunities:

- smaller teams may perform some work effectively with AI;
- AI may provide cross-functional perspectives;
- and experts may spend less time on routine integration.

It also creates risks:

- human diversity may be replaced by one model’s homogenising influence;
- role development may weaken;
- and apparent coordination may depend on the model’s hidden assumptions.

### 20.6 Human–AI Combination Is Not Automatically Synergistic

The meta-analysis reviewed in OI-BOK-003 found that human–AI combinations did not consistently outperform the better human-only or AI-only alternative.[^16]

Coordination design must allocate work according to:

- task fit;
- expertise;
- evidence;
- authority;
- and error mode.

### 20.7 Automation Surprise

Automation surprise occurs where the system acts:

- unexpectedly;
- under a different state;
- or beyond the user’s mental model.

### 20.8 Oversight Load

Adding human review to every AI action can create a new coordination bottleneck.

Oversight should be proportionate and supported by:

- evidence;
- exception signals;
- and clear authority.

### Multi-agent Failure Modes

- tool contention;

### Coordination Requirements

A multi-AI agent arrangement should identify:

- dependency graph;
- tool authority;
- output contracts;
- integration owner;
- budget;
- stop conditions;
- and human escalation.

### Project OI Guardrail

> **More AI agents do not create collective intelligence unless their dependencies, authority and integration are governed.**

- missed dependency;
- stale state;
- delay;
- conflicting output;
- role failure;
- resource contention;
- or changed condition.

### Containment

Prevent the failure from spreading.

### Reorientation

Update the shared understanding of:

- what happened;
- current state;
- and what matters now.

### Reallocation

Change:

- ownership;
- resources;
- timing;
- or authority.

### Recoordination

Select a new mechanism:

- direct command;
- mutual adjustment;
- emergency role;
- revised sequence;
- or escalation.

### Learning

Determine whether the repair should change:

- plan;
- workflow;
- interface;
- role;
- policy;
- or organisational memory.

### Repair Guardrail

Repeated successful improvisation can hide structural coordination debt.

## 23. Coordination and Collective Action Failure Modes

### 23.1 Communication Substitution

More messages or meetings are treated as proof of coordination.

### 23.2 Meeting Substitution

The issue is discussed repeatedly without commitments or dependency resolution.

### 23.3 Task-Assignment Substitution

Work is assigned without readiness, interface or integration criteria.

### 23.4 Activity Illusion

Visible activity is treated as collective progress.

### 23.5 Decision–Action Gap

A valid decision does not become actionable commitments.

### 23.6 Commitment Ambiguity

Actors do not know whether the decision is effective or provisional.

### 23.7 Invisible Dependency

Required relationships among activities remain unrepresented.

### 23.8 Wrong Interdependence Model

Sequential work is treated as independent, or intensive work is forced into a linear plan.

### 23.9 Ownership Vacuum

No actor owns the dependency.

### 23.10 Ownership Collision

Several actors believe they own the same contribution.

### 23.11 Shared-Responsibility Diffusion

Everyone is responsible, so nobody is accountable.

### 23.12 Authority Mismatch

The work owner lacks authority to resolve the dependency.

### 23.13 Role Rigidity

Actors follow role boundaries after the situation requires intervention.

### 23.14 Role Drift

Role expectations change without explicit coordination.

### 23.15 Resource Contention

Several activities depend on the same constrained resource without allocation rules.

### 23.16 Hidden Queue

Work waits in a person, system or approval path that is not visible.

### 23.17 Schedule Without Readiness

Timing is planned without confirming prerequisites.

### 23.18 State Drift

The shared representation no longer matches real work.

### 23.19 Version Divergence

Actors coordinate from different versions of the decision, plan or artefact.

### 23.20 Handoff Stripping

Context, rationale, evidence or uncertainty disappears at transfer.

### 23.21 Fire-and-Forget Handoff

The sender treats transmission as completion.

### 23.22 Translation Failure

Groups share information without reaching common meaning.

### 23.23 Pragmatic Boundary Denial

Different interests are treated as a vocabulary problem.

### 23.24 Local Optimisation

A unit improves its measure while degrading the system.

### 23.25 Coordination Overload

Actors spend excessive time managing dependencies manually.

### 23.26 Coordination Debt

Repeated workarounds make future coordination harder.

### 23.27 Brittle Standardisation

The standard works only under assumed conditions.

### 23.28 Protocol Rigidity

Actors follow protocol after the situation requires adaptation.

### 23.29 Premature Mutual Adjustment

Work relies on conversation where a stable interface or standard would be more reliable.

### 23.30 Implicit Coordination Overreach

Actors assume shared understanding that does not exist.

### 23.31 Escalation Vacuum

The current actor cannot resolve the dependency and no route exists.

### 23.32 Escalation Congestion

Every exception moves to one person or forum.

### 23.33 Exception Normalisation

Repeated exceptions become unrecorded ordinary practice.

### 23.34 Distributed-Team Drift

Distance and asynchronous work reduce awareness and common ground.

### 23.35 Relationship Deficit

Low trust or respect damages information exchange and adjustment.

### 23.36 Status Theatre

Reporting appears complete while blockers and dependency risk remain hidden.

### 23.37 Automation Surprise

Automated action violates human expectations of state or sequence.

### 23.38 AI Role Ambiguity

People cannot tell whether AI is advising, acting or waiting.

### 23.39 AI Handoff Failure

The model output lacks sources, uncertainty, work state or next responsibility.

### 23.40 AI agent Duplication

Several AI agents perform the same work without awareness.

### 23.41 AI agent Goal Conflict

AI agents optimise incompatible local objectives.

### 23.42 Tool Contention

Several automated actors compete for the same system, budget or credential.

### 23.43 Integration Failure

Local outputs are individually valid but cannot form one result.

### 23.44 Human-Oversight Bottleneck

Review load exceeds human capacity.

### 23.45 Accountability Diffusion

Nobody must explain the coordination failure.

### 23.46 Repair Without Learning

The organisation improvises successfully and repeats the same failure later.

### 23.47 Outcome Fragmentation

No actor or system can connect distributed activity to the final consequence.

## 28. Project OI Propositions

These are working propositions, not validated findings.

### P1 — Communication Is Not Coordination

> Communication contributes to coordination only where it improves accountability, predictability, common understanding or dependency management.

### P2 — Coordination Manages Interdependence

> Coordination requirements are produced by dependencies among activities, resources, interpretations, authority and outcomes.

### P3 — Different Interdependence Requires Different Mechanisms

> Coordination quality will decline where pooled, sequential, reciprocal or intensive work is managed as though the dependency structure were identical.

### P4 — Collective Action Requires Connected Contribution

> Aggregate activity becomes collective action only where contributions are oriented and adjusted to a wider system of action.

### P5 — Decisions Must Be Translated

> A legitimate decision will fail to mobilise coordinated work where commitments, dependencies, action ownership and review conditions remain implicit.

### P6 — Coordination Mechanisms Work Through Integrative Conditions

> Plans, meetings, routines, roles and schedules coordinate where they create accountability, predictability and common understanding.

### P7 — Roles Reduce Coordination Load

> Stable and visible role expectations allow actors to anticipate one another, but rigid roles can obstruct necessary intervention.

### P8 — Shared State Must Remain Purpose-Bound and Current

> A shared representation improves coordination only where it reflects material state and remains interpretable across roles.

### P9 — Handoffs Are Dependency Transactions

> A handoff is complete when the receiving dependency is accepted, not when the sender transmits an artefact.

### P10 — Common Understanding Does Not Require Uniform Interpretation

> Groups can coordinate through compatible views and shared objects while preserving legitimate local knowledge.

### P11 — Cross-Boundary Coordination May Require Transformation

> Information transfer will be insufficient where differences involve meaning, interests, consequences or authority.

### P12 — Implicit Coordination Is Conditional

> Implicit coordination improves performance where team situation models are sufficiently accurate and stable; it becomes dangerous under novelty, turnover and state ambiguity.

### P13 — Speed Can Depend on Better Coordination

> Fast collective action can use clear roles, current information, shared state and active adjustment rather than bypassing coordination.

### P14 — Local Autonomy Requires Dependency Visibility

> Local actors can exercise useful autonomy where the wider consequences, limits and escalation paths remain visible.

### P15 — Coordination Load Is a Design Cost

> Organisations should reduce unnecessary dependencies and use mechanisms proportionate to the remaining interdependence.

### P16 — Repeated Repair Can Conceal Coordination Debt

> Successful improvisation will not strengthen coordination where recurring causes are not incorporated into roles, interfaces or workflows.

### P17 — AI Can Reproduce Some Team Benefits

> AI may improve integration and cross-functional contribution for some tasks without becoming a substitute for authority, human diversity or organisational coordination generally.

### P18 — Human–AI Coordination Requires Shared State and Role Clarity

> AI participation will create coordination risk where humans cannot determine what the system knows, owns, changed or expects next.

### P19 — More AI agents Do Not Create Collective Intelligence

> Multi-agent performance depends on governed dependencies, context, authority and output integration rather than AI agent count.

### P20 — Coordination Must Connect to Outcome and Learning

> Coordination strengthens Organisational Intelligence where consequences improve future dependency design, role structure and shared memory.

## 30. Practical Principles

### 30.1 Start With the Authorised Commitment

Do not coordinate work around an ambiguous or provisional decision.

### 30.2 Map Dependencies, Not Only Tasks

Show what each activity needs and enables.

### 30.3 Match Mechanism to Interdependence

Do not force every type of work into one workflow or meeting cadence.

### 30.4 Make Ownership and Authority Distinct

The work owner may not hold authority to resolve every dependency.

### 30.5 Create Accountability, Predictability and Common Understanding

Use these conditions to evaluate coordination mechanisms.

### 30.6 Preserve a Usable Shared State

Do not coordinate from private or stale representations.

### 30.7 Define Interfaces

Specify:

- input;
- output;
- quality;
- readiness;
- acceptance;
- and failure behaviour.

### 30.8 Treat Handoffs as Two-Sided

The receiver must accept or reject the dependency.

### 30.9 Preserve Rationale and Uncertainty

Do not transfer only the visible output.

### 30.10 Use Roles to Reduce Load

Keep roles adaptable under exception.

### 30.11 Use Mutual Adjustment Where Specification Ends

Do not use conversation to replace stable interfaces unnecessarily.

### 30.12 Surface Shared-Resource Conflict

Queues and bottlenecks should not remain personal knowledge.

### 30.13 Protect Unique and Local Knowledge

Central coordination should not erase local context.

### 30.14 Make Escalation a Designed Route

Escalation should carry context, authority and response expectations.

### 30.15 Review Repeated Exceptions

They may indicate policy, role or workflow failure.

### 30.16 Monitor Coordination Debt

Repeated manual repair is architecture evidence.

### 30.17 Make AI Roles Explicit

State whether AI informs, drafts, coordinates, acts or waits.

### 30.18 Govern Multi-agent Dependencies

Assign integration ownership and stop conditions.

### 30.19 Separate Activity From Collective Progress

Measure dependency resolution and outcome integration.

### 30.20 Learn From Repair

Update the coordination system, not only the immediate work.

## 31. Relationship to Organisational Intelligence

Coordination converts authorised decisions into collective response.

The dependency can be represented as:

```text
Decision clarity
× dependency visibility
× integrative conditions
× adaptive capacity
= collective action potential
```

This is not a mathematical measurement model.

It expresses a dependency.

### Decision Without Coordination

```text
The organisation commits
→ work and dependencies remain unclear
→ implementation fragments
```

### Communication Without Coordination

```text
People exchange information
→ ownership, timing and integration remain unresolved
→ activity continues without coherence
```

### Execution Without Coordination

```text
Local tasks are completed
→ outputs conflict or arrive unusably
→ system outcome fails
```

### Coordination Without Adaptation

```text
The plan works under assumed conditions
→ surprise occurs
→ actors continue following the obsolete mechanism
```

### AI Without Coordination

```text
The model produces useful output
→ role, state, authority and handoff are unclear
→ humans repair the system manually
```

### Collective Action Without Learning

```text
The organisation responds
→ outcome and repair are not retained
→ future coordination cost remains unchanged
```

The higher-level Project OI principle is:

> **An organisation becomes more intelligent when it can convert legitimate decisions into interdependent action, preserve shared understanding and authority across boundaries, adapt when conditions change, and use outcomes to improve how future collective work is coordinated.**

## 32. Research Limitations

This chapter presently:

- synthesises organisation design, coordination theory, team cognition, relational coordination, boundary research and emerging human–AI studies;
- does not establish a validated Project OI coordination model;
- does not prove that the proposed Coordination and Collective Action Process is complete;
- and does not provide a universal method for selecting coordination mechanisms.

### Conceptual Breadth

Coordination appears in:

- economics;
- organisation theory;
- computer science;
- operations;
- sociology;
- and team research.

Definitions differ.

### Level of Analysis

Coordination among:

- two people;
- a team;
- several teams;
- organisations;
- and distributed technical agents

cannot be treated as identical.

### Power and Politics

Dependency management is not neutral.

Actors may use:

- information;
- roles;
- standards;
- and interfaces

to preserve power or shift burden.

### Informal Work

Formal coordination models may miss:

- relationship work;
- emotional labour;
- tacit repair;
- and invisible contribution.

### Culture

Roles, hierarchy, communication and mutual adjustment vary across cultures.

### Measurement

Coordination quality is often inferred from performance, communication or self-report.

These are imperfect substitutes.

### Causality

Good coordination can improve outcomes.

Successful outcomes can also strengthen relationships and perceived coordination.

### AI Evidence

The cybernetic-teammate study examines one firm and one product-innovation setting. Its findings do not establish general coordination superiority for AI across work.

### Multi-agent Systems

Technical multi-AI agent coordination research is extensive, but organisational equivalence should not be assumed.

### Ethics

A highly coordinated organisation can execute harmful objectives effectively.

Coordination quality does not establish the legitimacy of the purpose.
