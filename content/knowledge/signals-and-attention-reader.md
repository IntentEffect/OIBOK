# Signals and attention

On Monday, a customer accepts a quote by email. On Thursday, she rings to ask when someone is coming. Sales can show when the acceptance arrived. The scheduler can show that the job never appeared in the booking queue. Both accounts can be true. Between the inbox and the queue, a piece of news that mattered to the customer stopped travelling.

This fictional example asks a question you can take into your own workplace. How would you know when an event had received enough attention for someone to decide what it meant and what to do next?

## Follow the signal

Start with what the customer did. She accepted the quote. The time stamp is a record of that event. For sales, the acceptance is a *signal* that the job is ready to move. For scheduling, the same email might still leave an important question open. Does the customer need a particular date? Has anyone checked site access? A useful handoff carries enough information for the next person to make that judgement.

Imagine opening the records with the two people who handled the job. Sales marked the quote as won. The scheduler worked from the list of jobs marked ready to book. No one owned the step between those two states. Sending another email reminder might get this customer a visit. The following week, someone on leave could leave the next customer in exactly the same position.

Suppose instead that the scheduler had already checked the quote, called the customer and recorded that the only suitable technician was booked out. The delay would look identical from the customer’s end. The scheduling note would point you towards capacity. Changing how the team routes emails would do little for that case. Look for a record or a person who could establish which account fits.

## Why some things get noticed

William Ocasio proposed that an organisation’s rules, resources and relationships help determine which issues reach people who can act on them. Think about the difference between a shared inbox that anyone can open and a booking list that one person checks every morning. The team has access to both. Only one has a place in the day’s work. Ocasio’s theory gives you a reason to examine that arrangement before concluding someone needs to pay closer attention. [Ocasio, 1997, pp. 187–188](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/towards-an-attention-based-view-of-the-firm/).

Noticing also leaves room for disagreement. Sales may reasonably see an accepted quote as a commitment. A scheduler with experience of difficult sites may see it as a request that still needs checking. Daft and Weick proposed that organisations differ in how they look for information and interpret their surroundings. In our example, ask each person what the email meant at the time and what they could see. That question is OIBOK’s application of the theory. [Daft and Weick, 1984, abstract](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1984.4277657).

## What deserves attention here?

Start with the consequence of waiting. A missing access detail for a job next month can usually wait for clarification. The same detail matters immediately when a technician is about to set off. Someone needs a way to flag that difference. A useful question for the person doing the work is “When did you first think this job might go wrong, and where could you raise it?”

Listen for signals that never make the formal queue. A receptionist might hear three customers ask the same question while each call looks routine on its own. Keep enough of the original observation for someone else to check it. Record who will look again, when that should happen and what evidence would justify closing it. Asking the receptionist what became of the concern can reveal whether a quieter voice can influence a decision.

If software sorts or summarises requests, inspect one item it put low on the list. Can the person on the phones see why it landed there and get a consequential error reconsidered in time? An extra alert creates another demand on whoever receives it. Decide who would use the alert and which action it could change before adding it.

## Try the explanation on two cases

Bring one job that went through and one that stalled. Ask the person doing the work and the person receiving it to reconstruct each case from the records available that day.

| Ask | What you could check |
| --- | --- |
| Where did the first useful clue appear? | The original message or observation, when it arrived, and what the recipient could see. |
| What did someone actually do with it? | A question, scheduling entry, decision or reason to wait. A delivery receipt establishes only that a message arrived. |
| What else might explain the difference? | Compare missing information, available appointments and decision authority across the two jobs. |
| What would make you change your mind? | A timely scheduling note in the delayed case would weaken the idea that nobody noticed the acceptance. |

If the gap really was the handoff, try one small change within the team’s authority. For example, the scheduler could accept each ready job or send it back with a specific question. Check the next eligible jobs for both waiting time and extra checking work. Ask a colleague who missed the trial to handle a subsequent case. If it works only when one person intervenes, there is more to learn. [Use the one-page worksheet](/tools/two-case-review/).
