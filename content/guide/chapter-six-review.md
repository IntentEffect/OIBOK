# Evaluate the change

**OIBOK Guide 0.7 · Chapter 6 review copy · 25 November 2026**

**Working record.** Open the [Outcome and Learning Record](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/#evaluation-plan) before the change. Agree the observation window with the evidence owner and decision owner. The [completed OL-SG-01](https://oibok.org/guide/silvergum/#4-completed-outcome-and-learning-record-ol-sg-01) preserves the first plan and later adaptation.

A trial has finished. Someone now has to decide whether to keep the change, adjust it or stop. Ask who benefited, who did extra work and what else changed during the same period. If the team added AI alongside new instructions and extra checking, the result tells you about the combined arrangement. You need a closer comparison to judge what AI contributed. [Outcomes, feedback and evaluation](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/).

Use the **Outcome and Learning Record** to connect the intervention, evaluation plan, observations, decision and subsequent changes. Prepare the plan before starting wherever possible. If work has already begun, reconstruct the available baseline, identify decisions made after seeing results and record the limits this introduces. Evaluation may reopen the problem definition or work design at any point. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## Start with the decision

Identify who will decide whether to continue, adapt, stop or defer the change, and when that decision is needed. Agree what evidence would change their judgement. Specify the workflow, eligible cases, people affected and observation period. Distinguish the period in which cases enter the evaluation from the time allowed for their outcomes to emerge.

Write the expected mechanism in plain language: what will change in the work, why that should affect the outcome, and what must be true for it to work. Include the complete intervention. If it combines clearer policy, revised authority, training and AI assistance, those changes belong in the evaluation. Record the expected contribution of AI separately. Set locally justified outcome expectations, quality constraints and pause conditions before examining results; preserve any later changes and their reasons. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## Define measures that represent the whole job

Choose a small set of measures that can support the decision. Include an outcome, quality and total effort, with relevant safeguards and affected groups. Describe implementation sufficiently to know whether the intended change actually occurred.

| Measure | Operational definition to establish | Interpretation to protect |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | The change experienced by the intended beneficiary, its unit, start and end points, and follow-up period. | Faster task completion may leave the beneficiary's problem unresolved. |
| Quality | What counts as correct, complete and acceptable; who judges it; which cases are examined. | Separate defects in an intermediate output from defects reaching the final service. |
| Total effort | Work across preparation, delivery, checking, correction, escalation and maintenance; whose time is included. | Time saved by one role may become work for another. |
| Safeguards | The unacceptable consequence or boundary, the observation method and who can pause affected work. | An absence of observed incidents is limited by what was observed and for how long. |
| Implementation | Which eligible cases received the intended arrangement, including deviations and workarounds. | A weak result may reflect an ineffective design, incomplete implementation, or both. |

For each measure record the source, collection owner, frequency and calculation. Define the unit of analysis: a case, decision, person, team or another bounded unit. For a rate, specify both numerator and denominator. For duration, distinguish elapsed time from active working time. Keep setup effort visible separately from recurring effort, then explain how each affects the decision. Report model, software and service charges separately from staff time. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

Report how many cases were eligible, included, completed, excluded or still awaiting an outcome. Explain missing observations and whether their absence could favour the result. Do not silently remove difficult cases or count unresolved cases as successes. Keep intake cohorts and follow-up periods visible so that newer cases do not appear better simply because there has been less time for problems to emerge.

Where quality depends on judgement, give reviewers shared criteria and examine disagreements. A changing definition or a new measurement process can produce an apparent improvement. Preserve the original definitions and explain any break in comparability. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

## Follow the constraint after a task speeds up

The following proposal workflow and figures are fictional. This simple weekly capacity example assumes sufficient customer demand, comparable cases, no opening review backlog and unchanged quality. Every draft requires one review, passes that review and can then be issued. It ignores variation within the week so that the capacity constraint is easy to inspect.

| Weekly measure | Before faster drafting | After faster drafting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drafts entering review | 20 | 60 |
| Review capacity | 25 | 25 |
| Maximum accepted proposals completed | 20 | 25 |
| Queue growth at that completion level | 0 | 35 |

At full review capacity, the queue grows by 35 proposals each week because 60 arrive and 25 are completed. The figures describe a possible capacity limit, not an observed AI effect. A real review would also record queue age, customer turnaround, returned work, defects and effort across roles. More drafts alone cannot establish that customers receive a useful proposal sooner.

Inspect a waiting case before choosing a remedy. A complete, accepted case waiting for a fully occupied qualified reviewer suggests a capacity shortage. Missing information or an unaccepted transfer suggests a handoff problem. Repeated corrections suggest a quality problem. Waiting for a permitted decision suggests an authority problem. Insufficient customer demand weakens the case for producing more proposals. Several explanations may apply together; record evidence that would distinguish them before diagnosing Connection Quality. [Evidence on connection quality](https://oibok.org/research/integrated-evidence-notes/#09--quality-of-the-connections).

The service owner would decide which response to test, such as controlling the release of drafts, changing review support or correcting the inputs. Record who can authorise that response and which customer outcome should improve. Compare similar work, include the extra effort and follow the next limiting stage after the change. Use the existing evaluation record and [Companion diagnostic prompts](https://oibok.org/guide/companion/#run-a-diagnostic-review). The [November 2026 evidence update](https://oibok.org/research/working-with-ai/) supplies the research rationale; these invented figures supply no field evidence.

## Compare plausible alternatives

Use a baseline and, where feasible, a relevant comparison operating over the same period. Check whether workload, case complexity, staff experience, policy, incentives and available resources differ. A historical baseline may still be useful, but a before-and-after difference also contains changes that would have occurred without the intervention. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Include a credible **non-AI alternative**. Depending on the problem, this might be clearer guidance, a redesigned handoff, a search improvement or a simpler automation. Ask whether it could achieve the desired outcome with less effort or dependency. Where practical, compare the same improved working arrangement with and without AI. If that comparison is absent, report the result of the complete package and leave the AI-specific contribution unresolved.

Record competing explanations before deciding which account is most convincing. Consider easier cases, additional expert support, seasonal demand, staff becoming familiar with the work, concurrent changes and different measurement. Look for evidence that could contradict the preferred explanation. Observations, complaints and interviews can reveal mechanisms and omitted consequences; they complement the comparison rather than automatically establishing causation. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## State what the evidence allows

Separate observations, interpretation and uncertainty. An observation describes what the records show. An interpretation explains why it may have happened. An uncertainty identifies what remains unresolved and why it matters to the next decision.

Keep the claim proportionate. A descriptive claim reports a change. An associational claim reports that use of the intervention and an outcome varied together, without establishing cause. A contribution claim explains why the intervention plausibly helped alongside other factors. A causal claim requires a defensible account of what would otherwise have happened. The strength of the language should follow the evaluation design and evidence. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Show counts alongside percentages and explain the sample selection. A small, convenient or volunteer sample may omit rare events, difficult cases or people less comfortable with the change. Repeated observations from the same people or teams are not automatically independent evidence. Where estimates or uncertainty intervals are appropriate, use methods suited to the design; apparent precision cannot repair biased selection or missing outcomes.

Aggregate only where definitions, populations and periods support the comparison. When combining rates, use their underlying counts and denominators rather than averaging percentages indiscriminately. Show important variation alongside the summary: a better average can conceal worse outcomes for a subgroup or a transfer of effort between teams. Record affected people whose experience is missing. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

A successful workflow trial supports a claim about that workflow under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the whole organisation has become more intelligent. Wider claims require evidence connecting the result to organisational roles, authority, routines, memory and repeated consequences across the relevant boundary. Do not turn local measures into a single OI score. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Make and record the decision

Put the results side by side with the people who did and received the work. Which costs can the decision owner accept? Which conditions would require a pause? Record any disagreement before choosing what to do next.

| Decision | Grounds to record | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Continue | Evidence supports use within the tested scope and agreed conditions. | Maintain ownership, monitoring and a review trigger. |
| Adapt | A useful contribution remains plausible, but a correctable problem or uncertainty requires change. | Name the modification, affected scope and evidence needed before extension. |
| Stop | The change fails the agreed purpose or constraints, or a preferable alternative is supported. | Use the fallback, close dependencies and retain the explanation. |
| Defer | Evidence, authority or necessary conditions are insufficient for a responsible decision. | Specify what is missing, who will resolve it and when the decision returns. |

These decisions operationalise the record's options to continue, adapt, stop or gather more evidence. Deferring expansion need not suspend every existing activity; state precisely what remains authorised. Conversely, pausing affected use requires a clear fallback and restart conditions. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

**Review check:** bring the owner, someone doing the work and the evidence owner together. Invite an affected perspective and a challenger where useful. Can they trace the decision to the original question, inspect contradictory evidence and identify the next action? Record scope, owner, date and review trigger before closing the discussion.

### Worked decision on a booking handoff

The booking cases, figures and decision below are fictional. A service team is deciding whether to expand a trial in which the scheduler explicitly accepts each ready job or returns it with a question.

| Fictional trial record | Previous 10 eligible bookings | Next 10 eligible bookings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customers given a booking date within two working days | 6 of 10 | 8 of 10 |
| Median staff time spent checking a booking | 4 minutes | 7 minutes |
| Jobs requiring the specialist technician | 5 of 10 | 2 of 10 |

**What happened at the handoff?** In one delayed trial case the scheduler recorded the acceptance the same morning and named the technician needed. The next suitable slot was three days away. Another notification would have done little for that customer. In a second case sales marked the job ready, but the shared queue showed no receiving owner until the customer rang. A missed handoff remains a credible explanation for that delay. [Evidence on selective attention](https://oibok.org/research/integrated-evidence-notes/#02--selective-attention) · [Evidence on connection quality](https://oibok.org/research/integrated-evidence-notes/#09--quality-of-the-connections).

**What did the team learn?** The usual scheduler used the new routine on eight bookings. Their cover handled the other two and needed a private prompt from sales on one. Updating the instructions gave the team a record. The cover shift exposed a gap in using it. [Evidence on persistent learning](https://oibok.org/research/integrated-evidence-notes/#07--persistent-learning).

**The service manager’s decision was to adapt and extend the limited trial.** More customers received dates promptly, but this group also had fewer specialist jobs and checking took longer. The scheduling lead will add a visible owner for returned jobs. For the next ten eligible bookings, the lead will record specialist availability, handoff acceptance, customer wait and checking time. A cover worker will handle cases using the shared instructions. At the tenth booking the service manager will compare like cases and review any extra work with both teams. Continue locally if the handoff holds on cover shifts at an acceptable cost. Adjust the routine if work is shifting upstream. Stop it if the extra checking persists without a customer benefit.
