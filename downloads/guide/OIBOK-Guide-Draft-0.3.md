## Purpose and publication status

This guide helps leaders, practitioners and teams investigate organisational work, compare changes and examine their consequences. It connects the research chapters of the Organisation Intelligence Body of Knowledge (OIBOK) to practical tools.

Use it to identify improvements, examine AI proposals, clarify responsibilities and retain learning. Three fictional examples support the chapters. Working Companion 0.3 adds preparation guidance and a feedback record. This illustrated edition includes nine draft figures from Visual set 0.1, with full-size SVG and PDF downloads in the [diagrams collection](https://oibok.org/diagrams/).

**Status.** Guide 0.3 is based on OIBOK release 1.0 and Practical Toolkit 0.1. The body of knowledge is a literature-grounded working synthesis. This edition is prepared for practitioner review. External practitioner review, field application and independent validation of the complete model and guide remain pending. [\[R\]](https://oibok.org/research/release-record/) [\[M\]](https://oibok.org/methodology/)

**Scope.** Apply the guide with or without AI, alongside existing professional and organisational practices. It provides no certification, validated intelligence score or assurance of compliance with another standard.

**Preparation.** This draft was developed with AI assistance. Terminology, mapping and Harbour Services figures were checked against the sources in Appendix D. The new examples use invented inputs; the business arithmetic has been checked. This check did not independently reassess every underlying research paper. Independent editorial review is pending.

# Quick start

Choose one recurring decision or workflow where the current result is unsatisfactory or fragile. Examples include resolving a service complaint, approving a purchase, planning a delivery, responding to an operational incident or preparing an evidence-based recommendation. Define the start, the end and the people affected. A question such as "How can we resolve standard complaints accurately, with fewer delays and repeat contacts?" is specific enough to investigate.

Bring together the person who owns the work, people who perform it, someone who receives its output and the expertise needed to judge the evidence. Use existing records, contrasting cases and the current instructions. Someone with authority to change the work must receive the findings; they need not attend every discussion. [\[T0\]](https://oibok.org/tools/guide/) [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

## Examine the current response

Use the **Organisational Intelligence Practice Review** to follow actual cases. Identify what triggered attention, what context was needed, whose knowledge was used, who could decide and what happened after the decision. Include a case that went well and a case that was delayed, disputed or recovered from an error. Record how the cases were selected.

Use the **Context and Knowledge Map** to make important sources, definitions, expertise and gaps visible. Use the **Decision and Coordination Canvas** to identify authority, dependencies, handoff acceptance and exceptions. These two worksheets sit within the Practice Review and are also available as separate A3 canvases.

Separate observation from interpretation. "The receiving team returned seven of the cases we reviewed" is an observation if the records support it. "Staff need more training" is an explanation to examine. Look for alternatives before choosing a remedy. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

## Choose and define a change

Describe the outcome you want and what must remain acceptable. Compare current practice with a credible improvement that does not use AI. Add an AI option when there is a specific contribution to test, such as retrieving relevant sources, preparing a draft or identifying cases for review.

For an AI option, complete the **AI Opportunity and Work Design Canvas**. State what the system can see, produce and do; which person can authorise a consequential action; when the system should abstain; and how work continues if it fails. "A person will review it" needs a named role, suitable expertise, time and the authority to change or stop the result. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

For a change without AI, move from the Practice Review and decision design to Chapter 6 and the Outcome and Learning Record. Chapter 5 is for proposals involving AI.

## Plan the evaluation before starting

Use the **Outcome and Learning Record** to define the intervention, baseline, comparison, measures and review window. Include quality, total effort, delays, adverse effects and the experience of people who depend on the work. Specify which cases count, how incomplete records will be handled and who will collect the evidence.

Agree what would support continuation, require an adaptation, trigger a pause or justify stopping. The strength of the evidence should match the significance and reversibility of the decision. A small trial may inform the next limited test while leaving a wider investment decision unresolved. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## Review consequences and preserve learning

Examine whether the change was used as intended and whether the outcome improved. Ask what else could explain the result. Record a decision to continue, adapt, stop or defer, with the person responsible and the next review.

Translate useful learning into a change that people can find and use: a corrected source, a clearer decision right, a revised handoff, a tested recovery procedure or a different allocation of resources. Return later to see whether the change is still enacted, including when the original participant is absent or a different person performs the work. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/) [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/) [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

**The minimum useful result is a decision supported by evidence, a named owner for the next action and a record that a later colleague can understand.** Completing every box is not the objective. Record an explanation when a prompt does not apply, and leave unresolved questions visible.

# How to use this guide

Read Chapters 1 and 2 to establish the model and select the work. Chapters 3 to 5 help investigate and design the response. Chapters 6 to 8 address evaluation, persistence and extension. Appendix A follows Harbour Services through the approach, compares three catalogue workflow options for Wattle Supply and examines a non-AI decision at Linden Studio; Appendix B helps a facilitator prepare the sessions. Appendix C maps each guide chapter to the research and tools, and Appendix D identifies the source versions.

The reading order is a practical aid. In real work you may revisit the problem after examining the evidence, redesign the intervention after an exception, or revise the measures when the intended outcome becomes clearer. Record significant revisions rather than making the earlier rationale disappear.

The guide uses four distinct kinds of material. **Model statements** describe the current OIBOK working synthesis and cite its owning chapter. **Practice guidance** proposes how to apply that synthesis and the toolkit. **External guidance** is identified by source and version. **Illustrative examples** explain a method using invented situations or data. None of these labels turns an example into empirical evidence.

References such as O4 or T2 identify the research chapter or tool supporting a passage. The references are linked and listed in Appendix D. Follow the research chapter when you need its argument, bibliography, competing accounts or limits. Use the separate source record at the cited repository revision when exact version history matters.

# Choose the depth of the review

Choose the smallest review that can support the decision. These proposed routes consider consequences, reversibility, dependencies and uncertainty, with no numerical readiness score.

For every route, use a Practice Review and an Outcome and Learning Record for a selected change, reusing suitable records. Name an action owner and a later review point. Expand the supporting maps where gaps matter. [\[T0\]](https://oibok.org/tools/guide/) [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/) [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

| Route | People and minimum connected record | Decision and escalation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Small reversible change** within existing authority | Work owner, a worker and a recipient, with relevant expertise. Record the inquiry, contrasting cases, sources, competing explanations, proposed change, authority and outcome check. | Try the limited change, investigate or retain current practice. Deepen the review if authority is unclear, effects extend beyond the boundary or reversal is uncertain. |
| **Bounded AI pilot** in a reviewed workflow | Work owner, workers, knowledge and systems owners, evidence owner and affected users where relevant. Connect the Practice Review, AI canvas and evaluation plan. Cover alternatives, permissions, review, fallback and stop conditions. | Authorise a limited pilot when conditions are evidenced, choose a non-AI change, investigate or stop. Escalate missing evidence, inadequate review capacity, exceeded delegation or a stop condition. |
| **Consequential cross-team work** with interdependent commitments or effects difficult to reverse | Accountable decision owner, affected workflow owners and recipients, relevant specialists and evidence owner. Connect case evidence, dependencies, decision rights, affected outcomes, options, resources, approvals, recovery and evaluation. | Make a bounded commitment, commission inquiry, adapt or defer. Escalate unresolved ownership, conflicting constraints, consequences beyond authority or specialist evaluation and recovery needs. |

Complete the AI canvas when taking AI forward into work design or a pilot, including in consequential cross-team work. If the initial review defers AI before design, record the reasons in the Practice Review and revisit them if AI becomes a candidate again. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

Roles may be combined while required separation of responsibilities remains applicable. Record unresolved conditions, owners and evidence needed; revisit the route when circumstances change. Chapters 3 to 8 develop the methods. The Working Companion provides preparation, handoff and retention checks.

# 1 Understand organisational intelligence

## A working definition

OIBOK defines Organisational Intelligence as:

> Organisational Intelligence is the persistent organisation-level capacity to form an orientation from relevant signals and experience, coordinate collective response, and improve future response through feedback. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

The definition asks whether the organisation can respond coherently and keep doing so over time. An impressive analysis, a well-written AI answer or an exceptional individual performance may contribute. The organisational question is what allows people to recognise the situation, commit to an appropriate response, carry it through and use the consequences to inform later work.

An organisation might have detailed policy documents while staff cannot identify the current rule at the moment of decision. It might generate useful recommendations while nobody has authority to act. It might improve a result during a closely supported pilot and lose that improvement when the support ends. These are illustrative situations in which examining the connections between activities can help specify what needs investigation.

The guide therefore uses a recurring workflow as a practical starting boundary. You can observe cases, identify responsibilities and return to later episodes. A local review supports claims about that boundary. It does not automatically establish the intelligence of the whole organisation. [\[O10, sections 7 and 19\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Orient Coordinate and Improve

The working model organises the response around three connected transformations. **Orient** concerns how relevant signals, context and knowledge produce an understanding suitable for response. **Coordinate** concerns authorised commitment and reliable collective action. **Improve** concerns using consequences and credible feedback to strengthen or preserve future orientation and response through learning and adaptation. [\[O10, section 4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

These transformations can overlap, recur and operate at different organisational levels. A team may reconsider its understanding during execution. A later outcome may expose a problem in how the original issue was framed. Several teams may coordinate while each continues to learn. Avoid treating the model as a mandatory series of gates that every organisation must follow once in a fixed order.

Six operational capabilities sit within this working model. The short descriptions below are a practical paraphrase of Chapter 10, not a replacement for its full definitions.

| Capability | Transformation | What to examine in the work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sense | Orient | How material signals and experience are noticed, qualified and brought to organisational attention |
| Know | Orient | How relevant knowledge and expertise are created, checked, found, combined and used in context |
| Choose | Coordinate | How alternatives lead to an attributable and authorised commitment under uncertainty |
| Mobilise | Coordinate | How a commitment becomes coordinated, governed and reliable action |
| Learn | Improve | How credible feedback revises shared understanding, memory, routines and future response |
| Adapt | Improve | How capability, authority, structure and resources are developed, retired or reconfigured |

Contextualise is a connective mechanism in this model. It helps establish what a signal or piece of knowledge means in the circumstances. Organisational Orientation is an integrated state: a sufficiently shared or aligned account of the situation, its significance, material uncertainty, constraints and conditions for response. It can preserve disagreement and role-specific interpretations. Neither is an additional operational capability. Memory provides continuity across the model, and the Outcome-Feedback bridge connects consequences to material suitable for learning. [\[O10, sections 5 and 6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Look at the connections

When reviewing a case, examine what happens between activities. Did the relevant signal reach someone who could interpret it? Did the decision-maker receive the current knowledge and its uncertainty? Did the receiving team accept the handoff and have what it needed? Did outcome feedback reach someone who could change the practice?

OIBOK calls attention to **Connection Quality** as a provisional diagnostic idea. It proposes that the integrity of these transitions may help explain differences between organisations with apparently similar local strengths. The guide uses the idea to generate questions. It does not provide a validated connection score or assume that every poor result is caused by a handoff. Workload, resources, incentives, expertise and external conditions remain plausible explanations. [\[O10, section 9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

Evidence from both sides of a handoff is particularly useful. A sender may regard a record as complete while a recipient cannot use it. An escalation may have been sent without being accepted. A new policy may have been published without becoming available in the work. Record receipt, understanding and use where those distinctions matter.

## Include responsibility in the design

The response must be examined for its purpose, authority and consequences as well as its effectiveness. Identify who benefits, who carries extra work or risk and who can question the outcome. A locally faster process may leave another team with more correction work or make it harder for a customer to challenge a decision.

Identify applicable rights, legal duties and professional obligations alongside the interests and concerns of affected people. Give them a credible route to question the purpose, evidence or response, and identify who can resolve the challenge. [\[O10, section 3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

OIBOK distinguishes instrumental capability from responsible exercise. An organisation may be capable of achieving a purpose while governing that capability irresponsibly. In practice, assess both what the arrangement achieves and whether its authority, accountability and treatment of affected people are acceptable. Record disagreement about those questions instead of hiding it inside an aggregate result. [\[O10, sections 3 and 19\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Apply the idea to AI

AI can participate in organisational work by producing or transforming information, supporting analysis or carrying out permitted actions. Whether that participation is useful depends on the actual task, sources, work design and evidence. The model also applies to organisations that use no AI. [\[O10, section 16\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

Treat the AI proposal as an organisational change. Ask what information it will use, how its output will be judged, what action it can initiate and what people need to know when it fails. Check whether the proposal preserves access to expertise and the ability to perform or recover the work without that particular system. A tool-level benchmark can inform selection, but the workflow still needs evaluation in its own setting.

This approach changes the question from the general appeal of AI to a testable proposition about a particular response. A proposed drafting assistant, for example, can be examined for its effect on total handling effort, accuracy, reviewer workload and the customer's experience of resolution. Those measures should be defined before the organisation decides what a successful trial would mean.

## State the limit of the claim

A defensible claim names the workflow, participants, authority, relevant period and evidence. "In these cases, the revised handoff was used by both teams and reduced missing information" is more useful than an unsupported declaration that the organisation is intelligent. Even the narrower claim needs records showing what changed and alternative explanations that remain unresolved.

OIBOK 1.0 proposes examining persistence through a stated time horizon and at least two relevant forms of evidence, such as repeated enactment, use beyond one participant, retained memory, recovery after interruption, transfer or prior feedback changing a later response. This is the release's proposed evidence discipline. It has not been validated as a measurement threshold. Chapter 7 shows how to plan a proportionate persistence check. [\[O10, section 7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/) [\[R\]](https://oibok.org/research/release-record/)

# 2 Start with a worthwhile problem

## Define the outcome and the boundary

Choose work that matters to an identifiable group and occurs often enough, or has consequences significant enough, to justify investigation. Describe the difficulty in observable terms. "Improve organisational intelligence" is too broad to guide an initial review. "Reduce avoidable repeat contact after a standard service complaint without increasing policy errors" identifies an outcome and a quality condition.

State where the inquiry begins and ends. A useful boundary includes the trigger, the decision or response, the recipient and a suitable period for observing consequences. Include upstream and downstream dependencies that could explain the problem even if you cannot change them immediately. For a complaints review, closure may be an administrative event while repeat contact or an unresolved service issue reveals more about the outcome. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/) [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Record exclusions and their reasons. Standard cases and exceptional cases may require different authority or expertise. An initial scope can exclude exceptional work, but the team still needs to recognise and route it. Later claims must reflect those exclusions. A pilot involving routine cases cannot establish suitability for the cases it deliberately left out.

## Select a question the organisation can act on

Discuss candidate problems with the people who do the work and those who depend on it. Consider the consequences of the current difficulty, the evidence available, the authority to change it, the opportunity to observe another case and the effort needed for a useful inquiry. Use these considerations to explain the selection; a numerical priority score is optional and should not imply more precision than the judgement supports.

Select a first inquiry that can produce a meaningful decision. It may concern a single handoff, one recurring decision or a whole bounded service. If the proposal is already framed as "deploy an AI assistant", first identify the task and the outcome that would justify it. The resulting inquiry may support AI, a change without AI, further investigation or retaining current practice.

**A concise inquiry statement can use this form:** We are examining [recurring work] from [trigger] to [outcome window] because [observed difficulty] affects [people or purpose]. We want to improve [outcome] while maintaining [quality or responsibility conditions]. [Role] can decide on changes. We still need to establish [important uncertainty].

## Assemble the people and records

Ask a workflow owner to receive the findings and make or escalate the resulting decision. Include people with direct experience of the work, someone who receives its output and people who can resolve material questions about sources, authority or systems. Where the consequences warrant it, include a suitable representative of affected users or obtain their experience through an appropriate route.

One person may hold several roles in a small organisation. Record the roles explicitly so that the review still distinguishes doing the work, authorising a change, maintaining a source and judging an outcome. Where possible, ask someone other than the proposal sponsor to facilitate. This can help surface disagreements, but it does not make the review independent research. [\[T0\]](https://oibok.org/tools/guide/) [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

Bring actual cases and the records people used at the time. Current policy alone may not explain an earlier decision if a different version was available then. Include timestamps, handoff records, examples of accepted and rejected outputs, relevant feedback and explanations from participants. Keep sensitive evidence in an authorised location and reference it by identifier in the working record.

Choose contrasting cases deliberately and state how you selected them. A few convenient examples can reveal a mechanism worth investigating. They cannot establish how frequently it occurs across the organisation. If frequency matters to the decision, define the eligible population and obtain evidence suitable for that question.

## Build an evidence account

Use the Practice Review to distinguish the source record, what it shows, the interpretation and the unresolved question. This prevents a familiar explanation from becoming the agreed cause before it has been examined.

| Entry | Illustrative wording | What it permits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | The return record cites a missing policy version as the reason the receiving team returned the case | A description of that recorded event |
| Participant account | An adviser says current policies are difficult to find | An account of that person's experience |
| Interpretation | Unclear source ownership may contribute to incomplete handoffs | A hypothesis to investigate |
| Unknown | We have not checked whether workload differs across the cases | A limit and an investigation task |

The examples in this table are invented. In your own review, attach a source identifier and date to material entries. Record conflicting accounts and missing records. An absence of evidence may mean something was not recorded; it does not always mean the activity did not occur. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/) [\[M\]](https://oibok.org/methodology/)

## Compare explanations before choosing a remedy

For each main difficulty, write down more than one plausible explanation and the evidence that would help distinguish them. A delayed decision might result from unclear authority, missing context, insufficient capacity or an intentional control. If the delay is a necessary check, shortening it without preserving its purpose may worsen the result.

Walk through the evidence with participants. Ask where the pattern appears, where it does not and what changed over time. Look for cases that challenge the favoured explanation. Specify the next evidence to obtain when disagreement cannot yet be resolved. A useful review can conclude that more information is needed before a change is justified.

Express the proposed intervention as a mechanism to test: if the current source and its owner are visible at the decision point, advisers may spend less time resolving conflicting guidance while preserving accuracy. This wording identifies the proposed change, expected behaviour and outcome. It also leaves room for the explanation to be wrong.

## Agree the next decision

Conclude the initial review with a bounded next step. The owner may authorise a small reversible process change, ask for a work design using the AI canvas, commission further inquiry or decide that the current arrangement should remain. Record the evidence, rationale, unresolved conditions and the next review point.

Carry a common case identifier into subsequent tools. Assign owners for source correction, authority clarification, technical preparation and evaluation where applicable. Before implementing a change, confirm that the outcome plan in Chapter 6 is sufficiently clear to interpret what happens. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/) [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

You are ready to move on when the people involved can explain the same problem, point to the evidence and its gaps, identify who can decide and describe what the next action should resolve. Agreement about every explanation is unnecessary; visible disagreement is often part of a useful inquiry.

# 3 Understand the work and its knowledge

**Purpose:** establish what people need to understand to perform one recurring decision or workflow responsibly, and where that understanding currently breaks down.

Use the Organisational Intelligence Practice Review and its Context and Knowledge Map. Bring together people who perform the work, own its outcome and maintain the knowledge it depends on. Include an affected team or user where their experience could change the account. The output is an evidence-backed description of the work, a usable map of its knowledge and a short set of gaps to investigate or address. These are proposed practice methods for local testing. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

## Follow the work through actual cases

Define the outcome, where the work starts and ends, and which cases fall outside the review. Choose contrasting recent cases, including ordinary work and an exception. Ask participants to reconstruct what happened using records, observations and their accounts. Follow the work into the places where people clarify uncertainty, make informal arrangements or repair incomplete information.

Record observed evidence separately from interpretation and unknowns. A person's account is evidence of their reported experience; additional evidence is needed to estimate how often it happens or explain its cause. Preserve disagreements and contradictory cases. Keep source references, dates and coverage limits beside the findings so that a later reviewer can inspect them. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

Trace how each case first attracted attention. What made it significant? Who assessed urgency? Which signals arrived too late or failed to reach someone able to respond? OIBOK research Chapter 1 connects attention to purpose, consequence, uncertainty and routing. Use that connection to investigate whether the organisation recognises the right issues and gives them usable attention. A sent notification alone does not show that anyone could act on it. [\[O1\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/signals-and-attention/)

Keep competing explanations open. An apparent information problem might involve workload, unclear authority, inconsistent training or a changing mix of cases. Record what would support or weaken each explanation and which evidence is still missing. Select improvements after this initial investigation. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

## Assemble the knowledge needed for this work

For each important decision or action, ask what the person needs to know, where that knowledge resides and how they establish whether it applies. Include policies and records alongside experienced colleagues, team routines, external expertise and knowledge embedded in systems. OIBOK research Chapter 3 treats organisational knowledge as distributed across these arrangements. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/)

Complete the Context and Knowledge Map around the needs of the work. Useful entries identify:

- the knowledge required and the consequence of getting it wrong;
- the source, current version and conditions under which it applies;
- the accountable owner and route to relevant expertise;
- access restrictions, conflicting sources and unresolved gaps.

Add enough context to make the information interpretable: purpose, current state, material history, applicable constraints and uncertainty. OIBOK research Chapter 2 proposes minimum sufficient context as a working principle. The practical aim is to provide what this actor needs for this task, with access to deeper evidence when required. Relevance, currency and inspectability should guide selection. [\[O2\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-context/); [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

Distinguish the person credible about a claim from the person authorised to decide what happens next. A policy specialist may explain the governing rule while a delegated manager decides an exception. Record both relationships. If sources disagree, assign resolution to an appropriate owner; preserve the disagreement until it is resolved. If knowledge remains tacit or situation-dependent, record when experienced judgement is needed and how to obtain it. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/)

## Agree a small shared vocabulary

Some breakdowns involve incompatible meanings. Teams may use the same word for different states, or different words for the same commitment. A small shared representation can help: a shared account of the things that matter to this work, their meanings and their relationships. This is a limited use of an organisational ontology for the selected work. OIBOK research Chapter 2 provides a working taxonomy rather than a validated ontology. Begin with the misunderstandings found in the review. This is a proposed design technique informed by the OIBOK's treatment of context, language and knowledge representation. [\[O2\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-context/); [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/)

A small table or existing system register may be sufficient:

| Item to describe | Useful questions | Relationship to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Case or request | What is included? What uniquely identifies it? | Relates to a customer, service or commitment |
| Policy or rule | Which version applies, when and to whom? | Governs a decision; supersedes an earlier version |
| Decision | What was authorised, by whom and under what conditions? | Depends on evidence; authorises specified action |
| Role or expertise | Who owns the responsibility? Who can interpret the issue? | Decides, performs, advises or receives |
| Work state | What does this status mean? What remains unresolved? | Shows completion, dependency or exception |

Test the vocabulary against the contrasting cases. Resolve differences that obstruct work and retain distinctions that matter. Assign an owner for changes. A spreadsheet, glossary or linked records can support this exercise; a graph database is optional. Existing terms should be reused where they work. This keeps the representation proportionate to the problem.

## Make the map usable and maintainable

Preserve links to controlled sources where possible. Collect only information needed for the review, use approved access arrangements and avoid copying sensitive case material into shared worksheets. Context may need different views for different roles. Give each unresolved gap an owner, next action and review date. [\[O1\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/signals-and-attention/); [\[O2\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-context/); [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

Ask someone who did not conduct the review to reconstruct a case using the map. Can they locate the applicable source, distinguish evidence from interpretation, identify missing expertise and explain where clarification should go? Use their difficulties to improve the representation. This is a practical adequacy check proposed by this guide, with a modest purpose: checking whether the map supports the selected work.

Finish with a decision about what to clarify, repair or investigate. Carry the review identifier and material uncertainties into the Decision and Coordination Canvas. An unresolved knowledge gap may need attention before the organisation can responsibly redesign the workflow. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

# 4 Clarify decisions coordination and execution

**Purpose:** establish how understanding becomes an authorised commitment, how people accept responsibility and how the organisation knows what actually happened.

Use the Decision and Coordination Canvas within the Practice Review. Walk through the same cases, concentrating on decisions, handoffs, waits, exceptions and completion. OIBOK research Chapters 4, 5 and 6 provide the underlying distinctions between deciding, coordinating and executing. Keep those distinctions visible even when one person performs several roles. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/); [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/); [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

![How a handoff becomes an accepted commitment](https://oibok.org/assets/diagrams/accepted-handoff.svg)

**Figure. How a handoff becomes an accepted commitment.** Draft fictional Linden Studio illustration. Preparation, submission, acceptance, action and verified effect have distinct meanings. In this example the sender owns the handoff until acceptance. Returned, waiting or exceptional work retains a named owner; this ownership rule is specific to the example. [Open the figure and full-size downloads](https://oibok.org/diagrams/accepted-handoff/).

## Make authority specific enough to use

For each material decision, name its owner and identify the source of their authority. Describe the cases covered, limits, required evidence, approvals and exclusions. Where authority is delegated, record when it expires or requires reconsideration. A role name alone rarely explains these conditions. OIBOK research Chapter 4 also distinguishes knowledge authority from the right to commit the organisation. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/)

Use the canvas to clarify four contributions:

| Contribution | What needs to be clear |
|---|---|
| Prepare and advise | Who gathers evidence, interprets it and proposes options? |
| Decide and authorise | Who may commit the organisation, within which limits? |
| Perform and verify | Who acts, checks the effect and records unresolved uncertainty? |
| Receive and continue | Who accepts the result and owns the next dependency? |

The same person may cover several contributions in a small team. Material conflicts or existing separation requirements may require different people. Tailor the arrangement to consequence, uncertainty and reversibility. Routine work may need a short delegation and clear exceptions; consequential decisions may require specialist input and independent challenge. Use the organisation's existing governance and approval requirements when setting these conditions. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

Record enough rationale to explain why a material decision was made, the evidence available at the time and what could reopen it. Preserve significant dissent and its disposition. Affected people and relevant specialists need a usable route to challenge facts, interpretations or authority. Clarify who decides after challenge and who handles an appeal. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/)

## Treat a handoff as an accepted dependency

Trace what the sender knows and what the receiver needs. A useful handoff carries purpose, current state, completed work, the expected next contribution, material evidence, uncertainty and applicable authority. Include timing and dependencies where they affect the receiver's ability to act. OIBOK research Chapter 5 describes handoff completion in terms of acceptance or explicit failure of the receiving dependency. [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/)

Define acceptance in the language of the work. The receiving person should be able to confirm readiness, request clarification or identify an incomplete input. Assign ownership while a handoff is disputed or waiting. Otherwise, both parties may reasonably believe the other is responsible.

Use the walkthrough to remove unnecessary dependencies as well as improve necessary ones. Ask what each meeting, approval or status update enables. Where participants repeatedly chase information, investigate whether a clear owner, shared state or simpler interface would address the problem. Record the proposed change and the evidence needed to evaluate it. [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/); [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

## Specify completion exceptions and recovery

Describe the observable effect required before work is considered complete. Distinguish an action requested, an action performed and an outcome verified. Where the result is uncertain, retain that uncertainty in the record. OIBOK research Chapter 6 highlights the danger of assuming that an interrupted action either succeeded or failed. [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

For each material exception, agree the trigger, responsible person, required context, expected response and work that may safely continue. Identify a backup when the usual decision-maker is unavailable. An exception should preserve its reason, scope and authority, with any expiry or policy review recorded. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/)

Design recovery around actual consequences. A repeated action can duplicate an effect; a reversal may require a separate authorised correction. Identify who checks the real state, contains the problem, restores acceptable service and informs affected people. Make the authority to pause work visible. For a manual fallback, confirm that the person has access, current instructions, available capacity and enough understanding to resume from the recorded state. [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

## Check the arrangement before changing the technology

Walk through ordinary work and a plausible disruption with the people involved. Introduce missing evidence, an absent approver, a rejected handoff or an uncertain external result. Ask each participant to explain what they would do, what they may decide and who owns the unresolved case.

Use this rehearsal as an adequacy check proposed by this guide. It should reveal gaps in responsibility and shared understanding before they become embedded in a new workflow. Adjust the canvas where participants disagree. Preserve unresolved conditions with owners and dates, and decide whether to proceed with an improvement, investigate further or pause. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/)

The resulting record should explain who decides, who acts, what receiving acceptance means and how exceptions are contained and resolved. Carry these arrangements into the AI Opportunity and Work Design Canvas if AI remains a credible option. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/); [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

# 5 Design how people and AI work together

**Purpose:** select a useful change and define a bounded arrangement for people, knowledge, technology and accountability.

Use the AI Opportunity and Work Design Canvas after the initial review has established the problem and its uncertainties. Include people who perform and own the work, maintain its knowledge, support the systems and assess outcomes. Give affected users a way to influence the design where their experience matters. The canvas supports an explicit decision: a limited AI pilot, a change without AI, further investigation or no change. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

![People AI and authority in a workflow](https://oibok.org/assets/diagrams/people-and-ai.svg)

**Figure. People AI and authority in a workflow.** Draft fictional Harbour Services work design. AI retrieves and drafts within approved sources; a named adviser reviews and authorises the response. Escalation and fallback are part of the work. Harbour AI has no external action authority. Other designs require their own explicit permissions and evidence. [Open the figure and full-size downloads](https://oibok.org/diagrams/people-and-ai/).

## Compare credible responses to the problem

State the outcome sought and the proposed mechanism of improvement. Explain which difficulty AI could address, why that task fits and what would still depend on people or other changes. Keep the expected benefit labelled as a hypothesis until evaluation supports it.

Compare the current arrangement, a feasible improvement without AI and AI within a redesigned workflow. The second option might clarify guidance, simplify a handoff, alter capacity or use ordinary rules-based automation. Develop it seriously enough to provide a useful comparison. For each option, record expected benefit, dependencies, uncertainty and total effort, including setup, integration, training, review, correction and continuing support. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

Consider whose work changes. Faster production in one part of a workflow may leave another team checking more material or resolving more exceptions. OIBOK research Chapter 5 directs attention to dependencies and oversight load. Evaluate the proposed arrangement across the bounded workflow and identify burdens shifted to workers, customers or other teams. [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/)

Compare setup effort with recurring effort and charges. Include preparation, human review, correction, maintenance and recovery across the workflow. Released staff time is potential capacity; identify whether it can be used for worthwhile work. A resource comparison does not establish a cash saving unless expenditure can actually be reduced. Appendix A uses Wattle Supply to show the arithmetic and a sensitivity that reverses the apparent preference. Its inputs are fictional planning assumptions. Guide chapter 6 explains how to test the outcome claims. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/) [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## Give AI a bounded contribution

Write a plain-language task description that identifies approved inputs, expected outputs, permitted activity, prohibited activity, human ownership and escalation. Separate assistance with information, recommendations, decisions and external actions. Each involves different organisational authority. A system's technical capability provides no independent permission to act. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/); [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

Make the task description specific enough for another person to test. An output might need to distinguish source material, generated interpretation and uncertainty. Record which sources govern, when specialist judgement is required and what the system should do when information is missing or contradictory. Preserve relevant source and version references so that a reviewer can check applicability. [\[O2\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-context/); [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/); [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

Where the proposed system can act, define the permitted operations, cases, tools and conditions, along with approval, pause and recovery arrangements. Access to information and permission to change an external record should be recorded separately. Increase authority only through an explicit organisational decision supported by the required evidence. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

Record the configuration to which the evidence will apply: task, domain, setting, date, provider, model and product version where available, tools or interface, retrieval sources, material instructions, permissions and human roles. Identify unavailable version details rather than guessing. Preserve changes during the trial and state which changes require a new review. Evidence about one arrangement should remain attached to that arrangement when the technology or sources develop. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Design meaningful human work

Specify what reviewers must check and how they can check it. Relevant checks may include source currency, factual accuracy, policy application, missing context, unsupported conclusions and whether the proposed action falls within authority. Give reviewers the evidence, time, expertise and ability to reject or escalate the output. A review step needs these supporting conditions to be credible. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/); [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

Consider when a person should form an independent view before seeing AI advice, particularly where an early recommendation could shape their judgement. Plan how staff retain or develop the expertise needed to recognise unfamiliar cases and take over during disruption. OIBOK research Chapter 3 asks organisations to consider both immediate performance and future capability when designing human and AI work. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/); [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/)

Test the review burden with the workers who will carry it. If responsible checking takes longer than anticipated, reconsider the task, scope or benefit claim. Do not assume that inserting a human approval point establishes an effective control. [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/); [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/)

## Specify information boundaries and failure behaviour

Record the data permitted for the task, restricted material, access permissions, retention arrangements and where outputs and decision records belong. Use approved systems and the minimum case information needed. Identify who maintains sources and who resolves ambiguity about access or use. Keep sensitive material in controlled locations and link to it where appropriate. [\[O2\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-context/); [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

For systems that retrieve documents or receive messages, distinguish source content from governing instructions. A retrieved instruction should not expand the system's authority. Include this distinction in the design review for tools that can change records or communicate externally. [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/)

Define responses to missing or conflicting knowledge, unreliable output, system unavailability and excluded cases. Assign escalation and fallback owners. Explain how an affected person can obtain a correction or review. Verify that the fallback can continue the work from its actual state, including any partially completed action. [\[O6\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/execution-and-operational-reliability/); [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

## Decide whether the proposed pilot is ready

Before a pilot, inspect representative cases and deliberate exceptions, including stale guidance, contradictory evidence and incomplete inputs. Check boundaries, source visibility, review behaviour, stop controls and recovery. This guide proposes these checks to expose weaknesses in the designed arrangement; successful checks do not establish general effectiveness.

Agree the eligible work, comparison, measures, evidence sources and decision criteria before the trial. Include outcome quality, errors, exceptions and total human effort. Record other changes occurring alongside AI so that later interpretation can consider alternative explanations. Define incidents that pause the pilot, immediate fallback, the incident reviewer and the evidence needed for restart. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

Finish with a named decision owner, rationale, unresolved conditions and next review date. Completing the canvas records the proposal; deployment still depends on the organisation's applicable decisions and requirements. Transfer the agreed measures and rules into the Outcome and Learning Record, preserving the review and proposal identifiers. Revisit the design when the work, knowledge, authority or technology changes. [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/)

# 6 Evaluate the change

Evaluation connects what happened to a decision about what to do next. Its purpose is to establish what changed, for whom, under which conditions, and what the evidence supports. A completed task, a well received demonstration or increased use of a tool does not establish a better outcome. Equally, an improvement after introducing AI does not establish that AI caused it. The OIBOK separates these claims so that useful evidence can inform action without being overstated. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Use the **Outcome and Learning Record** to connect the intervention, evaluation plan, observations, decision and subsequent changes. Prepare the plan before starting wherever possible. If work has already begun, reconstruct the available baseline, identify decisions made after seeing results and record the limits this introduces. Evaluation may reopen the problem definition or work design at any point. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

![Evaluating the change and AI contribution](https://oibok.org/assets/diagrams/evaluating-change.svg)

**Figure. Evaluating the change and AI contribution.** Draft evaluation illustration. Comparing current work with a combined package addresses a different question from comparing the same improved arrangement with and without AI. Comparable measures, implementation evidence and competing explanations shape the conclusion; the diagram alone establishes no causal claim. [Open the figure and full-size downloads](https://oibok.org/diagrams/evaluating-change/).

## Start with the decision

Identify who will decide whether to continue, adapt, stop or defer the change, and when that decision is needed. Agree what evidence would change their judgement. Specify the workflow, eligible cases, people affected and observation period. Distinguish the period in which cases enter the evaluation from the time allowed for their outcomes to emerge.

Write the expected mechanism in plain language: what will change in the work, why that should affect the outcome, and what must be true for it to work. Include the complete intervention. If it combines clearer policy, revised authority, training and AI assistance, those changes belong in the evaluation. Record the expected contribution of AI separately. Set locally justified outcome expectations, quality constraints and pause conditions before examining results; preserve any later changes and their reasons. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## Define measures that represent the whole job

Choose a small set of measures that can support the decision. Include an outcome, quality and total effort, with relevant safeguards and affected groups. Describe implementation sufficiently to know whether the intended change actually occurred.

| Measure | Operational definition to establish | Interpretation to protect |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | The change experienced by the intended beneficiary, its unit, start and end points, and follow-up period. | Faster task completion may leave the beneficiary's problem unresolved. |
| Quality | What counts as correct, complete and acceptable; who judges it; which cases are examined. | Separate defects in an intermediate output from defects reaching the final service. |
| Total effort | Work across preparation, delivery, checking, correction, escalation and maintenance; whose time is included. | Time saved by one role may become work for another. |
| Safeguards | The unacceptable consequence or boundary, the observation method and who can pause affected work. | An absence of observed incidents is limited by what was observed and for how long. |
| Implementation | Which eligible cases received the intended arrangement, including deviations and workarounds. | A weak result may reflect an ineffective design, incomplete implementation, or both. |

For each measure record the source, collection owner, frequency and calculation. Define the unit of analysis: a case, decision, person, team or another bounded unit. For a rate, specify both numerator and denominator. For duration, distinguish elapsed time from active working time. Keep setup effort visible separately from recurring effort, then explain how each affects the decision. Report model, software and service charges separately from staff time. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

Report how many cases were eligible, included, completed, excluded or still awaiting an outcome. Explain missing observations and whether their absence could favour the result. Do not silently remove difficult cases or count unresolved cases as successes. Keep intake cohorts and follow-up periods visible so that newer cases do not appear better simply because there has been less time for problems to emerge.

Where quality depends on judgement, give reviewers shared criteria and examine disagreements. A changing definition or a new measurement process can produce an apparent improvement. Preserve the original definitions and explain any break in comparability. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

## Compare plausible alternatives

Use a baseline and, where feasible, a relevant comparison operating over the same period. Check whether workload, case complexity, staff experience, policy, incentives and available resources differ. A historical baseline may still be useful, but a before-and-after difference also contains changes that would have occurred without the intervention. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Include a credible **non-AI alternative**. Depending on the problem, this might be clearer guidance, a redesigned handoff, a search improvement or a simpler automation. Ask whether it could achieve the desired outcome with less effort or dependency. Where practical, compare the same improved working arrangement with and without AI. If that comparison is absent, report the result of the complete package and leave the AI-specific contribution unresolved.

Record competing explanations before deciding which account is most convincing. Consider easier cases, additional expert support, seasonal demand, staff becoming familiar with the work, concurrent changes and different measurement. Look for evidence that could contradict the preferred explanation. Observations, complaints and interviews can reveal mechanisms and omitted consequences; they complement the comparison rather than automatically establishing causation. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## State what the evidence allows

Separate observations, interpretation and uncertainty. An observation describes what the records show. An interpretation explains why it may have happened. An uncertainty identifies what remains unresolved and why it matters to the next decision.

Keep the claim proportionate. A descriptive claim reports a change. An associational claim reports that use of the intervention and an outcome varied together, without establishing cause. A contribution claim explains why the intervention plausibly helped alongside other factors. A causal claim requires a defensible account of what would otherwise have happened. The strength of the language should follow the evaluation design and evidence. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Show counts alongside percentages and explain the sample selection. A small, convenient or volunteer sample may omit rare events, difficult cases or people less comfortable with the change. Repeated observations from the same people or teams are not automatically independent evidence. Where estimates or uncertainty intervals are appropriate, use methods suited to the design; apparent precision cannot repair biased selection or missing outcomes.

Aggregate only where definitions, populations and periods support the comparison. When combining rates, use their underlying counts and denominators rather than averaging percentages indiscriminately. Show important variation alongside the summary: a better average can conceal worse outcomes for a subgroup or a transfer of effort between teams. Record affected people whose experience is missing. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

A successful workflow trial supports a claim about that workflow under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the whole organisation has become more intelligent. Wider claims require evidence connecting the result to organisational roles, authority, routines, memory and repeated consequences across the relevant boundary. Do not turn local measures into a single OI score. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Make and record the decision

Conflicting results require an explicit judgement. Faster service, higher checking effort and uneven customer outcomes should remain visible together. Identify which constraints must be met and which trade-offs the accountable owner is authorised to accept. Record affected perspectives and unresolved disagreement. A benefit in one measure does not erase an unacceptable outcome elsewhere. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

| Decision | Grounds to record | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Continue | Evidence supports use within the tested scope and agreed conditions. | Maintain ownership, monitoring and a review trigger. |
| Adapt | A useful contribution remains plausible, but a correctable problem or uncertainty requires change. | Name the modification, affected scope and evidence needed before extension. |
| Stop | The change fails the agreed purpose or constraints, or a preferable alternative is supported. | Use the fallback, close dependencies and retain the explanation. |
| Defer | Evidence, authority or necessary conditions are insufficient for a responsible decision. | Specify what is missing, who will resolve it and when the decision returns. |

These decisions operationalise the record's options to continue, adapt, stop or gather more evidence. Deferring expansion need not suspend every existing activity; state precisely what remains authorised. Conversely, pausing affected use requires a clear fallback and restart conditions. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

**Review check:** bring the owner, someone doing the work and the evidence owner together. Invite an affected perspective and a challenger where useful. Can they trace the decision to the original question, inspect contradictory evidence and identify the next action? Record scope, owner, date and review trigger before closing the discussion.

# 7 Turn experience into lasting capability

A review creates value when it changes what the organisation can understand or do in later work. A document in a repository may preserve information without making it usable. The OIBOK treats organisational memory as distributed across people, routines, authority, relationships and systems. Learning becomes organisational when a meaningful change in understanding or future response is retained beyond the originating episode. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/)

Use the learning actions and persistence fields in the **Outcome and Learning Record** to connect the evaluation to maintained working arrangements. The question is: what must remain available after the pilot, meeting or enthusiastic individual has moved on? [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

![From a lesson to lasting capability](https://oibok.org/assets/diagrams/lasting-capability.svg)

**Figure. From a lesson to lasting capability.** Draft practice illustration. A lesson becomes useful through owned changes to working arrangements and later enactment. Reuse, succession and changed conditions provide different evidence about continuity. Record what remains untested and distinguish an action completed from capability demonstrated later. [Open the figure and full-size downloads](https://oibok.org/diagrams/lasting-capability/).

## Form a lesson that can be challenged

Distinguish the event from the lesson. Record what happened, what was known at the time, the interpretation now proposed and the evidence supporting it. Preserve contradictory observations and plausible alternatives. Avoid replacing the original decision record with an account written entirely in hindsight.

State the conditions under which the lesson is expected to apply, its limits and what would prompt reconsideration. Ask whether the response requires a correction within existing assumptions or a change to the assumptions themselves. A recurrent handoff failure may call for clearer information; it may also expose an unresolved conflict over who can decide. Investigate that distinction before selecting training or another tool as the remedy. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/)

Review successful outcomes as well as failures. A good result may depend on an expert quietly repairing the process. A near miss can reveal an effective safeguard and an underlying weakness at the same time. Capture both, including the effort needed to prevent the failure. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/)

## Translate the lesson into working arrangements

A useful learning action specifies a change people can encounter in their work. Depending on the finding, this may involve:

- Updating authoritative guidance, with a named owner, scope and review condition.
- Changing a decision right, escalation route or approval boundary.
- Revising a handoff so the receiving person gets the information needed to act.
- Adjusting a system configuration, access permission, retrieval rule or quality check.
- Providing practice and feedback for the people expected to exercise new judgement.
- Allocating time, staffing or other resources needed to make the revised arrangement workable.

Attach an owner, due date and completion evidence to each action. Identify which actions are prerequisites for restarting or extending use. Include the communication needed for affected people to recognise the change and the older instruction it supersedes. Completion evidence should show the arrangement operating, not merely that a document was circulated. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

Describe the intended capability as an ability under stated conditions: what the organisation should be able to do, to what quality, with which authority and resources. Check the whole configuration. A new procedure without access, time or decision authority may remain unusable. A training session cannot compensate indefinitely for a contradictory operating arrangement. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

## Make the knowledge usable at the next decision

Place the lesson where the relevant work will call for it. This might be a decision template, an onboarding exercise, the authoritative policy page or an escalation workflow. Link it to the supporting evidence and the decision it changed. Avoid maintaining competing copies with unclear status.

Retain enough context to distinguish current guidance from historical evidence. Record ownership, source, scope, version, sensitivity, review condition and supersession. Keep access proportionate to the material. Historical records support accountability; current instructions need an unambiguous location and status. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/)

For AI-assisted retrieval, preserve the route back to authoritative sources. A generated summary should not become the sole surviving account of the lesson. Check whether outdated or conflicting material can still be returned and whether the person using it can recognise uncertainty and escalate. Ownership of source quality remains an organisational responsibility. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/)

## Test reuse succession and persistence

Schedule a later check appropriate to the work's frequency and consequences. The first review can establish implementation; later work can test whether the change remains usable and whether the claimed benefit persists. Record the distinction between an action completed and a capability demonstrated. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/); [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

Use three practical tests:

1. **Reuse:** follow a later relevant case. Can the worker find the current guidance, understand its scope, apply it and recognise an exception? Examine the outcome and the effort involved.
2. **Succession:** have another suitably prepared person perform the work within their authority, without relying on the original champion to fill undocumented gaps. Observe access, understanding, judgement and escalation.
3. **Changed conditions:** examine a relevant variation, such as a different case type, changed policy or unavailable provider. Does the arrangement adapt or fall back within its agreed bounds?

Choose safe cases or rehearsals where necessary. Record which conditions were examined and which remain untested. An untested succession plan is a plan, not evidence of continuity. Persistence does not require complete independence from individuals; it requires credible continuity over the horizon of the claim. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

If performance depends on temporary expert assistance or an external provider, make that dependency explicit. Decide what support remains necessary, what capability must stay internal and how recovery or exit would work. A tool's continued availability alone does not demonstrate that the organisation can reproduce, govern or adapt the outcome. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

## Maintain and revise what is retained

Give the capability owner responsibility for currentness as well as initial implementation. Review material changes to policy, workload, people, technology and provider arrangements. Retire superseded instructions deliberately while preserving necessary decision history.

Where later evidence contradicts the lesson, revise the lesson and the arrangements built around it. Where the evidence supports retaining current practice, record that reasoned choice. More change is not automatically more learning. The persistence review should leave a visible connection between experience, revised understanding and future response. [\[O8\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/organisational-memory-reflection-and-learning/); [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

# 8 Tailor and extend the approach

Choose the smallest boundary that includes consequential decisions, handoffs, feedback and dependencies. The questions can support a workflow, service or wider inquiry. An incident may begin with evaluation; a strategic change may begin with purpose and coordination. Revisit earlier questions when evidence warrants it. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Match the depth to the decision

Use the route selector near the front of this guide to choose an initial depth, then revisit it when evidence changes. The Linden Studio case in Appendix A shows a bounded non-AI proposal with evaluation still to be done.

Increase the depth where consequences are harder to reverse, affected groups are more varied, dependencies are less understood or uncertainty changes the decision. Judge the quality of inquiry required, rather than prescribing pages or meetings. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

In a small organisation, one person may hold several roles. Make those roles and conflicts visible and obtain challenge where needed. In a larger organisation, connect local records to existing service ownership, risk, architecture, quality and investment processes. Reuse suitable records instead of creating a separate OIBOK administration system. Preserve the essential questions: purpose, authority, evidence, affected outcomes, ownership and later learning. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/); [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Treat extension as a new contextual inquiry

Before transferring an approach, ask what made it work and whether those conditions exist in the receiving setting. Compare the problem, population, sources, language, skills, authority, workload, interfaces and available resources. A copied template transfers an artefact; it does not establish the receiving organisation's capability. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/)

Specify what can remain common, what requires local adaptation and which uncertainties need a bounded trial. The receiving owner should accept responsibility for local implementation and evaluation. Retain links to the original evidence while recording the new scope and conditions.

Increased volume can change checking capacity, escalation demand, access arrangements and dependency on shared services. Examine effects outside the adopting team, including work displaced to customers or supporting functions. Reconsider safeguards, recovery and ongoing resources before extension. Where AI is involved, changes to models, retrieval sources, permissions or providers may require renewed evaluation. [\[O9\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/capability-development-and-adaptation/); [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

Several local successes do not establish organisation-wide capability. Examine learning across boundaries, resolution of conflicting priorities and persistence across people, conditions and time. Preserve local variation when aggregating findings. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## Bring in evaluation expertise when it changes the decision

Obtain specialist evaluation support when a material investment depends on causal attribution, when selection or shared team effects make the comparison difficult, or when rare but serious consequences cannot be assessed adequately from routine records. Complex experimental and quasi-experimental designs require expertise in their assumptions and implementation. Involve that expertise during planning where possible. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

Seek independent or external review when the decision is contested, conflicts of interest could undermine trust or internal capability is insufficient. External reviewers need access to the operating context; internal reviewers need enough independence to challenge it. Combine methodological competence with people who understand the work and those affected by it. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/)

## Improve the guide through documented use

Working Companion 0.3 includes reviewer instructions and an editable feedback record. Identify whether a finding comes from reading, an observed walkthrough, live application, an outcome review or later persistence evidence. These support different claims. Practitioner review of this edition is pending.

Describe the context, approach, evidence, limitations and resulting changes. Include unclear, unhelpful and contradictory experiences. Share only authorised material. Traceable accounts can inform revisions and research questions; an individual case does not validate the OIBOK model. [\[O10\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/integrated-and-responsible-organisational-intelligence/)

## A1 Harbour Services

**Fictional example.** Harbour Services follows the case in Practical Toolkit 0.1. Its records, decisions and results are invented to illustrate interpretation. The later persistence check remains pending. [\[T1\]](https://oibok.org/tools/practice-review/) [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/) [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

### Establish the problem and evidence

Harbour Services wants to resolve standard complaints about delayed or cancelled visits accurately, with fewer delays and repeat contacts. The inquiry begins when a complaint is received and ends after closure and seven calendar days of follow-up. Legal disputes, specialist vulnerable-customer cases and unusual compensation sit outside this initial scope.

The Service Operations Lead owns the improvement decision. A front-line adviser, team leader, policy owner, systems lead and analyst contribute. A team member independent of the sponsor facilitates the initial review. One case identifier, HS-01, connects the Practice Review PR-HS-01, work-design proposal AI-HS-01 and Outcome and Learning Record OL-HS-01.

The baseline contains 100 eligible complaints received over four weeks, all followed through closure and seven further calendar days. The case audit records 28 repeat contacts, 32 incomplete handoffs, eight customer-facing policy errors and no unauthorised actions. Timestamps show median resolution of 4.8 business days. Median total human handling, including checking and correction, is 38 minutes.

Staff walkthroughs describe inconsistent access to current policy, unclear refund authority and context lost between teams. Those accounts suggest explanations to investigate. The team also retains workload, case mix and training as alternatives. The baseline counts describe the selected period and do not establish which explanation caused the difficulties.

### Connect the findings to the work design

The Context and Knowledge Map identifies gaps in finding current policy, retaining case explanations through handoffs and using recurring complaint causes to revise procedures. Each has an owner.

The Decision and Coordination Canvas separates policy interpretation, response authorisation and sending the final reply. The policy owner maintains the governing source. A named authorised person decides the response within their authority. The adviser approves each external reply, preserves the rationale and records the outcome. Missing or conflicting policy goes to a supervisor, and excluded cases leave the standard workflow.

The team compares current practice, knowledge and process changes without AI, and a combined package using AI for retrieval and draft assistance. It selects a limited trial of the combined package. The package includes a current policy source with an owner and version, clear authority, minimum handoff information and AI assistance. This choice means the trial can examine the package but cannot isolate AI's contribution.

AI has no authority to send messages, approve refunds, change policy or act externally. A named adviser checks the applicable source, version and proposed response before sending. Missing or conflicting policy requires abstention and escalation. The established manual process remains the fallback. Tests of typical and exceptional cases, source selection, permissions and fallback are conditions before live use.

### Plan the comparison and decision criteria

The example pilot receives 40 eligible new complaints over two weeks. Twenty use the package and twenty use the existing process. Allocation alternates within issue and severity categories, with exceptions recorded. It is neither randomised nor blinded. Every pilot case is followed through closure and seven further days.

The locally chosen targets are median resolution no greater than 3.5 business days and repeat contact no greater than 20 per cent. In the package group, final policy errors, unauthorised actions and confidentiality incidents must be zero. An incident in those categories pauses the affected use for review. These are fictional local choices, not OIBOK benchmarks or general safety thresholds.

The analyst collects timestamps, handling records and outcomes. The policy owner reviews reply accuracy. Draft defects and customer-facing errors are recorded separately to distinguish draft quality from the work of the review control.

### Review the illustrative results

All figures below are fictional and reproduced from the toolkit example. The baseline and pilot have different intake windows; the two pilot groups operate concurrently. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

| Measure | Baseline 100 cases | Comparison 20 cases | Package 20 cases |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median resolution in business days | 4.8 | 4.4 | 3.1 |
| Repeat contact within seven days of closure | 28/100 | 6/20 | 4/20 |
| Median total human handling in minutes | 38 | 36 | 30 |
| Final customer-facing policy errors | 8/100 | 1/20 | 0/20 |
| Unauthorised actions | 0/100 | 0/20 | 0/20 |

No confidentiality incidents are observed in either pilot group. AI drafts contain unsupported policy claims in three of the twenty package cases. Review catches all three before sending: one used a superseded policy and two misapplied exception wording.

The package meets the example resolution and repeat-contact targets. The pattern supports further investigation. Twenty cases cannot establish reliability or rule out rare incidents. Alternating allocation retains selection uncertainty; case mix, extra attention and staff learning could affect results. The combined design leaves AI's additional contribution unknown.

Review prevented the recorded draft defects from reaching customers. Preserve that process evidence, the continuing checking effort and the underlying failures alongside the zero final-error count.

### Decide and retain the learning

The Service Operations Lead decides to **adapt and extend a limited pilot**, after correcting version selection and exception handling, retesting and confirming named reviewers. AI remains unable to send or approve actions. The policy error in the comparison group also requires the usual service correction and owner review.

The decision assigns follow-up actions; their completion is not yet recorded. Before restart, the policy owner is to correct the current-version rules, the systems lead is to retest retrieval and exceptions, and the team leader is to revise review and handoff guidance and check it with advisers. At the next pilot review, the analyst is to report on the next twenty package cases and the draft-defect log. The service owner is to examine the causes of cancelled visits, because a more efficient complaint response does not itself prevent those underlying failures.

At week eight, the Service Operations Lead plans to examine later cases handled by another adviser, check whether the revised policy and authority remain usable and review whether the outcomes persist. That check is pending. The example therefore supports a decision about the next limited trial, with no claim of sustained organisational improvement.

### What the example leaves open

A further evaluation could compare the improved policy, authority and handoff arrangement with and without AI, provided the design is feasible and appropriate. It could examine whether additional reviewing effort remains manageable, whether excluded cases are recognised reliably and whether the benefits survive without the original pilot support. These are possible next inquiries, not additional results in the existing case.


## A2 Wattle Supply business decision

**Fictional planning example.** Wattle Supply is an invented office-supplies distributor. This example is separate from Harbour Services and adds no results to that case. The current-practice baseline below is an assumption; the proposed options are untested estimates. There are no observed results. Volumes, times and AUD charges are invented teaching inputs, not industry benchmarks or supplier quotations. The comparison applies the work-design and evaluation methods in guide Chapters 5 and 6 and the existing toolkit. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T2\]](https://oibok.org/tools/ai-opportunity-and-work-design/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

### Define the decision and the alternatives

The catalogue owner wants staff to publish accurate routine product entries with less total effort. The workflow starts with an approved supplier specification file and ends with a checked entry and any subsequent correction. Products requiring specialist safety or regulatory claims remain outside scope. Three options enter the planning comparison:

- **Current practice:** staff find the relevant file, prepare the entry, check it and publish it.
- **Non-AI improvement:** maintain one current source location, use a structured entry template and ordinary validation rules, and assign source ownership and a review checklist.
- **The same improvement with AI:** add source-linked draft preparation using the approved files. A person still checks the facts, resolves uncertainty and authorises publication. AI cannot change the source or publish an entry.

The decision is whether either improvement merits a bounded comparison, including which assumptions need testing. Accuracy and usable turnaround remain outcomes to evaluate; this calculation supplies only the resource part of the decision.

### Make the assumptions inspectable

Assume **240 eligible entries per month** and value existing staff time at **AUD 60 per hour** across the participating roles. This is a planning allowance for comparing resource use. It is not an assumed reduction in payroll. Use average minutes across all eligible entries, since those averages can be multiplied by the volume.

| Planning input | Current practice | Non-AI improvement | Improvement with AI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| One-off setup, staff hours | 0 | 10 | 30 |
| Preparation, minutes per entry | 9 | 6 | 2 |
| Review, minutes per entry | 2 | 2 | 3 |
| Correction allowance, minutes per entry | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Maintenance, staff hours per month | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Additional cash charges per month, AUD | 0 | 0 | 120 |

Preparation includes locating information, completing the entry, recording the work and manual publication. The correction allowance is averaged across every eligible entry, including those needing no correction. Maintenance includes source updates, checking the workflow and maintaining templates or AI configuration. It is separate from case handling.

Setup includes configuration, test cases, staff practice and evaluation planning. The AI setup already includes the non-AI changes. No additional external setup payment is assumed. Existing unchanged platform costs, other shared overheads and tax are excluded from all options. The AI charge covers an invented combined subscription and usage allowance.

### Calculate effort before claiming a benefit

Monthly staff hours equal entry volume multiplied by preparation, review and correction minutes, divided by 60, plus maintenance hours. Multiply those hours by the staff-time allowance, then add the new cash charges. Keep this combined **resource total** distinct from cash expenditure.

| Calculated planning result | Current practice | Non-AI improvement | Improvement with AI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Staff hours per month, including maintenance | 50 | 39 | 29 |
| Staff-time value plus new cash charges per month, AUD | 3,000 | 2,340 | 1,860 |
| One-off setup resource total, AUD | 0 | 600 | 1,800 |
| Setup plus 12 operating months, AUD | 36,000 | 28,680 | 24,120 |

For example, the AI option requires `240 × (2 + 3 + 1) ÷ 60 + 5 = 29` staff hours monthly. Its resource total is `29 × AUD 60 + AUD 120 = AUD 1,860`. The horizon calculation holds workload and rates constant for 12 operating months after setup; it does not model a rollout ramp-up.

Under these assumptions, the non-AI option releases 11 staff hours monthly. AI releases another 10 hours compared with that improvement, while adding AUD 120 in monthly cash charges. Its incremental resource advantage is AUD 480 monthly, or AUD 4,560 across setup and 12 operating months after allowing for its higher setup effort.

These hours represent **potential released capacity**. The owner must identify useful work that can absorb it and check whether fragmented time is actually usable. No cash saving is claimed: no payroll, overtime or contractor reduction is assumed. The AI option instead adds AUD 1,440 in cash charges across 12 operating months.

### Test an assumption that could reverse the decision

Suppose responsible AI review needs **six minutes per entry instead of three**, with every other input unchanged. AI then requires 41 staff hours monthly, compared with 39 for the non-AI option. Its resource total rises to AUD 2,580 monthly and AUD 32,760 across setup and 12 operating months. That is AUD 240 more per month and AUD 4,080 more over the stated horizon than the non-AI improvement.

The original estimate makes a limited AI comparison worth investigating. Under the longer-review assumption, the owner would prioritise testing the non-AI improvement and defer AI expansion on the present resource case. A separately demonstrated quality benefit could justify reconsideration; none is assumed here.

Before authorising either change, replace the assumed baseline with case records. Collect total time across roles, final accuracy, turnaround, corrections, maintenance and missing observations. Compare equivalent work, preserve differences in case mix and record actual charges. Review whether the expected capacity can be redeployed. Retain the original estimates so later findings can challenge the business decision, rather than quietly replacing its assumptions. [\[O7\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/outcomes-feedback-and-evaluation/); [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

## A3 Linden Studio non AI decision

**Wholly fictional example.** Linden Studio, its people, documents and decisions are invented. This case illustrates choosing a change without AI when authority is unclear. It ends with a decision and evaluation plan. No implementation, measured benefit or later persistence is presented as an observed result.

### Start with the commitment

Linden Studio is a small design business. A client lead discusses prospective work, a delivery lead allocates designers, and the owner approves departures from standard commercial arrangements. The proposed inquiry covers the handoff from an agreed client brief to an accepted delivery plan. Its purpose is to give the client a reliable account of scope, responsibilities and when work can begin.

In this scenario, those roles have different understandings of when a proposed start date becomes a commitment. The client lead treats a date in an email as provisional. The delivery lead expects an approved brief before reserving capacity. The client may reasonably understand the same email as confirmation. A designer receiving the work cannot resolve that disagreement simply by writing a clearer summary.

The client lead proposes an AI assistant to turn emails into onboarding briefs. This could address preparation effort, but its value remains a hypothesis. The owner asks the team to examine the authority and handoff first.

### Make the uncertainty visible

The Practice Review would assemble contrasting client files, the terms communicated to clients, delivery schedules and participants' accounts. The team would distinguish recorded commitments from recollections and interpretations. Missing information would remain visible rather than being reconstructed as an agreed fact.

The proposed Context and Knowledge Map identifies the governing brief, scope changes, client dependencies, capacity information and the people able to resolve disagreements. The Decision and Coordination Canvas separates preparing a brief, accepting delivery responsibility and authorising an exception. These distinctions draw on OIBOK research Chapters 3 to 5. [\[O3\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/knowledge-and-expertise/) [\[O4\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/decision-and-authority/) [\[O5\]](https://oibok.org/knowledge/coordination-and-collective-action/)

Unclear authority is one explanation to examine. Workload, changing client requirements, incomplete briefs or unavailable capacity could also create delay. The review has no measured findings establishing their relative importance.

### Choose a bounded change without AI

The owner decides to defer the AI proposal and test a clearer handoff using existing records. The proposed arrangement makes requested, provisional and confirmed dates distinct. The client lead prepares the brief; the delivery lead accepts scope, dependencies and available capacity before a date is confirmed. Exceptions go to the owner. Existing disputed commitments require an authorised resolution with the client.

The sender retains responsibility until the receiving owner accepts the handoff. If it is returned, the sender owns clarification and resubmission. A missing approval remains a visible exception with an owner. It must not disappear inside a completed onboarding document.

AI drafting remains deferred while the commitment and acceptance rules are clarified and the remaining drafting burden is examined. The decision does not establish that AI would be ineffective. It identifies a simpler intervention to examine first and an unresolved question about whether drafting effort warrants additional technology.

### Plan what would justify the next decision

Before changing the workflow, the owner and delivery lead would record eligible cases, exclusions and existing performance. The Outcome and Learning Record would track time to an accepted plan, clarification and rework, commitments changed after confirmation, client understanding and total staff effort. Collection responsibilities and the observation period would match the workflow. [\[T3\]](https://oibok.org/tools/outcome-and-learning/)

The review would ask whether the new arrangement was used, whether commitments became clearer and whether waiting simply moved elsewhere. Case complexity, capacity and additional attention would remain competing explanations. Later work handled by another suitably prepared colleague would help examine continuity.

All results remain pending. The example ends with a testable non-AI change, named responsibilities and a reason to revisit the AI proposal if a material drafting problem remains.

# Appendix B Facilitating the work

## Prepare a useful review

Agree the purpose, boundary and decision owner before inviting participants. Ask for contrasting case records, the instructions used at the time, relevant outcome information and known exceptions. Explain how material will be handled and which parts of the discussion will be recorded. Give participants a route to raise concerns that should not be attributed in a shared worksheet.

Prepare a common case identifier and links to evidence in the authorised workspace. Copy the relevant blank worksheets. Keep the fictional example separate so that its assumptions and targets do not become the team's default answers. Clarify whether the session will decide an action or prepare a recommendation for someone with authority.

## An initial Practice Review

The following 90-minute agenda is a starting suggestion, consistent with Toolkit 0.1. It is not a tested duration requirement. Large dependencies, sensitive decisions or poor evidence may require preparation or follow-up rather than a longer meeting. [\[T0\]](https://oibok.org/tools/guide/)

| Time | Activity | Working result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 minutes | Confirm purpose, scope, exclusions and who can decide | One inquiry statement |
| 20 minutes | Follow contrasting cases and record evidence | Events, sources, disagreements and unknowns |
| 20 minutes | Complete the Context and Knowledge Map | Important meanings, sources, expertise and gaps |
| 20 minutes | Examine decisions, handoffs and exceptions | Authority, acceptance and recovery arrangements |
| 10 minutes | Compare explanations and possible changes | A proposed intervention or further inquiry |
| 10 minutes | Assign the decision and evaluation preparation | Owners, conditions and next review |

Ask participants to explain the actual work before seeking agreement about an ideal process. Invite the receiver of a handoff to describe what was usable. When an explanation is asserted, ask which evidence supports it and which case might challenge it. Preserve disagreement in neutral language. The facilitator's role is to make the reasoning visible, not to manufacture consensus.

End with the decision, the unresolved conditions and the next action. Read back the owner and expected evidence for each material action. If the discussion identifies a problem outside the chosen boundary, record the dependency and route it to an owner rather than silently enlarging the project.

## Design and evaluation sessions

Toolkit 0.1 suggests about 90 minutes for the AI Opportunity and Work Design Canvas, 30 minutes to prepare an outcome plan, 45 minutes for the results review and 15 minutes for a later persistence check. Adjust these suggestions to the work and the evidence available. An outcome review cannot be completed before the relevant observation period has finished. [\[T0\]](https://oibok.org/tools/guide/)

During work design, ask someone to explain the credible non-AI option, even if the group is enthusiastic about AI. Ask the intended reviewer to demonstrate what they would check. During the outcome review, show adverse and contradictory evidence alongside the headline measure. During the persistence review, inspect a later case or a safe rehearsal instead of relying only on assurances that the new guidance is available.

## Keep a small connected record

Working Companion 0.3 provides a case index, record handoff checks, annotated Harbour Services excerpts and a feedback record. The connected-records illustration shows how the fictional case fits the existing tools. Start real work from the separate blank canvases.

At minimum, retain the inquiry and case identifier, the evidence references, the proposed change, its authority and scope, the evaluation plan, the resulting decision and the learning actions. Preserve dates and versions so that a later reader can distinguish the initial expectation from the eventual finding. Use existing records where they already serve this purpose.

![One case across the OIBOK working records](https://oibok.org/assets/diagrams/connected-records.svg)

**Figure. One case across the OIBOK working records.** Draft fictional Harbour Services illustration. A shared case identifier connects evidence, design, evaluation and later actions. Records can develop together. Preserve proposed arrangements, conditional authorisation, completed work and pending checks as distinct statuses. Harbour follow-up corrections and its week-eight persistence check remain pending. [Open the figure and full-size downloads](https://oibok.org/diagrams/connected-records/).

Before closing a review, ask:

- Can another person locate the evidence and distinguish observation from interpretation?
- Does every consequential action have a person with authority to own it?
- Can the people receiving the work identify what they must accept or challenge?
- Have important adverse effects, missing outcomes and alternative explanations been retained?
- Is the next review tied to an observable event or appropriate time horizon?
- Will the lesson change something usable in later work, with a named owner?

These questions check the usefulness of the record. They do not produce an organisational intelligence score.
